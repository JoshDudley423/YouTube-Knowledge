# The Smart Way to Start Small and Still Win Big
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZamYEo_mvxk | Published: 20250715

Return interview with Daniel Cronauer (owns gyms, a roofing/marketing business, and a power-washing/softwash company — "Apex Softwash"). Broad tactical/strategy conversation, not one single business idea. Organized by topic below.

## Home service business model: "truck and phone" / marketing-only operator
- Daniel's roofing company literally owns no truck — it's pure marketing/lead-generation, and 100% of the actual work is subcontracted out. Thesis: "if you know how to market, you could do anything" — the physical asset (truck, tools) is optional; marketing is the actual skill that matters.
- His launch playbook for a new "truck and a phone" home service business:
  1. Steal a proven sales/marketing playbook from an established company in a different industry (he modeled his approach on Cutco's door-to-door knife-sales training material) rather than reinventing a process.
  2. Invest first in professional branding: a well-thought-out business name (not your initials, not something generic like "Quality Innovations") and a professional logo — before spending on service delivery.
  3. Skip paid digital ads for local service launch: he considers Facebook Ads and Google Ads "a waste of money," too expensive per dollar of return for this category. Prefers cheap physical marketing — flyers/door hangers/rack cards — because you know exactly who your customer is (hyper-local targeting).
  4. Critical targeting insight for services like power washing: **flyer the clean/nice houses, not the dirty ones** — the customer isn't "the person with the problem," it's "the person who already cares about cleanliness/upkeep." Cites the P&G Febreze case study (from the book *The Power of Habit*): Febreze failed when marketed as "get rid of tough odors" (people whose houses smelled bad didn't think their house smelled bad, so didn't buy) and succeeded once repositioned as "finish your clean" (tied to an existing habit loop for people already inclined to clean) — directly generalizes to service-business targeting: sell to people already predisposed to value the outcome, not people who "need" it most.

## Facebook Marketplace as an organic lead channel
- Cites secondhand example: a fencing company doing $2.5M/year at double-digit margins, sourcing 100% of leads organically from Facebook Marketplace, posting about once a week with no paid boosting.
- Daniel's own example: ran a Bitcoin-mining-hardware resale company that did $9.8M in its first 90 days, 100% via organic Facebook Marketplace posts.
- Tactic: use **multiple Facebook accounts** to get around Facebook limiting how often/how many times you can post the same listing — necessary when you have many SKUs/variants (e.g., dozens of miner makes/models) and want more frequent exposure; funnel all resulting leads back to one central intake.
- More ethically gray tactic discussed: using fake/multiple Facebook accounts to post fabricated "recommendations" for your own business inside local Facebook groups (e.g., seeding 8 fake accounts recommending the same power-washing company in a "recommend a local company" thread) to manufacture social proof/trust. Both hosts flag this as ethically questionable but acknowledge it's effective and reportedly common; no dedicated "trustworthy" agency currently exists for this (floated as its own business idea).

## Opening a gym next to (and specifically to beat) existing competitors
- Deliberate competitive-market-entry strategy: only enter a market where a nearby existing gym is known to be poorly run/outdated (validated via community word of mouth) — explicitly avoid opening next to a strong competitor (e.g., Planet Fitness) you're not confident you can beat yet ("don't dive headfirst into a 3-foot pool" — analogous to a laundromat-business host previously interviewed [see other TKO episode] who deliberately opens only at intersections with existing run-down laundromats to capture pent-up local demand).
- Concrete "steal the customer" tactic: when a nearby competing gym was closed on Sundays, Daniel ran a promotion — show an ID from any other local gym on a Sunday and work out free at his gym. That competitor was out of business within a month.
- Belief that a genuinely better product/service naturally wins via word of mouth over time in tight-knit local communities (gym-goers talk to friends at other gyms) without needing aggressive tactics in every case.
- Cheap, creative local marketing/attention-getter: parked an idle branded construction-company trailer on vacant land next to his gym (cost: ~$1,200 in gravel for a small driveway) and had a friend post "what's going in next to [gym name]?" in the local Facebook group — drove curiosity/engagement and cross-promoted both the gym and his separate construction company for minimal spend.
- Marketing ROI note: trailer wraps are called out as a great ROI marketing spend — you can buy/wrap ~10 trailers for the cost of one wrapped truck (a wrapped trailer costs roughly $5K vs. ~$40K for a comparable wrapped truck), useful as a static "billboard" placed at a high-visibility location (e.g., a gym's entrance, mimicking a tactic borrowed from Planet Fitness).

## Idea/market validation ("how deep is the pool") before committing capital
- Core analogy from Daniel: you can be confident in your ability to "dive" (execute) without knowing the depth of the market — validate depth before investing heavily.
- Host's parallel example: before building out a backyard pickleball club (a $30-40K capital build-out), instead of assuming a theoretical 1-2% share of ~10,000 local weekly pickleball players would show up, he ran cheap Facebook ad tests (instant-form ads, Messenger ads, and a link to a detailed Typeform capturing price sensitivity, play frequency, singles/doubles preference, referral potential) to gauge real demand before construction — generated leads at under $2 each with high purchase intent within 2 days (~20+ leads), used as a cheap proxy for real market depth.
- Both agree: "sell it before you build it" — pre-validate/pre-sell a product or service concept via ads, pre-orders, or direct outreach before investing capital, rather than testing the market only after fully building the offering.
- Cheap community-engagement tactic that doubles as free market research and buy-in: crowdsourcing a new business's name from the local community (e.g., a Facebook post asking "help us name our gym") generates high engagement (~200 responses in one example) and makes many respondents feel invested in the business's success even though only one name is chosen — increases the odds those people become customers.
- Framing: rather than fixating on a speculative "end number" (e.g., "we'll do $100M"), focus on **systems and processes that are within your control** (e.g., "I will cold call 100 people a day for 100 days") — the controllable input, not the uncontrollable outcome.

## Why most home-service businesses (80-90%) never scale past 1-2 trucks
1. **Lead volume** is the first constraint — if you don't have leads to support more trucks/crews, you're structurally capped; conversely, small "wasted" lead-generation lapses matter enormously (Daniel treats an employee failing to place a $7-11 yard sign at a job site as a fireable offense, since that sign is a lead-generation touchpoint for the whole company).
2. **Fear of losing control / inability to accept employee mistakes** — many small operators can't tolerate delegating because employees will make mistakes; acceptance of "mistakes will happen, that's what insurance is for" is a prerequisite to scaling past solo/family operation.
3. Once leads and delegation tolerance are solved, the next lever is **systems/documentation** — including things like a written company mission statement communicated regularly to staff (Daniel was initially skeptical of "soft" management concepts like mission statements until attending a conference by John Wilson on scaling service businesses to $5M+, which changed his view).
4. Employee-value framing: rejects "average employee will only ever be 70% as good as the owner" — argues strong employees can exceed the owner in specific functional areas (cites a gym manager he considers better than himself at operations, being groomed as a regional manager), and that the "70% rule" still nets positive scale math (two 70%-as-good employees = net 40% growth over doing it solo).
5. References the framework from the book *"Three Signs of a Miserable Job"*: employees become disengaged from irrelevance (no sense of higher purpose), immeasurability (no metrics tied to their role — example: tracking "% of drive-thru customers you made smile" for an otherwise unmeasurable role), and anonymity (manager doesn't know them personally) — addressing these three is framed as key to retaining staff capable of supporting scale.
6. Delegation targets specifically called out as easy, high-leverage removals from an owner-operator's plate: bookkeeping/accounting (outsource immediately), mail handling (have an assistant filter/scan it), and invoicing (a virtual assistant can handle weekly invoice generation/sending) — anything not directly tied to sales is framed as a distraction from the owner's highest-leverage activity (selling).

## Business-model philosophy: diversification vs. concentration
- Given a choice between one $5M/year company at 25% margin vs. five $1M/year companies at 40% margin, Daniel unequivocally prefers five separate businesses — risk diversification against any single business/trend collapsing (cites a real example: a friend nearly bought an Italian-passport-servicing business that looked excellent on paper, didn't close the deal, and six months later the Italian government abruptly changed passport policy, cratering that business's numbers — insider information the seller likely had that motivated the sale).
- General acquisition due-diligence lesson: a business that "looks too good on paper" (unusually high margins relative to peers) warrants extra scrutiny into *why* — the seller may know about an impending regulatory or market shift.

## Hot takes on specific home-service niches
- **Most overrated home service business right now**: power washing — driven by TikTok virality; extremely low barrier to entry (~$300 for a Home Depot pressure washer), leading to an oversaturated, ever-growing pool of new entrants (Daniel, who runs a softwash company himself, still says this "flat out" despite being in the industry).
- Second most overrated: landscaping — very low differentiation/niche potential ("grass grows"), pure price competition, no barrier to entry (Daniel ran one himself at 16 with his dad's tractor).
- If starting completely from scratch: would rather **buy** an existing business (track record de-risks it — most people fail at their first startup) than start one. If starting a service business from zero, he'd pick **HVAC** — a true necessity (unlike roofing, which people will tarp/bucket around rather than fix immediately), and an industry that generally suffers from too many leads and not enough labor (opposite of Daniel's own power-washing business, which has a leads problem, not a capacity problem). Would buy an existing HVAC operator specifically to bypass needing to personally get licensed/credentialed (cites a real anecdote about DOT medical-card licensing friction on one of his trucks).
- Franchise business model critique: believes most franchises are "a waste of money" for the franchisee, though he doesn't consider all franchising a scam — specifically flags franchises that target military veterans with savings and minimal real support (just a logo and email address) as predatory. Describes his ideal franchisee persona: 30-60 years old, $150-500K/year earner, burnt out on employment, has a safety net (six-to-seven figures saved/home equity), craves structure/a proven script rather than creative control — the opposite of an entrepreneurial founder type.
- Contrasting vertical-integration example (Braums, an Oklahoma-based ice-cream/burger/small-grocery chain, ~100-200 corporate-owned locations, no franchising, owns its own dairy farms/cows) vs. a franchise like Cold Stone Creamery for the same product category — Braums can sell a comparable product for roughly 70% less due to no franchise fees (~8% royalty + 2-3% marketing fee cited as typical), no markup-laden supply chain, and lower buildout costs, illustrating how vertical integration/no-franchise models can out-compete franchised peers on price with equal or better quality.

## Personal mindset/psychology notes (tangential, low actionability for a business database but potentially useful context)
- Daniel credits a childhood financial trauma (father quit a stable job to trade stocks poorly, household income collapsed) as the root of his strong "bias for action"/inability to sit idle — frames running a business as literally an anxiety-management mechanism because "there's always something to improve."
- Advocates nightly family gratitude practice (three things each person is grateful for) as a practical, actionable substitute for prayer, and links deepening gratitude to increasing curiosity, which he considers foundational to an entrepreneurial "bias for action" (not something he believes can be taught directly if someone isn't naturally wired that way — recommends leaning into gratitude practice instead).
- Uses a personal "$2M/year" filter for entertaining brand-new business ideas (an idea has to plausibly reach that on paper or he won't act on it) — but treats bolt-on expansions of an existing operation (e.g., a third gym location) differently since the systems/infrastructure risk is already de-risked.

## Caveats / ethics flagged in the episode
- Fake-Facebook-account tactics (multi-posting to bypass platform limits, or manufacturing fake peer recommendations) are explicitly discussed as ethically ambiguous — both speakers acknowledge effectiveness while expressing discomfort, and note fake accounts do get shut down by Facebook periodically.
- Franchise-veteran predation and empty-support franchise models are called out directly as exploitative, without naming specific companies.
