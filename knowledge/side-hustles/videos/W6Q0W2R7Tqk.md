# My 5 Favorite AI Tools in 2026
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W6Q0W2R7Tqk | Published: 2025-04-18

"HoldCo Bros" episode (Chris + Nik/Nikonomics). Nik reports findings from 30 interviews with entrepreneurs about how they actually use AI tools in their businesses; the pair also workshops a real-world billboard marketing experiment and floats a couple of adjacent business ideas. Less a single business case study than a tools/tactics roundup with embedded business-idea brainstorming.

## Survey findings: how 30 real entrepreneurs actually use AI tools (Nik's research)
- **Most preferred primary LLM**: ChatGPT — but Claude is actually used by *more* people in aggregate when counting secondary/tool-stack usage (people often prefer ChatGPT as their main tool while also regularly using Claude for something specific).
- **ChatGPT's dominant use case**: general "thought partner" — brainstorming/ideation ("I have an idea for X, how would I approach Y and Z") more than pure email-rewriting. Usage skews non-technical to intermediate.
- **Claude's dominant use case**: coding assistant — usage skews intermediate to expert/technical. Second most-cited strength: project organization — Claude's "Projects" feature lets users attach persistent instructions/files as ongoing context per project (one interviewee, a professional writer, maintains an evolving "brand voice" file plus recent work samples that she updates over time) — perceived by interviewees as more usable/flexible than ChatGPT's equivalent project/file feature at the time.
- **Perplexity's use case**: real-time web research — multiple interviewees said it performs comparably to "Deep Research" (OpenAI's premium research feature) but crawls the live web in real time.
- **Grok**: no single standout use case, but users were broadly impressed by response speed and access to real-time data, and impressed that xAI built a competitive top-tier model in a short time.
- **Gemini**: least-mentioned/most-criticized model in the survey — but its two validated strengths are (1) best-in-class for automating workflows inside Google Workspace (Docs/Sheets/Gmail), and (2) by far the largest context window among major models at the time.
- **Context window sizes cited** (tokens): ChatGPT ~120,000 (roughly a 300-page book equivalent); Claude ~200,000; Gemini ~1,000,000.
- **Most-used non-LLM AI tool**: Cursor (AI coding IDE) — several technical interviewees said they think "Cursor will be bigger than Google." Multiple experienced coders specifically criticized Replit by comparison.
- **Replit vs. Cursor tradeoff** (Nik's framework, "push-pull of usability vs. functionality"): Replit is more approachable/beginner-friendly but less functional as a serious coding assistant; Cursor is harder to use without some coding background but meaningfully more functional/capable. Framed as a general product-design principle: most products excel at either great UI or great functionality, rarely both (the rare exception, e.g., Airbnb, achieving "unicorn status" by nailing both simultaneously).
- **Nik's personal admission**: has ~7 unfinished projects started in Replit — illustrates a broader theme that AI tools make starting things trivially easy but "the last 10%" (finishing/shipping) remains the hard part; "there's a ton of good ideas, not a lot of good execution."
- **Top overall business use case across the 30 interviews**: sales-related applications — AI used for sales-team follow-up automation, lead tracking, after-hours phone answering, customer-facing chatbots, and call-prep — even though interviewees were business owners/coders, not sales specialists themselves (ruling out sampling bias toward salespeople). Framed with a general startup principle: "stay close to the money" — build tools directly tied to revenue generation (not adjacent functions like HR or admin) for the highest odds of traction, since customers can easily attribute your product's value to money made.

## Real-world marketing experiment: podcast billboard test (Chris)
- Chris put up a physical highway billboard (Clear Channel) for his podcast in Tomball, TX (a ~10,000-person city near Houston), cost ~$950 to print.
- Pre-testing method: had a designer (Simon) generate 34 visually-identical billboard mockup variants (same podcast art, logo, platform icons) differing only in a 2-5 word catchy slogan (AI was used to generate many of the candidate slogans) — examples tested: "Your dream is dying," "9-to-5s kill dreams," "Own your time," "Fire your boss," "Best business podcast," "Quit your job!"
- Ran identical Facebook/Meta ads (across Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, Marketplace) using an actual photo of the physical billboard (with background trees, not just the raw graphic) as the ad creative, geo-targeted to the counties around the billboard location, explicitly excluding anyone who already follows/has interacted with his content (to isolate "cold," unfamiliar audience reaction) — judged winning slogan by lowest cost-per-click.
- Surprising early result: "Best business podcast" outperformed other slogans even among a cold, non-follower audience — Chris was unsure whether this was genuine confirmation-bias-driven trust in a bold claim (referencing a story about Greg Eisenberg becoming an 8-year loyal customer of a dentist purely because a sign said "voted best dentist," with no verification of who voted or why) or contaminated by residual audience recognition — flagged as an open, unresolved question at time of filming.
- Attribution/tracking approach: no QR code (not permitted by Clear Channel); instead used podcast hosting platform Buzzsprout's per-city download export to compute the percentage of total downloads originating from the billboard's target cities (Tomball, The Woodlands, Spring, Houston) as a baseline (measured at 0.012% of all-time downloads), then re-exported weekly to watch for a statistically meaningful increase. Acknowledged limitations: raw download counts don't reveal unique-listener counts (one person downloading 25 episodes vs. 25 different people downloading once each look identical), and any broader traffic spike (e.g., a viral episode) could produce a proportional bump in the target cities that looks like — but isn't — attributable billboard lift. Suggested mitigation: feed the download data to an LLM and ask it to run a regression/statistical-significance model rather than eyeballing raw numbers.
- Suggested (not yet executed) enhancement: run a second, geofenced Facebook ad campaign targeting people whose phone location passes the literal latitude/longitude of the billboard, to create layered brand exposure — traded off against making attribution even messier, but argued that once you're confident something generally works, perfect attribution stops being the point ("I'll spend $780 on a billboard and $1,000 on Facebook ads — who cares about attribution, I know it works").
- Adjacent idea floated (not executed): pick a smaller/lower-competition city (Nik suggested "run a contagion-analysis prompt in ChatGPT asking which US city under 1 million people would spread brand awareness fastest," citing Des Moines' high airport-connectivity as an example output) and fully saturate that one city with ads/brand presence across platforms to become locally famous as a lower-cost, higher-density test of brand marketing versus trying to stand out in an oversaturated major market like NYC/LA/SF.

## Business idea: AI-assisted mediation/arbitration service
- Concept discussed: for business or personal disputes, both parties feed their side of the story (optionally supplemented by recorded life-logging data, e.g., a Limitless AI pendant transcript) into a single shared, neutral AI chat session and have both parties agree in advance on the prompt used to adjudicate — treating the AI as an impartial arbitrator.
- Acknowledged core difficulty: agreeing on the prompt beforehand, and more importantly, agreeing to accept the output afterward (a losing party may simply reject the AI's ruling as invalid, same failure mode as human mediation).
- Framed as a potential productivity/scaling lever for an existing professional mediator's business: a human mediator's business is fundamentally bottlenecked by their own billable hours; if AI tooling (a custom prompting system/wrapper) could compress the information-gathering part of each mediation by even an hour, the mediator could take on meaningfully more clients per month without additional headcount — described as "growing your business in an AI-enabled way" rather than replacing the mediator outright.

## Business idea: a "rating/certification agency" racket
- Concept (attributed to a conversation with Greg Eisenberg): sell small businesses (or SaaS products, or "AI wrapper" products) a paid certification/badge (e.g., "$200 gets you a sign") that lets them claim "voted best [category]" without the claim needing to mean anything rigorous — works because consumers respond to the claim itself, not its actual validity (per the dentist anecdote above).
- Chris drew a parallel to his own prior project "No BS Crypto," describing it as functionally a similar "rating agency" concept in the crypto space.

## Tangential technical build walkthrough (Nik): turning podcast transcripts into a structured entrepreneur-AI-usage database
- Problem: wanted to aggregate and analyze many past podcast transcripts (about AI usage specifically), but combined transcript length exceeded ChatGPT's ~120,000-token context window, so raw transcripts couldn't be fed in directly.
- Workflow used: cleaned transcripts (removed timestamps, filler/dead space, kept speaker labels) → used Google Colab (a hosted Python notebook environment) to write and run a script that combined/processed the transcripts, iterating between ChatGPT (initial script) and Claude (debugging/fixing the script) until it worked → fed the processed output back into ChatGPT to structure it into a clean spreadsheet capturing: interviewee name, business type, industry, technical-skill tier, primary LLM used, main use cases/tasks, motivation for using AI, strengths/weaknesses noted, "ideal user profile," and a short key-insight summary per conversation.
- Initially used Zapier to connect the pipeline to the OpenAI API, then simplified by attaching the OpenAI API key directly inside Google Colab, removing the Zapier dependency.
- End result repurposed as a potential lead magnet: asked ChatGPT to generate an interactive quiz ("which AI model should I use?") for business owners, drawing on the structured interview dataset to power its logic.

## Caveats / risks raised
- Both hosts flag real, unresolved uncertainty about whether their billboard/ad experiment's early "winning" result (the "best business podcast" slogan) reflects a genuine cold-audience marketing insight or a data-attribution artifact from residual audience familiarity — presented as an open experiment, not a proven conclusion.
- Nik explicitly notes his own pattern of starting many AI-assisted coding projects (7+ in Replit) without finishing them — framed as a cautionary, self-aware example of the "good ideas, poor execution" gap that easy AI tooling doesn't automatically solve.
- The mediation-AI and rating-agency ideas are explicitly brainstormed concepts, not validated businesses with real revenue or user data — treat them as speculative, unlike the tool-usage survey findings which are drawn from real interviews.
- Chris acknowledges ChatGPT's apparent "unbiased" quality on factual questions (e.g., debunking industry-funded nutrition studies) is not structural neutrality but simply a different incentive alignment (keeping the user engaged/subscribed) — he explicitly predicts this apparent objectivity "won't stay like this forever" as monetization incentives evolve, and separately notes it tends to be sycophantic/over-flattering on subjective feedback requests unless explicitly prompted to act as a "hard-nosed, no-nonsense" reviewer.
