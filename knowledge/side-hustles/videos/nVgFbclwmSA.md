# The $0 Side Hustle Anyone Can Start
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nVgFbclwmSA | Published: 2026-07-31

Interview with Trevin Peterson (Viral Vue), covering the Amazon Influencer Program: making money by filming simple phone video reviews of products and uploading them to Amazon product listings.

## The business model
- Film a video review of a product with your phone and upload it through the Amazon Influencer Program; when a shopper watches the video on that product's listing and buys, you earn a commission.
- Zero capital required to start: you do not need to buy the product yourself. Any item sold on Amazon qualifies, even if you never bought it there (bought at HomeGoods, found in an Airbnb, borrowed from a family member's house, etc.) — you just need access to the physical item to film it.
- No inventory, no fees, no shipping, no ad spend — commissions go straight to your bank account. Contrasted directly with selling physical products on Amazon (guest's other background), which involves seller fees, shipping, advertising, and tariffs eating into margin.
- You do not need to be an "influencer" in the traditional sense — reviews are shown on Amazon product pages, not on your personal social feed, so there's very low risk of people who know you seeing it. Guest describes clients ranging from teenagers to 70-75-year-old grandparents, including people who "can barely work technology."
- You don't have to show your face — POV-style videos (hands only), voiceovers, or even AI voice translation (used by Spanish-speaking creators to convert audio to English) all work.

## Where videos appear / the competitive mechanic
- Each Amazon product listing has a video icon showing three types of clips: seller-provided videos, "videos related to the product" (this is where Influencer Program videos appear), and customer video reviews.
- There is a "top carousel" with typically 6 available video slots per listing — your video competes only against other creators who reviewed that specific product (unlike YouTube/Instagram where you compete with the entire platform's content).
- There are 30+ million products on Amazon and new ones launch constantly, so competition is fragmented per-product rather than concentrated.
- Amazon rotates and split-tests videos in the carousel; a video that isn't in top rotation initially can still get tested in and take over if it out-converts (watch time) the current occupants.
- Longer videos tend to perform better than short ones because they increase watch time on Amazon.com, which the algorithm favors (Amazon's video-ranking algorithm is described as far less complex than YouTube's/Instagram's).
- Being first to review a hot/newly launched product is an advantage, but arriving late with a better video can still displace existing videos due to ongoing rotation/testing.
- Videos are not yet personalized to the individual searcher (guest predicts this is coming, similar to a fully personalized algorithm), but currently rotate for everyone somewhat.

## Getting approved (two-stage gate)
1. **Account approval**: apply via the Amazon Influencer Program signup page; can use an existing or new Amazon account (linking your shopping account makes tagging purchased products easier but doesn't affect approval odds). Requires linking a public social media account (Instagram, TikTok, Facebook, or YouTube) — must remain public even after approval. A brand-new social account with zero post history will likely be rejected; building up a small following (even ~10-100 followers) with some post history over a few weeks to a couple months is described as sufficient and commonly used as a workaround if you want to keep your main account private.
2. **On-site placement approval** (required for videos to actually appear on product pages / be seen by shoppers): submit 3 videos that are manually reviewed by actual Amazon staff (and screened by an AI bot first). Only 3 attempts allowed total — failing the third means starting over from scratch with new accounts.
   - Rules for the first 3 videos: must be horizontal, good lighting/audio, no loud background noise, no visible license plates/cars/street signs/houses/personal identifying info or labels showing personal data, no price mentions, no direct "buy now"/"click the link" calls to action, and no risky claim words (e.g. "free," "BPA free," "eco-friendly," "sexy," or any health/safety claim that could invite liability).
   - Recommended first-3-videos product picks: simple, low-liability items like a phone tripod, backpack, or soccer ball — avoid vitamins/supplements (health claims) and baby products (safety claims).
   - Must tag the exact matching product (same brand, color, variant) — tagging a similar-but-different item is a compliance violation.
   - After 20-30 total videos performing reasonably (not exceptionally), creators automatically unlock the "Bronze" tier, which grants access to increased/bonus commission campaigns ("Creator Connections") — the tier requirement is about consistency, not video quality/virality.

## Video production guidance
- Horizontal format outperforms vertical: data shows horizontal videos are more discoverable and perform better, partly because desktop shoppers (who do more research/comparison before buying, vs. impulse mobile buyers) see more screen real estate and encounter more of the video carousel.
- Tone: talk to the camera like you're excitedly telling a friend or family member about the product — no script required, though scripting via ChatGPT + a teleprompter app, or filming raw and adding a voiceover in CapCut/similar apps, both work fine.
- "Hook stacking": open by previewing what you'll cover later in the video ("I'll show you how to set it up at the end, but first...") to encourage viewers to keep watching or skip ahead, without needing an aggressive attention-grab like short-form social content requires.
- Sweet spot length: at least ~2 minutes is currently recommended (trend has shifted over time — early on, super-produced videos worked; then very short/low-effort videos worked; now moderate length with decent-but-not-fancy production performs best). Some successful videos are still only 30-60 seconds.
- Equipment: a smartphone's built-in mic is sufficient to start — don't delay posting waiting for gear. Four rough equipment tiers mentioned: phone mic (free) -> ~$25 cheap lav mic -> ~$100 mic -> ~$300 mic (e.g. DJI Mic 3); guest says the average viewer can't tell much difference between tiers, but a good mic can itself become a reviewable product and profit center.
- Comparison videos (e.g., comparing several lav mics side by side) are called out as a particularly effective content format because customers actively research before buying comparison-driven purchases.
- B-roll overlay editing (filming supplemental close-up footage of the product and layering it over the main talking segment) adds retention without needing advanced editing skill.
- Authenticity matters: customers specifically distrust overly polished, AI-generated, or brand-produced marketing video and prefer a "real person" review; guest maintains it's fine to be positive/optimistic even about a flawed product (e.g., "a bit flimsy, but the audio quality is great") but personally declines to review products he considers genuinely bad, even messaging the brand to say so, rather than posting a review for the commission.

## Product/niche selection strategy
- Step one and easiest source of ideas: log into an Amazon account's order history and review things you already own (works even for someone starting with zero disposable income). Also works using someone else's order history you have access to (e.g., a parent's or spouse's account) since eligibility is based on Amazon selling the item, not on your own purchase record.
- Third-party tools (including the guest's own product) can scan Amazon order history going back years to flag which owned products currently have the "most potential" (highest current sales) to review first.
- Key strategic metric: total influencer video count *actually* competing for a listing — Amazon's UI only shows the 6 carousel videos, but a product can have hundreds or thousands of total submitted videos competing behind the scenes. The guest's tool, Viral Vue, surfaces this true competition count so beginners can prioritize higher-demand-but-lower-competition products rather than the ones with 1,000+ existing videos.
- Reviewing everyday, easily-overlooked bathroom/kitchen items that people don't think to review (napkin holders, soap dispensers, shower heads, paper towel holders) can be a low-competition strategy — example given: reviewing a Kohler shower head at a conference took ~60 seconds and earns a modest but real recurring monthly amount.
- Once you find one product in a category that sells well, buying/reviewing similar competing products in that same category is a reasonable extrapolation strategy (worked for the guest with umbrella base stands — see numbers below).

## Commission economics
- Amazon's standard/base influencer commission rate ranges roughly 0.5% to 3% depending on category (rates change periodically — Amazon reportedly cut standard rates in recent months from a 2-5% range down to roughly 0.5-3%).
- Higher-paying standard categories: "outdoor" items (lawnmowers, weed whackers, trimmers) — combination of higher percentage (~3%) and higher unit price. Lower-paying: beauty products (~0.5%).
- The real money is in "Creator Connections" — brand/seller-funded campaigns offering boosted commissions of 10% up to 50% on specific products to incentivize creator content; these are only available to creators at "Bronze" tier and above.
- Guest's own numbers: of $12,000 in commissions over a recent 30-day period, $10,000 came from boosted Creator Connections campaigns and only $2,000 from standard Amazon commission rates — he says he now only reviews products that qualify for a boosted rate.
- Creator Connections program has scaled fast: went from roughly 30,000-50,000 active campaigns to over 800,000 in about a year, per the guest, as brands increasingly recognize creator video content converts sales.
- Interesting dynamic: despite Amazon cutting standard commission rates in recent months, guest reports creators are earning *more* overall than before, because the growth in boosted Creator Connections campaigns has more than offset the base-rate cut — and the rate cut filtered out less-committed creators, reducing competition for the committed ones.

## Reported earnings figures (guest's direct claims — unverified beyond his say-so)
- Beginner, doing this consistently: "easily hundreds of dollars a month," conservatively.
- Typical average creator: $1,000-$3,000/month net commissions.
- Creators who get consistent/decent at it (not necessarily elite): $5,000-$10,000/month.
- Guest himself: $10,000+/month, working "no more than 4-5 hours per week"; has ~5 years of general content creation experience (Instagram) but no formal video/audio training.
- His brother-in-law: made his first 700 videos within the first week of starting (July), and within 3 months the household reached $15,000/month, allowing his sister to quit a six-figure corporate job. That brother-in-law now averages $25,000-$30,000/month in profit off ~700 total videos (note: he does buy the products he reviews as part of a more deliberate purchasing strategy, unlike the 95% of users who review only items they already own or receive for free).
- Another creator with ~1,100 videos made $40,000 in a single recent month.
- A different creator (a friend) with ~4,000 videos made $27,000 in a month — illustrating that pure volume alone isn't the deciding factor; strategic product selection matters more than sheer video count ("volume negates luck," but strategy beats volume alone).
- Roughly an 80/20 (or steeper) distribution applies: a minority of videos/creators account for most of the earnings.
- Anecdotal smaller success stories cited: a stay-at-home mom neighbor bought a car in cash after ~12 months part-time; a mother of four in Idaho earning $12,000/month part-time (her husband has since joined too); users report "first $100 day" milestones in the guest's community.
- Single standout example: a 60-second free-product voiceover video of a pool umbrella base stand, posted ~2.5 years before this interview, still earns him roughly $300-$500/month in ongoing commissions (one single day cited at $150) — driven by 3% base + ~15% boosted commission on a ~$70 product with relatively low competition and an authentic pool-side setting.

## Getting free products from brands
- Once you're active/approved (especially Bronze tier+), you can proactively reach out to brands/sellers and request free products to review — described as extremely low-friction: message the brand as "an Amazon Influencer," include your shipping address in the first message, and products often just start arriving with no negotiation.
- The economics favor brands: their cost on a product may be $3-$10 while it retails for $20-$50, making sending free units an "asymmetric bet" they're happy to make for organic video content.
- Guest and family accumulated so many free products this way that they held two free garage sales to give away the surplus.
- Additional monetization paths beyond Amazon commissions, once you have relationships with brands (mostly overseas/Chinese sellers, per the guest):
  - Reselling the free product after filming it (recovering most/all of its retail value).
  - Being paid directly by brands/agencies for creating the video ($20-$40/video upfront cited as typical), separate from any commission.
  - Selling the "video rights" so the brand can reuse your footage on their own site/social ($40-$50 typical, though guest says some agencies pay as much as $500,000 for UGC-style content in aggregate/bulk deals).
- Ethical note on returns: some creators buy a product, review it, then return it to Amazon and keep the video live (video stays up even after a return) — guest explicitly says he personally doesn't do this and doesn't recommend it, though he knows people who do (sometimes routing the return through a different family member's account to obscure the pattern). Notes Amazon already shuts down shopper accounts for excessive returns generally, independent of the influencer program, so this carries account-risk.

## Cross-posting and additional distribution
- Automation tools (including the guest's own, Viral Vue) can auto-post the same review video to multiple Amazon international marketplaces (US, Canada, UK, Germany, Singapore, Australia) as well as YouTube and Pinterest from a single upload.
- Cross-posting to YouTube (as regular horizontal long-form content) with an Amazon affiliate "deep link" in the description is a second, separate revenue stream (Amazon Associates/affiliate program, distinct from the Influencer Program's on-listing commissions). Guest's own YouTube channel generated ~$500 in a recent 30-day period purely from affiliate link clicks, despite the channel not yet being monetized by YouTube itself.
- "Deep links" matter for affiliate conversion: a normal link opened inside an app's in-app browser (e.g., Instagram) keeps the user trapped in that browser; a deep link instead opens the destination app itself (e.g., the Amazon app, where the user is already logged in with saved payment info), increasing the odds of completed purchases.
- Amazon's "halo effect"/cookie mechanic: once someone clicks your affiliate link, you earn a commission on anything they buy within 24 hours — not just the specific linked product. Example: a viral tweet linking a $3,000 novelty product ($10,000 actual list price, received for free) generated ~$3,000 in affiliate commission from unrelated purchases the visitors made afterward, without selling a single unit of the linked item.

## Scaling the business
- Simplest scaling lever: consistency and volume — "you only fail if you don't upload; you can't really lose money."
- Can hire virtual assistants to shoot, edit, and/or upload videos on your behalf.
- More advanced outsourcing model cited: one user in Canada coordinates ~10 people spread across the US, has brands ship free products directly to each person's address, has them film the reviews, then personally uploads/manages all the resulting content himself — essentially running a "creator farm" without doing any filming himself.
- Program has existed since roughly 2020-2021; guest has been active in it about 3 years. Was more heavily gatekept/low-awareness in its early years; described as still growing rapidly on both sides (creator supply and brand/seller demand via Creator Connections), not yet saturated.
- Available mainly in English-speaking/developed markets currently (heavy usage in US, Canada, UK) but Amazon does support other-language marketplaces (e.g., German-language reviews on Amazon Germany) via the same underlying account structure.

## Tools mentioned
- Viral Vue (viralview.com / viralvue.com) — the guest's own tool (built out of his family's real usage, ~10+ family members active in the program): scans Amazon order history for reviewable products with revenue potential, surfaces the true (not just Amazon-displayed) competing-video count per product to gauge real competition, prioritizes products eligible for boosted Creator Connections commissions, and automates cross-posting/deep-linking across YouTube and other Amazon marketplaces.
- ChatGPT — used to draft video scripts and to sanity-check whether a product type is safe to promote under Amazon's compliance rules.
- A ~$20/year teleprompter app — used to read a scripted review off-screen while filming a straight-to-camera selfie video.
- CapCut / "edits" app — free/near-free tools used for basic splicing and adding voiceover audio to raw product footage.

## Caveats
- Reported earnings (including the $25-30K/month and $40K/month figures) are the guest's personal anecdotes about himself, family, and community members — not independently verified, and he explicitly frames the more extreme numbers as outlier/top-of-top cases, not typical results.
- Success is driven by strategic product selection (low competition + high current sales + boosted commission eligibility) more than raw content quality or sheer volume alone — the two together ("quality and quantity") is characterized as ideal.
- Buy-then-return-while-keeping-the-review-live is described as an ethically questionable practice some creators use; not recommended by the guest and flagged as a likely future enforcement target for Amazon.
- Amazon's compliance rules for the first 3 "approval" videos are strict (no pricing mentions, no calls-to-action, no visible personal info, exact product-tag matching) and getting this stage wrong forces a full restart of the account approval process.
