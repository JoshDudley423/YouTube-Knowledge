# How to Make Money on Airbnb (Even With $0)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pKja9oxDAaQ | Published: 2026-07-05

Interview with Kyle Miklasz, founder of Big Sky Barnhouse / Wild Wild Warehouse — converts overlooked old barns and warehouses in rural Midwest America into large-group "experience" Airbnbs. Covers his acquisition, financing, buildout, and marketing playbook across 15 properties, plus co-hosting as a no-capital entry path.

## Core business thesis
- Don't compete on price in the generic "2-3 bedroom place to sleep" middle of the Airbnb market — that segment got "crushed" post-COVID as interest rates rose and travel patterns shifted. Instead follow a barbell strategy: either the cheapest/smallest units that will always book, or very large (16+ person) group properties where the nightly cost splits painlessly across multiple families — avoid the "messy middle" (generic 2-4BR homes in oversaturated markets like Orlando or Broken Bow, OK).
- Compete on **experience**, not price: build large-group, amenity-dense "destination" properties (indoor pools with swim-up bars and rock climbing walls, helipads, indoor petting zoos, commercial playgrounds, karaoke bars, mini golf) designed specifically to be shareable/viral on social media, rather than copying generic competitor amenities.
- Deliberately invent new/unique amenities rather than copy what's already common in a given vacation market (e.g., most cabins in a market like the Smokies have the same features) — originality is treated as a moat.
- Niche: acquiring **overlooked commercial structures** (century-old dairy barns, abandoned warehouses) in **rural towns** (often under 1,000 people) that are nonetheless within about a 60-minute drive of a fast-growing major metro area. Sources deals off traditional MLS, instead using commercial platforms: Crexi, LoopNet, and Land.com.
- Market-selection criteria for the "metro proximity" side: prioritizes metros with strong population growth, corporate/Fortune 500 relocation activity, and overall economic growth (used the Bourbon Trail near Lexington/Louisville, KY and the fast-growing Des Moines, IA metro as examples) — deliberately avoids only investing near his own home turf ("everyone wants to invest in their backyard," which he calls an overly limiting bias).
- "Moat" framing: pick property types and markets that are hard to literally replicate (e.g., a specific 100+ year old dairy barn that used to feed the surrounding region can't be copy-pasted the way a themed decor concept can) — this defends against competitors copying a successful concept nearby.

## Property #1 case study (Big Sky Barnhouse flagship, Arena, WI)
- Found via monitoring realtor.com's weekly "most organically viral" home listings list — noticed a dilapidated 1800s dairy barn conversion ranked #1 on that list (even above a $50M Tennessee estate), then discovered via public records it had been taken off-market.
- Cold-outreach method: used White Pages to find every phone number matching the owner's last name, made ~50 calls in 30 minutes until reaching the actual owner, then drove out to tour the property in person the same day and got a handshake deal before leaving — explicit advice: for a unique, unconventional ask like this, in-person meetings dramatically outperform remote outreach ("belly to belly").
- Owner had taken it off market because 2023's rising interest rates cooled buyer demand and he'd priced it above what the softened market would bear (originally listed at $945,000).
- Purchase price: $775,000. Commercially reappraised roughly a year later (as a short-term rental business, not a standard residential comp) at $2.1 million.
- Financing: standard structure used across his portfolio — commercial loans through small local community banks, 20% down, 25-year amortization, 3-5 year balloon. For this specific deal, buildout capital came from a business line of credit against a Tennessee property, maxed equity on a Smoky Mountains cabin, a HELOC on his primary residence, and personal savings — he described it as going "all in," risking personal bankruptcy if it failed.
- Renovation/buildout cost: $100,000-$125,000, funded largely by sweat equity (his family: wife, kids, grandparents, siblings, cousins) rather than paid crews for this first property (later properties use full-time paid carpenter crews). Completed the full buildout in 30 days (bought Sept 1, 2023; launched Sept 30, 2023).
- Added amenities: renovated indoor and outdoor pools, deck slides, an indoor jungle gym/commercial playground, a separate "party shed" structure converted with a 9-hole commercial mini-golf course and arcade built where cows used to be milked.
- Pre-launch marketing (the standout tactic): started a brand-new Instagram account with zero followers, posted one Instagram Reel (not even video — static images with amenity callouts) roughly a week after buying the property (while still under construction), then put **$500 total** behind it as a one-time paid Meta ad, geotargeted to nearby major metro zip codes (Madison metro plus other cities within a 3-5 hour drive: Chicago, Minneapolis, Des Moines, southwest Michigan). Used renderings of not-yet-built amenities on the listing, disclosed as such, and updated with real photos post-construction — noted this small risk (a guest seeing real photos differ slightly from renderings after booking) was worth taking.
- Result: that single $500 ad reportedly drove 500,000+ views on the Reel and generated 4-5 bookings/day from launch, with some days hitting 5-6 bookings; average booking size $4,000-$5,000. By the time construction finished and the property actually opened, they had already generated roughly $75,000-$80,000 in advance bookings — before a single guest had stayed.
- Growth trajectory: $200,000 in cumulative bookings by 45 days post-launch, $300,000 by 3 months post-launch. At that point he quit his corporate job (data/software role) to run the business full time, driven partly by demand signals: 10-15 people/day reaching out because desired dates were already booked, and a waitlist of "hundreds" for a second property.
- Pricing mistake acknowledged: ran flat weekend/weeknight rates for the first several months before adopting dynamic pricing software (PriceLabs) — after switching, nightly rates became highly elastic based on real-time demand: dropping from $1,500 to as low as $700-800/night for dates within 2-3 months of check-in with low demand, but climbing as high as $4,000-5,000/night for highly sought-after dates with strong click activity (compared explicitly to airline dynamic pricing). Average booking lead time is 6-8 months out.
- Occupancy: this flagship property runs around 65% occupancy; the broader 15-property portfolio averages 45-48%. Deliberately does not want portfolio occupancy above ~50%, reasoning properties need downtime for maintenance/wear-and-tear repair (analogous reasoning given for other asset classes, e.g., an RV park at 100% occupancy signals underpricing; 93-95% is closer to an ideal equilibrium).
- 2025 financial results for this single flagship property: ~$330,000 revenue, ~$130,000-$140,000 net profit after operating expenses (low-40% margin) — after also refinancing at the new $2.1M appraised value (70% loan-to-value, 30% equity retained), pulling out cash that funded acquisition of three additional properties. Refinanced note payment rose from roughly $4,000-5,000/month to about $9,000/month.
- Attributes ~10-20% of bookings to organic drive-by/local discovery (helped by a newly added 100-foot highway-facing mural), aided by being located directly on a well-traveled US highway (a hotel-style location strategy rather than a typical hidden vacation-rental approach) and adjacent to existing regional tourism (Frank Lloyd Wright sites in Spring Green, WI).

## Property scaling / newest launch: Wild Wild Warehouse (Lacona, IA, ~45-50 min from Des Moines)
- A separate but related brand (distinct from the 14-property Big Sky Barnhouse brand) built around converting large-format commercial warehouse space rather than barns — chosen because warehouses offer much larger open floor plates (this one: ~7,000 sq ft as bought, expanded to 8,000 sq ft with an added second-story "motel style" overlook of new bedrooms, 16-20 ft ceilings) allowing many big-ticket amenities to be "stacked" in one open space.
- Financing structure (his current standard approach for newer deals): a single commercial loan covering both acquisition and buildout costs combined, with 20% down on the full combined project cost. For this property: $400,000 purchase price + $550,000 buildout = $950,000 total project, 20% down on the full $950K (rather than needing separate financing/equity for the renovation).
- Buildout completed in 40 days. Amenities included: an indoor heated year-round pool room, a full-court basketball/soccer/football-toss arena, a commercial-grade playground (deliberately positioned against typical backyard wooden playsets, which age/rot — chose durable commercial-grade equipment instead), a custom infinity mini-golf course wrapping the playground, a themed "cowboy karaoke bar" with a real karaoke machine, custom murals throughout by a partnered artist (Eric Herald), and 300 ceiling-mounted hex lights with a music-reactive mode, plus an outdoor fire pit and hot tub.
- Sleeps up to 30 guests.
- Launch performance: opened the booking page June 4; by June 29 (25 days later) had generated roughly $190,000 in bookings and 15 million+ views across social/influencer content — described as by far the most successful launch of any of his properties to date, on pace to potentially surpass the flagship's performance within 2-3 months.
- Marketing for this launch relied entirely on organic content (no paid Meta ads at all at this stage of the company) — an in-house media/content team plus a small number of travel influencers given free stays (with their friends/family) in exchange for content and collaborative posts. Notes the shift away from paid ads happened once the brand had already built a large organic following, making in-house content production a better use of marketing dollars than continued ad spend.

## Financing/growth mechanics across the portfolio
- 35% of bookings now come through a direct booking website rather than OTAs (Airbnb, VRBO) — reduces platform dependency and fees over time.
- Sizing thesis: initially targeted a "magic number" of sleeping 20 guests per property; has since trended toward 30-40 person capacity properties (40 is the largest built so far) — beyond roughly 30 guests, bookings shift more toward corporate retreats and youth groups rather than family reunions.
- Party/liability risk management: mitigates destructive or unauthorized (e.g., teenage) parties primarily through positioning and guest-avatar-targeted marketing (family-focused branding attracts family-focused bookers) rather than heavy manual vetting; acknowledges some incidents have still slipped through, including law enforcement having to remove groups using fraudulent booking accounts, and credits Airbnb's platform-level anti-fraud measures for reducing this over time.
- Portfolio-wide 2025 pacing: approximately $3,000,000 gross revenue across all properties, with gross profit margins in the 40% range.

## Tools and tech stack named
- **Hospitable** — property management software used to sync all direct-booking and OTA listings, automate multi-touchpoint guest messaging (about 10 automated messages from inquiry through post-checkout), and as the API backbone for their own direct booking website. Described as the "glue" of operations.
- **BNB Calc** — a short-term rental market/deal analysis tool (compared favorably to AirDNA) that generates customizable, brandable property performance projections for a given address; used both for internal acquisition analysis and, notably, as a sales tool when pitching prospective co-hosting clients (shows the owner a credible projection of what their property could earn). Also has a market-comparison tool for evaluating markets or competing property managers side by side.
- **Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT** — used heavily for generating design/renovation renderings and creative inspiration (e.g., visualizing an indoor pool with a rock-climbing wall and swim-up bar, or converting an empty warehouse shell into a themed concept) — cited as replacing what used to require CAD drawings and architects taking weeks, now producing usable renderings within minutes.
- **PriceLabs** — dynamic pricing software (mentioned above) used to replace flat-rate pricing with demand-based nightly rate adjustments.

## Co-hosting as a low/no-capital entry path (the "$0" part of the title)
- Airbnb "arbitrage" (leasing a property long-term, furnishing it cheaply — cited example ~$5,000 in furniture — then subletting it short-term on Airbnb with landlord permission) was a viable low-capital strategy roughly 2010-2020, but Kyle says it has become "immensely harder" to succeed with today, though some operators still do it well.
- Kyle's recommended better path for someone with no capital: **co-hosting** — effectively acting as a property manager (but more hands-on/involved) for someone else's existing property who either isn't yet on Airbnb, is unhappy with their current property manager, or owns a second home they're not actively renting.
- Typical co-host compensation cited: roughly 20-30% of gross revenue generated.
- Path to legitimacy/landing clients: come across as a credible expert rather than a random cold-pitch — specifically, use a tool like BNB Calc to generate a customized, professional-looking revenue projection/performance report for the target property and use that as the centerpiece of your pitch, rather than making a vague, unsubstantiated offer to "manage their Airbnb."
- Cited anecdotal outcomes: knows people who signed 10-20 co-hosting clients within as short as 6 months, later using that cash flow to begin buying their own properties.
- Caveat on renovation-heavy properties: co-hosting works more easily for properties that are largely renovation-ready (5-6 bedroom homes in a good market that are simply mismanaged or not yet listed) — if a property genuinely needs major capital improvements, the co-host has to convince the *owner* to fund those renovations themselves, since the co-host typically isn't putting up renovation capital.
- Recommended prep before attempting outreach: build baseline market knowledge first by consuming existing free content from established short-term-rental/co-hosting educators — named John Bianchi, Jeremy Werden, Bill Faeth, and Patrick Sweitzer (who Kyle describes as running one of the largest co-hosting companies and whose conference he'd attended) — rather than cold-pitching with no foundational knowledge.
- Suggested cold-outreach pitch structure once prepared: research a property (tax records, Zillow/Redfin) in a high-demand area/unit type, then approach the owner with a specific, data-backed pitch (e.g., "your property could rent for $1,200/night at ~50% occupancy; I'll manage everything, no risk to you, for 30% of gross revenue").

## Other niches/property types the guest flagged as promising but isn't personally pursuing
- Homes with a private, non-shared pond or lake on the property.
- Large-acreage properties with room for amenities like ATV trails, golf-related features (mini golf, a par-3 hole, home putting green, bunkers, golf carts) — golf-adjacent amenities specifically called out as broadly appealing across different property concepts.
- If choosing NOT to go large-scale/group-oriented, his advice is to go very small instead (one-bedroom cottage/couples getaway) rather than the crowded 2-4 bedroom middle — and specifically suggested a "resort" model of many (10-20+) individually-listed one-bedroom couples cabins clustered around shared outdoor amenities (pond, grounds) as a scalable version of the small-property approach.
- Generally advises avoiding urban city centers for this large-group/unique-structure model due to zoning restrictions and neighbor complaint risk.

## Caveats
- All revenue/profit figures are the guest's self-reported numbers, not independently verified.
- Results are explicitly framed by the host as "not typical" for the flagship property's $500-ad outcome; the guest's outsized success also involved significant personal financial risk (maxed-out credit lines, HELOC, near-bankruptcy exposure) that he acknowledges could have failed.
- Co-hosting and arbitrage are both explicitly distinguished from "scammy" Airbnb arbitrage courses/gurus the host has seen — the guest frames co-hosting as legitimate but still requiring real persistence, market knowledge, and professionalism, not a guaranteed or trivially easy path.
