# Starting a Business Has Never Been Easier. Here's How to Win
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6aUtwj27IpE | Published: 2025-06-21

"HoldCo Bros" episode with Nik (Nikonomics Podcast) — a rapid-fire brainstorm of many small business ideas, mostly leveraging AI/automation for cold outreach and fulfillment. Dense idea list; grouped by concept below.

## Real examples cited (proof AI has lowered the barrier to entry)
- A guy selling "automations as a service" (AI automation implementation) to Fortune 500 companies at $7,000/month, with a 33% close rate (in-person only) and low churn. His playbook: (1) a strong cold email; (2) closes exclusively in person; (3) a guarantee — "I'll meet with you 30 minutes in person and find ways to save you more money than my service costs; if I can't, I'll donate [X] to a charity of your choice." He also proactively discloses his young age (20s/early 30s), which combined with the charity guarantee reportedly resonates strongly with Fortune 500 white-collar buyers. His actual offering: interviews employees about their role/responsibilities, then builds custom automations that make them more effective (framed as empowering, not replacing, staff) — found by scraping which roles a company is hiring for on LinkedIn/Indeed as a signal of pain points.
- A 24-year-old accountant who sold a prior company, owns 8 rental duplexes, and runs a side business teaching CPA firms to implement automations. Charges just $50/month for an automated accounting service to 140 clients (~$7,000/month from that alone) plus upsells automation implementation work (another ~$8,000/month) — ~$16,000/month combined, ~1 hour/week of his own time. Deliberately underprices relative to market (typical outsourced bookkeeping runs $1,000-$2,000/month) as a low-friction foot-in-the-door strategy, then upsells once trust is established. Kept his day job purely for a sense of security, not financial need.
- Lesson drawn: cold outreach has never been cheaper/easier (tools: Lemlist, GMass, AI cold calls, ringless voicemail drops, mass texting) AND fulfillment/build capability has never been easier (no-code/AI app builders), so the combination removes historical excuses not to start something.

## Piggybacking framework (recurring theme — attach to an existing business's customer base instead of finding your own)
- **Fabric ceilings for pergolas**: fabric "sail" ceilings (popular in the Mediterranean/Greece, not common in the US) installed as an upsell add-on to pergola installation companies (pergolas cost $8,000-$15,000+). Pitch: "I install fabric ceilings, you upcharge the customer ~$1,000, you keep $800, I keep $200 for referring/doing the labor." Estimated $200-$300/hour of value captured for a couple hours of installation labor.
- **B2B stump grinding** (referenced from an earlier episode/"HoldCo Bros" #37): buying a stump grinder and renting out grinding services to tree-trimming companies that don't want to own their own $50,000+ machine — described as inventing a new B2B category. Real example: "Tyler" did $7,500-$9,000 his first month, $20,000 second month, and $30,000/month two months prior to this recording, essentially solo, from a $60,000 financed stump grinder (~$450/month payment), a financed truck (~$450/month) and trailer, and a $250/month parking lot — no building needed.
- **B2B pool digging (excavator rental/service)**: a guy with no website, no Google Business Profile — just a phone number and relationships with pool companies — rents an excavator from United Rentals (~$700/day; excavators cost ~$70-80K to buy) and does the digging portion of pool installs for ~$5,000/job (pool companies subcontract everything: digging, framing, plaster, landscaping/tile, slide install — none of it is done in-house, so they're not price-sensitive about any one line item on a $100K-$150K pool). Referenced pool costs: ~$70K in 2019, now $100K-$150K+ in Texas.
- **General piggyback method for high-ticket home services**: identify big-ticket categories (pools, tree trimming, landscaping/hardscaping, deck/patio installation, fencing) and find the "annoying" sub-task those contractors don't want to own themselves, then offer that sub-task as a rented service: e.g., disposal/dumpster rental for landscapers/deck removal (can start with just a trailer and truck hitch before buying an actual dumpster), auger-as-a-service for post-hole digging ahead of fence installation (cites personally paying ~$350 for someone to dig 13 post holes), or land-grading with a rented Bobcat ahead of a big backyard landscaping job.
- **Validation method for piggyback ideas** (same method Tyler used): scrape a list of the target contractor type in a market, test demand there before spending money on equipment, and only invest once demand is confirmed.

## Skilled-trade / hands-on-skills business ideas
- **"Fantasy camp" model for adult skill-building**: referenced a real business, "Metal Shop Fantasy Camp," where adults pay to spend a few hours learning to weld (e.g., $130 for a 1-hour welding intro, $250 to make a metal cube, $190 for a steel bowl class) — not a certification, just hands-on experiential learning. Chris/Nik hypothesize this format works because it satisfies an innate desire to create something tangible with your hands (a physical object you can show someone), and speculate it might generalize to woodworking/cabinetry (creation-oriented) more readily than pure home-repair skills (fixing, not creating).
- **"Handyman mentor" marketplace** (Chris's favorite idea in the episode; he bought the domain handymans.com): a marketplace connecting people who don't know basic home skills (fixing a sprinkler, changing a breaker, basic plumbing) with retired tradesmen/older "boomers" who know how to do it and might enjoy mentoring — NOT a same-day licensed contractor dispatch service, but a low-stakes, judgment-free peer-mentoring connection. Motivation cited: contractors for HVAC/plumbing/electrical are typically booked out weeks/months, but many home issues don't require licensed expertise, just someone experienced to show you how.
  - Go-to-market plan discussed: start hyper-locally and manually (a "do things that don't scale" approach) — physically go to Home Depot/Lowe's across shifts to recruit older, knowledgeable retail employees/shoppers as "mentors" (collect name/phone/email — building the supply side of a two-sided marketplace first), separately recruit "helpless millennials" as demand via targeted Facebook ads to the local geography, then manually matchmake via group texts; observe how interactions naturally happen (e.g., discovering mentors prefer in-person visits over FaceTime) before locking in a pricing/service structure. Small payment (e.g., Venmo $20) to the mentor for the exchange of value, rather than a large fee.
  - Explicitly framed as one of the few ideas in the episode they'd consider raising venture capital for, once proven in one city (e.g., Greenville, TX) — the logic being it's a "sweaty," manual-effort launch that could scale nationally like the historical "unbundling" of cable/social platforms once proven.
  - Adjacent variant mentioned: a friend "Hector" sells HVAC certification courses (~$8,000-$12,000, often government-funded) that include a VR/Oculus headset (~$400) with white-labeled software simulating HVAC repair "in the metaverse."
  - Adjacent variant: an "AI handyman mentor" subscription (~$10-20/month) wrapping ChatGPT's live video/FaceTime feature — Chris demonstrated using ChatGPT's voice+camera mode to diagnose his own broken sprinkler in real time (correctly diagnosed a low point in the yard needing a check valve) and his own HVAC issue (walked him through checking the coil, reading the unit's warranty label, etc.). Claim: most people don't know ChatGPT's live camera/voice feature can do this, so a simple wrapped/branded product could capture that value.
  - Adjacent variant: a mail-order hands-on DIY kit/course — e.g., a small drywall practice panel with materials (drywall scrap, electrical wiring, outlet cover) mailed to the customer with a guided course teaching a specific skill (like cutting a drywall opening for an outlet) without risking damage to their real house. Suggested partnering with (piggybacking off) an existing DIY influencer on Instagram/TikTok/YouTube who already has an audience, rather than building the audience from scratch.

## AI-tool arbitrage / consulting-style ideas
- **Custom GPTs as a sellable product**: build a GPT (or a simple chat "wrapper" app via Replit/Lovable/etc.) encoding your own specific expertise (e.g., "help me decide which business to buy," "help me write a book") as a structured, interactive Q&A flow, then sell/share it via the GPT Store or as a standalone wrapped app. Key process tip: use ChatGPT itself to write the GPT's own system prompt/instructions (describe your goal, ask it to draft the prompt, paste directly into the custom GPT builder) — described as accessible to non-technical people.
- **Underpricing as a deliberate go-to-market wedge**: the $50/month CPA-automation example above illustrates deliberately pricing far below market to make adoption a no-brainer, then monetizing via high-trust upsells once the customer relationship is established, rather than leading with a $1,500-$4,000/month agency-style price that's harder to sell cold.
- **"Running away from friction" framework** (Chris's stated business philosophy): the goal of getting a first paying customer fast isn't really about revenue — it's about reducing friction/preserving momentum so you don't abandon the venture; conversely, quickly recognizing and exiting the ~75 of his ~80 started businesses that didn't reduce friction fast enough (rather than "running toward monetization") is presented as the more useful mental model than chasing a monetization milestone per se.

## Family/relationship-tech ideas (tangential to side hustles, but pitched as buildable products)
- **AI-facilitated family trivia game app**: use ChatGPT's voice mode to generate an age-appropriate, personalized trivia game among family members (demoed: dad vs. 9-year-old son, later extended to 3 people each with their own topic — e.g., Minecraft, geography, history). Business idea: build this as a lightweight mobile app/wrapper (suggested tools: Lovable, Replit, Windsurf, Cursor, Bolt, v0, Vercel) charging a small fee (~$3/month or ~$20 lifetime); can be reskinned into niche variants (father-son, mother-daughter trivia, etc.) and tested for which resonates, since the backend logic is reusable across niches.
- **AI-facilitated family storytelling/legacy capture app** ("One Life Tale," a real product being co-developed by a named associate, Owen): an AI-guided conversation tool that prompts family members (including elderly relatives, e.g., in assisted living) to tell/record their life stories (using ElevenLabs on the backend to preserve voice), consolidating multiple people's input into a narrated story; discussed extending it into a phone-call-based AI voice agent so elderly users could simply call a number and be interviewed about their life stories without needing to learn an app.

## Home-services + AI image generation idea (a strong, concretely-numbered idea)
- **AI "before/after" visualization to remove sales friction for home-improvement services** (painting, roofing, windows, gutters, landscaping): use AI image generation to show a homeowner what their own house would look like with a proposed change (new paint color, new roof, new windows) based on a photo of their actual home (e.g., pulled from Google Street View), dramatically increasing close rates by removing the "I can't picture it" friction. Chris live-demoed generating a renovation visualization of his own overgrown yard from his own photos, calling the output impressively accurate.
- Related real-world case study (an operator named "Hire Bus"): built a Replit-based app that pulled homeowner addresses, grabbed each house's Google Street View image, and printed custom postcards showing that homeowner's own house with a QR code linking to a pitch video. Results: 15% engagement rate, 3% scheduled a call, 1% closed — at ~$1 cost per postcard (~$100 per 100 sent), this produced roughly 1 closed deal worth an average $4,000 ticket per $100 spent.
- Extension idea floated: apply the same "AI-edited house photo" concept to physical flyers/mailers (e.g., mail a neighborhood of red-brick homes a mockup of their house painted white to provoke reactions/conversation — even negative reactions ("that's ugly") generate organic social sharing/free marketing) or give door-to-door sales reps a tablet app to instantly show a homeowner an AI-edited preview of their house with a proposed change during the pitch itself.

## Direct-mail arbitrage ideas
- **"Wedding/baby shower invite to corporations" gift-solicitation service**: inspired by a real example of a newlywed who mailed ~150 physical wedding-shower invitations to major corporate addresses (Target, Bed Bath & Beyond, etc.) and received gifts/gift cards/cash from ~25 of them (companies do this deliberately because recipients often post about the gift on social media — free marketing for the brand). Proposed productized version: build a website/database of ~1,000+ corporate addresses (mix of large and small companies); customer selects how many invitations to send (e.g., 50/500/1,000), payment covers cost via a direct-mail API (~$0.45/invitation cited), charge a flat service fee (e.g., $199) with margin baked in, market with a stated expectation (e.g., "customers typically receive $500-$1,000 in free gifts").
- **Robotic handwritten direct mail for response-rate lift**: a service (ghosthandwriter.com, mentioned by name) that has robots physically handwrite marketing letters on paper — response rates are reportedly much higher than printed/computer-generated "handwriting" fonts because it looks genuinely handwritten. Contrasted with a cheaper prior approach (uploading a CSV to a postcard-printing service using a simulated handwriting font).

## Caveats / risks
- Several ideas in this episode are explicitly framed as speculative brainstorm-level ("someone should build this") rather than validated businesses with real revenue numbers — treat the trivia app, storytelling app, and mail-order DIY kit as unvalidated concepts, unlike the stump grinding, pool digging, and postcard/AI-image case studies, which have real reported numbers.
- The handyman-mentor marketplace is explicitly described as needing an experimental, flexible launch (pricing/structure not decided up front) because "people aren't doing this" yet — it's positioned as higher-risk/higher-reward and is the one idea they'd consider raising venture money for after local proof of concept, unlike most of the smaller piggyback ideas which are meant to be self-funded and cash-flow-first.
- The Fortune-500 automation-consultant example and the $50/month CPA-automation example both depend on scarce/valuable technical skill (automation/AI implementation) — not turnkey for someone without that background.
