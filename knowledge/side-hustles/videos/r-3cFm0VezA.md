# AI Won't Replace You If You Do This!
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r-3cFm0VezA | Published: 2025-03-21

Part 1 of a two-part conversation between Chris Koerner and Connor Gross (bizbrainstorms.com) on practical AI applications for business and personal productivity. Less structured "business idea" content than other episodes — mostly practical AI-use case discussion with a few concrete business angles.

## Running multiple autonomous AI agents in parallel (the headline anecdote)
- Chris ran ChatGPT's "Operator" agent feature in 10 browser tabs simultaneously to test concurrency; found 6 could run at once before hitting a cap (7th attempt failed), so he'd close a finished tab and launch the next, cycling through all 10.
- What the parallel agents were doing: one was scanning for arbitrage opportunities in used cars, cross-referencing NADA and Kelly Blue Book values; another was scraping Chamber of Commerce websites for email addresses (had ~100 tabs open within its own session); others ran with no supervision needed.
- Framing/thesis: this is "as high-leverage as it gets" — effectively hiring hundreds of workers via a ~$200/month subscription, and is presented as a preview of how few employees future entrepreneurs may need.

## AI for personal/executive-assistant leverage — a concrete case study
- A friend's significant other, in a sales role, built a small non-technical automation: their company round-robins inbound sales leads into a Slack channel, giving reps 15-30 minutes to respond before a lead becomes "up for grabs" to whoever reacts fastest (via clicking a link that auto-assigns the lead in the CRM). This rep — not a developer — used an AI app-building tool to create a program that runs on their computer, watches the Slack channel, and instantly auto-clicks the claim link the moment a lead posts, making them the top sales rep at the company by capturing nearly every lead.
- Framing/lesson: for anyone in a commission-based or leverage-sensitive role, learning to build even simple automations like this can "2-3x your salary" without doing more actual work; described as a case for individual employees using AI to make themselves dramatically more valuable rather than being replaced by it.

## AI adoption gap anecdote
- A relative took a sales job at a ~$130M family-owned, "old school"/Boomer-run company (~100-something employees); when he casually mentioned ChatGPT to colleagues, most had no idea what it was (one asked if it was a cryptocurrency). Self-reported estimate: only ~5% of his colleagues use AI even occasionally (host initially assumed people wouldn't believe a number that low and almost inflated it to 15% before confirming 5% was accurate).
- Takeaway: in less tech-forward industries/companies, baseline AI literacy is dramatically lower than in tech/entrepreneurial circles — an opportunity for early-adopting employees or vendors serving these companies.

## Concrete use case: AI for legal/insurance document review
- Chris's 60-year-old father, not tech-savvy, needed to figure out what he was eligible for on an insurance claim; sent a 125-page insurance policy binder PDF to Chris, who uploaded it to Claude/ChatGPT with the prompt "here's exactly what happened, find everything relevant," and returned a formatted summary (with specific page/section references) as a Google Doc/PDF within about 15 minutes total. Cited as a "textbook, cut-and-dry" high-value personal use case for large document analysis.

## Business idea sketches discussed (less developed than a typical episode)
- **Niche AI-powered "employee replacement" services**, argued to work best when built around a specific, narrow role rather than a general-purpose wrapper (broad tools like ChatGPT itself already do everything reasonably well, so a wrapper needs a specific niche to defend). Named candidate roles: executive assistant services (framed as the biggest opportunity — ~304,000 executive assistants employed in the US per cited job-market figures, and most offshore-recruiting agencies already funnel candidates toward this role specifically, rather than more niche roles like video editors or bookkeepers), bookkeeping automation/software, and a more targeted "admin"/project-manager-style AI service (deliberately avoided over-broad "admin" framing since it's viewed as increasingly commoditized/crowded, e.g., a company recently raised $50M to build an AI sales-process agent, illustrating how much capital is chasing generic agent wrappers).
- **AI-assisted recruiting/talent evaluation tooling**: discussion of where AI can and can't yet help a recruiting business — sourcing/keyword-matching candidates (e.g., via LinkedIn Recruiter filters) is already largely solved by existing filters, so no major AI edge there; the harder, still-unsolved problem flagged as a real opportunity is using AI to evaluate candidate *quality/fit* (e.g., analyzing a submitted Loom video or demo call to gauge candidate signal) rather than just keyword-matching resumes.
- **Third-party visibility layer for applicant tracking systems (ATS)**: a recruiting-agency pain point raised — most ATS tools don't let a client see the full candidate pipeline in real time, forcing agencies to manually copy candidates into a shared spreadsheet; floated idea of having an AI agent with browser/mouse-click training (referencing ChatGPT Operator's ability to learn from recorded manual actions) log into the ATS and handle this in place of an API integration, when no such official integration exists.
- **Tools named/mentioned as relevant to these workflows**: Limitless AI (a wearable + app, rebranded from "Rewind," that passively records audio/screen activity so you can later query it — e.g., "what was that tweet I saw three weeks ago" — explicitly noted as not yet capable of the more ambitious "watches how you work for a month and then executes tasks the way you would" vision); Lindy (described as "Zapier plus AI," used as an example of chaining a trigger like "email a codeword to Lindy" into pulling data from a CRM/HubSpot, generating a custom payment link, and sending it to a client automatically).

## Broader/strategic points raised (less directly actionable, more framing)
- Argument that in AI-wrapper businesses, the AI itself is not the defensible value; the real value is that the product is "that much easier" to use for a specific audience ("it feels like magic") — positioning and distribution matter more than the underlying model, especially since functionally similar wrappers can be built by many competitors.
- Distribution/first-mover analogy: cites a claimed ~10x sales gap between Bud Light and the second-place cheap domestic beer (Miller Lite) pre-scandal as an example of how dominant the #1 mental-availability slot can be in any category, used to argue why so much capital is chasing "be the #1 AI [wrapper] in a category" bets.
- Skepticism voiced (Koerner) that even a well-funded, well-designed AI wrapper app achieves fast mass-market penetration, given how small the share of the general population is currently aware such tools/use-cases exist — described as still "early innings" in terms of mainstream (non-tech, non-entrepreneurial) adoption.

## Caveats
- This episode is lighter on concrete, replicable "start this business" playbooks compared to other Koerner Office episodes — most content is anecdote/observation about AI capability and adoption rather than a fully specified business model with numbers.
- No revenue, cost, or pricing figures are given for any of the sketched "niche AI service" ideas (executive assistant AI, bookkeeping AI, recruiting-evaluation AI) — these are directional opportunity flags, not validated case studies.
