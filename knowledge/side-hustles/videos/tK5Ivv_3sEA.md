# The Side Hustle AI Was Supposed to Kill Is Booming
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tK5Ivv_3sEA | Published: 20260918

Interview with Steph Spector, a freelance writer/editor/coach making roughly $6-7K/month, on why companies still pay for human-written content in the AI era.

## The business idea
- Freelance B2B content writing (blog posts, comparison/alternatives articles, tutorials) for SaaS/tech companies, using AI as a research and drafting assistant but not as a replacement for the writer's own structure, voice, and firsthand product testing.
- Side/adjacent revenue lines: editing resumes for an executive writing coach, coaching college-application essay writing, general freelance editing.
- Framed against a real market signal: Google Trends data for "hire a writer" dipped when ChatGPT launched in 2023, then reversed and has been climbing for the past two years — demand for writers went up, not down, once the initial AI substitution effect wore off.

## Numbers
- Current rate: $900-1,200 per blog post, ~2,500-3,000 words, taking about 5-6 hours per article — roughly $150-240/hour effective rate.
- Of the finished words in a typical article, she estimates ~70-80% end up being her own writing/phrasing even with AI assistance; the underlying idea/structure/heading plan is close to 100% hers.
- Current monthly income: ~$6,000-7,000, mostly from writing, plus periodic income from college-essay coaching (sold in packages of 5 sessions at $90/hour = $450/package) and other coaching work.
- Client examples named: Zapier, Jotform, SaaSWorthy — any B2B SaaS company was cited as a realistic prospect; harder-to-break-into targets include the AI labs themselves (OpenAI/Anthropic), though those companies do hire AI-model trainers/writers at ~$90/hour.
- A tutorial article she wrote for Zapier (documenting a new MCP integration) was later referenced/linked by Anthropic in one of their own newsletters, driving significant traffic back to Zapier's site — cited as concrete proof of a $1,000 article's downstream value.
- Cold-pitch success example: contacted a company after genuinely trying their product, offered to write for their blog, and was hired on the spot with no negotiation.
- As an experiment for this interview, she posted a $20 job on Upwork asking established freelance writers (making $3,000+/month) for a 20-minute paid conversation about how newcomers can break in; the post got 15 thoughtful applications in 2 days, and several respondents stayed on the call well past the paid 20 minutes.

## Process / steps
- AI-assisted writing workflow (learned at a prior full-time job, Zapier): feed the AI a written style guide (capitalization rules, formatting conventions, banned words) plus reusable content templates so AI-generated drafts already match house style and structure; for tutorials, screen-record yourself performing the task while narrating your live reactions/confusion points ("What if I click here?"), then feed that recording through one AI tool for video/audio transcription and a second AI tool (chosen for its writing quality) to turn the transcript + template into a first draft, which she then hand-edits. Reported model preferences: Gemini for processing video/audio, Claude for marketing/creative writing, ChatGPT for research.
- Research phase (much of the 5-6 hours per article): personally sign up for and use the tool/product being written about rather than only reading about it; read Reddit threads and niche community discussions to find real user complaints/confusion (e.g. about pricing) worth digging into and addressing in the article.
- Finding clients: started and continues to use Upwork (10 years running) — apply to ~10 carefully chosen jobs/day rather than spamming every listing, favor postings with growth potential over one-off gigs, and proactively offer to expand scope (e.g. also handle content uploading) once a good working relationship is established.
- Cold outreach beyond job boards: engage genuinely with people on social media (comments, DMs) rather than mass-blasting; a strong cold pitch is 4-5 sentences that reference something specific and true about the target (not just their most recent post) — one detailed reference pulled from an entire podcast episode led directly to a client call.
- Building a portfolio from zero: produce 4-5 pieces (e.g. 3 blog posts + a landing page) even without a paying client yet — write speculative pieces for real companies you admire and simply give the piece away rather than claiming it was a paid assignment; host the portfolio on a simple personal website rather than a shared drive folder.
- Starting rate guidance: many successful freelancers on Upwork start around $30-40/hour; expect some client pushback/negotiation on rate and treat that as normal, not a sign to quit.
- Project scoping: for a defined single deliverable, gather word/page count expectations and review any existing copy up front to estimate hours needed, then quote a flat project-based price (not hourly) once scope is clear — clients prefer no-surprises pricing even if it's internally calculated off an hourly rate. Suggested using a meeting-recording/notetaking AI tool (Granola) during scoping calls with a prospect's permission to capture requirements accurately.

## Tools/platforms named
- ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — used deliberately for different strengths (research, writing/voice-matching, video/audio processing respectively).
- Cursor — routes a workflow across multiple AI models in one place (e.g. send video to Gemini, send resulting notes to Claude for drafting).
- Granola — AI meeting transcription/notetaking tool, used to capture client scoping calls.
- Upwork — primary client-sourcing platform, both as a freelancer seeking work and (in this interview, as an experiment) as an "employer" paying other freelancers for insight.

## Caveats / risks
- She has personally tried and failed to fully automate her own writing style by uploading 100 past articles to an AI tool — it captures voice somewhat but has no access to her personal memories/experiences, which is often where an article's opening hook comes from.
- Warns against over-automating the writing process end-to-end: writers who try to configure AI to do everything often spend more time managing the tooling than they save, end up with lower-quality output, and describe "AI brain fry" from overuse.
- Notes a real corrective cycle already visible in the market: some companies laid off writers to go all-AI, then rehired freelancers later once quality suffered — cited as evidence that full replacement isn't holding up in practice.
- References Anthropic's AI-content watermarking as an emerging trend (alongside Snapchat banning AI video and YouTube demonetizing faceless AI channels) toward platforms and audiences favoring visibly human-made content — though notes tools already exist that claim to strip such watermarks.
- If a writer's income depends on being visibly more valuable than raw AI output, continuing to develop a personal voice/expertise (rather than letting AI do all the writing) is framed as necessary to stay ahead, not optional.
