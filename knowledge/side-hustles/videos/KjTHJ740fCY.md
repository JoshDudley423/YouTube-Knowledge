# The Best Side Hustle for Beginners (Any Age)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KjTHJ740fCY | Published: 2026-05-24

Interview with Lucas and John (early 20s, "Reselling University" on Instagram), two friends from rural NC/VA who scaled from selling childhood Legos to a wholesale used-electronics reselling operation on eBay.

**Business model progression (explicit staged playbook)**
1. Sell things already in your house (their start: Lucas, age 15, sold ~$5-10K worth of his own Lego collection on eBay over one summer using his mom's account).
2. Once out of personal items, buy from thrift stores/yard sales/flea markets, resell on eBay -- this stage nets an estimated extra $1,000-$5,000/month for someone spending just 2-5 hours/week ("weekend warrior" level), according to the hosts' recommended entry point for anyone.
3. Once you deeply know one category (they say you need to have sold 100+ items in a category to "really know it"), start buying in bulk lots (via eBay, Marketplace, or wholesalers) rather than one item at a time.
4. Eventually build direct relationships with recurring suppliers (electronics wholesalers/e-waste companies/liquidators) for larger, cheaper lots.
5. At scale, diversify across multiple suppliers -- explicitly flagged as a lesson learned the hard way (see "bad batch" below).

**Category focus: used/vintage consumer electronics**
- Chosen because of extremely high sell-through rate on eBay (they cite ~100% for electronics -- nearly every listed unit sells within about a month) and consistent demand, despite higher defect/return rates than categories like Legos (they saw ~2% returns on Legos vs. much higher on electronics -- unpredictable "tests fine, breaks in shipping" failures).
- Deliberately avoided clothing (high return rate: wrong fit/doesn't match photos) despite it being one of the most popular reselling categories.
- **Specific product examples with real numbers**:
  - VCRs: buy for ~$1-5 (thrift stores/yard sales often bin them as "worthless cords"), resell for $100-200+ (up to ~100x markup) because the last VCR manufacturer went out of business -- pure supply/demand scarcity play. Same dynamic applies to camcorders and older digital cameras.
  - Digital cameras/DSLRs bought for ~$1-15 at thrift stores, resold for $25-200+ depending on brand (Kodak ~$25-30, Canon ~$100-200).
  - Docking stations (e.g., Dell) often thrown by thrift stores into "cord bins" priced ~$1, resell for ~$60 on eBay.
  - Conference/office equipment (e.g., Owl conferencing devices, Jabra brand) sells well.
  - Label printers (Dymo/Zebra brand thermal printers) resell for $80-200.
  - TI-84 graphing calculators: buy ~$15-50, sell ~$70.
  - Game Boys: sold 3 in one week at $100 each.
  - AirPods: sold individually (left/right separately) rather than as a pair, since buyers missing just one earbud will pay a premium for a single replacement.
  - One-off high flips mentioned: a $50 thrift-store "stereo thing" resold for $700; a device resold to a flea-market buyer for $300.
  - Medical devices and supplements sell fast but require caution/compliance awareness (not elaborated).
  - Vintage toys and pre-2010s electronics generally described as reliable sellers; actual VHS tapes explicitly called a "boomer bust" -- mostly low value except rare exceptions, despite hype around things like Disney "Black Diamond" VHS editions.

**Sourcing at scale (the core operational content)**
- Found an overseas (European, e.g. Denmark-area) e-waste/recycling supplier who was already selling "untested" electronics lots individually on eBay at very fast sell-through (a signal of underpricing). Cold-DMed him for a bulk off-platform deal; he initially ignored the message sent by one but not the other partner. Eventually wire-transferred ~$5,000 USD (paid in euros) for ~500 mixed untested units at ~$8-10/unit with no manifest and no real photos (used Google Lens on blurry supplier photos to identify models) -- shipping/payment took roughly 3-4 weeks to a month.
- First bulk lot results: roughly 60-80% of units were functional; found a flea-market vendor buyer in New York City willing to pay near full eBay retail price for cameras in bulk (avoiding eBay's ~12% fee), converting the $5,000 lot into an estimated $8,000-10,000 gross profit for about 15 combined hours of labor between the two of them (~$400-500/hour combined).
- Scaling attempt failed due to single-supplier dependency: the original supplier began favoring a competing Chinese buyer (who paid more), diverting ~1,500 units away from them; that Chinese buyer's company later collapsed despite reportedly doing $1.2M/month. Around September, they got a "bad batch" (~500 units, "literally none of them worked"), losing an estimated $4,000-5,000 -- traced to the supplier unknowingly buying from other resellers rather than true e-waste (resellers strip working units before reselling "untested" junk as if it were raw e-waste). Lesson stated explicitly: never depend on a single supplier.
- **Recovery/diversification strategy**: switched to domestic (US) sourcing to avoid ~10% import tariffs and multi-week overseas shipping (US lots typically arrive in 3-4 days). Made roughly 1,000 cold calls and sent ~12,000 emails (email lists generated using AI) to junk-removal companies, electronics liquidators, and existing eBay sellers found by searching completed/individual listings and reaching out directly -- yielded about 5-6 usable ongoing suppliers, a roughly 0.5-1% conversion rate on cold calls. Framed as an easier cold-call pitch than typical sales calls because it's "can I give you money" rather than "can I sell you something."
- Domestic suppliers often source from schools/businesses upgrading equipment (e.g., a school replacing iPads that still work, just being decommissioned) -- these lots have notably higher working-unit rates than overseas e-waste lots. Domestic unit costs run higher (~$15-40/unit vs. ~$8-10 overseas) but with better quality/lower defect rates; a "manifested" (documented) lot commands a price premium ($20-40/unit) over an unsorted/undocumented one (~$15-20/unit).
- One "pure margin, zero labor" anecdote: bought ~500 untested TI-84 calculators from a supplier for ~$5 each, shipped them unopened directly to a Tennessee buyer who resells to schools and pays $20/working unit or $10/broken unit sight-unseen based on trust -- no testing, no handling required by the hosts at all.

**Business economics at time of filming**
- Monthly revenue: roughly $10,000-20,000 at "peak" with current (post-diversification) suppliers; crossed six figures in a prior year of combined revenue while both were still in college/part-time.
- Profit margins: roughly 50% during the peak overseas-supply period, down to roughly 30% after shifting to costlier-but-more-reliable US suppliers.
- Both operate this part-time alongside personal/individual reselling and, for one of them, full-time college.

**Additional acquisition tactics mentioned**
- Post a "we buy your old electronics/cameras" ad on Facebook Marketplace to generate inbound leads (reports ~20 messages/day, though notes quality varies and volume is lower in a rural/small-population area).
- Buy on Facebook Marketplace, resell on eBay -- flagged as a large opportunity specifically for people in big metro markets (most Marketplace sellers don't cross-check eBay sold prices). Demoed live during the interview: searching "VCR," "camcorder," "digital camera," and "TI-84 calculator" on Facebook Marketplace within a given radius turned up many underpriced listings in real time (e.g., a $150-value digital camera listed for $15; a $150-value VCR/TV combo listed for $20; a free listing for an item that resells for $75).
- Door-to-door buying (host's own prior side hustle, "Door to More" in college): going through neighborhoods offering to buy old electronics on the spot via Venmo -- framed as an easy pitch ("I want to give you money," not "buy something from me"). Similarly, arranging a school or club fundraiser as an "e-waste drive" (host's own past example: a wrestling team fundraiser where donated old electronics were resold on eBay with proceeds going to the team) is offered as a template anyone (including for personal profit) could replicate.
- General advice to treat reselling as a relationship business: after buying from any individual or eBay seller, ask them to reach out whenever they get more of the same kind of item -- both hosts say this now generates near-daily inbound supply messages.
- Speculative idea floated (not implemented): using an AI agent to conduct the high-volume outreach/negotiation conversations (Marketplace messaging, haggling, arranging pickup/delivery) at scale, with humans only handling physical packing/shipping.

Caveats explicitly raised: electronics carry meaningfully higher return/defect rates than lower-tech categories like Legos or vintage toys; overseas sourcing is exposed to tariffs, long shipping times, and supplier reliability risk; single-supplier dependency nearly sank the business once already; competition is increasing as more of their own domestic suppliers realize they can sell directly on eBay themselves rather than wholesale to resellers, so specific supplier relationships have a shelf life.
