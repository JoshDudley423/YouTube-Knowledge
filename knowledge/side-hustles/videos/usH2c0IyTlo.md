# Why Dog Electrolytes Are the Next Million Dollar Idea
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=usH2c0IyTlo | Published: 2024-05-30

Podcast-style episode (Chris Koerner + Nik, "HoldCo Bros") covering a premium-event business breakdown, a pet-product idea, a home-lab-gadget idea, and a discussion of AI's threat to SEO/digital agencies.

## Business idea: "Salty Dog" — dog electrolyte supplement
- Concept: a powdered electrolyte shaker (salt + potassium + magnesium, similar composition to "Lite Salt" plus added magnesium) that dog owners shake onto dog food, marketed as replenishing electrolytes — essentially the pet-industry equivalent of a Gatorade/5-Hour Energy/Liquid Death style repackaging play.
- Explicitly framed as a "the product is basically salt in different packaging" idea — compared directly to 5-Hour Energy (repackaged energy drink -> billion-dollar company) and Liquid Death (repackaged water -> billion-dollar valuation) as proof that packaging/branding/marketing alone can create a large business around a commodity ingredient.
- Validation note: searched and found very few existing direct competitors (some dog electrolyte chews, one or two shaker-type products) — market described as open/underserved.
- Extension idea floated: "dog seasoning" — a flavor topper for kibble so dogs don't eat the same food every day (same emotional/empathy-driven framing as the electrolyte idea) — plus a joke tie-in pet-cremation-adjacent brand ("Burning Dogs," selling potassium/electrolyte products with cross-promotion) — treated more as comic riffing than a serious separate venture.
- Why the category is attractive: pet-industry tailwinds cited — people having fewer kids and more pets, increased "pet as family member" spending post-COVID, more disposable income directed at pets, and growth in adjacent pet services (vet clinics, pet insurance, pet cremation) as supporting evidence of a "riding a market tailwind" rather than "inventing a market" opportunity.
- Caveat raised (unresolved in the episode): potential secondary health effects of added sodium on dogs were flagged as an open question, addressed only jokingly, not substantively researched on-air.

## Business model breakdown: premium in-person conference/networking events
- Case study: "Capital Camp," a paid investor/entrepreneur networking event, cost ~$13,500/person for ~350 attendees over ~3.5-4 days (discount codes available; host declined to state his actual discounted price).
- Rough unit economics estimated live: ~$3.5M gross revenue (350 x $10,000 average); estimated costs ~$450,000 lodging (350 people x 3.5 nights x ~$350/night), ~$375,000 food (~$100/person/meal x 3 meals x 3.5 days), ~$250,000 venue rental, plus staffing quoted by an organizer at roughly $1,000/head/day covering planning, marketing, logistics coordination — total estimated cost ~$3M, implying roughly $500,000 net margin on the event (co-host was skeptical this number was accurately/fully disclosed).
- Framed explicitly as not primarily a profit center for the organizers but a strategic play: positions the organizing company as the top networking/deal-flow hub in its niche (equity/investing), building relationship capital and industry centrality that pays off elsewhere in the business.
- High-touch details cited as differentiators that justified premium pricing: staff greeting attendees by name (having reviewed their LinkedIn/bio in advance), proactively refreshing water bottles, free unlimited branded swag (leverages a psychological reluctance to take "too much" even when told to), staff proactively engaging any attendee sitting alone so no one feels excluded.
- General thesis stated: in-person premium events/conferences/bootcamps are becoming increasingly valuable and sought-after, driven by two tailwinds — (1) post-COVID remote work leaving companies with more travel/event budget and employees craving in-person connection, and (2) social-media fatigue making real in-person experience feel more valuable by contrast. Host notes he personally attended 4 conferences that year (a tree-trimming bootcamp, "SMB Bash," Capital Camp, and an upcoming ConvertKit conference) as evidence of rising demand for this event format — flagged as a viable business area to expand into (they had already run their own bootcamps).
- Skepticism/caveat raised by co-host: distrust of businesses that downplay their profitability ("we're just barely breaking even," citing Sam Parr's Hampton community as a similar example) — argues operators should "own their price" rather than claim it's not a money-maker, and that the true margin on high-ticket events is likely understated in public discussion.

## Business idea: consumer/home "bomb calorimeter" (DIY calorie-measurement device) + companion YouTube channel
- Concept: a simplified, home-usable version of a bomb calorimeter (the lab device that measures a food's actual caloric content by dehydrating, pulverizing, and combusting a sample to measure heat released) — positioned as a way for health-conscious consumers to verify calorie counts of meals they eat repeatedly, since nutrition labels are acknowledged to be inexact/loosely regulated.
- Precedent pattern cited for consumer adoption of former lab-only tools: infrared thermometers, pH meters (now used for home pool testing), spectrophotometers (adapted to food industry use), UV sterilization devices (a Shark Tank product sanitizing phones), and Geiger counters (now sold to consumers, e.g. near Chernobyl-adjacent markets) — general thesis that "lab tools migrate to consumer/prosumer markets over time" as trust in institutional accuracy declines and personal-data curiosity grows (tied to broader concern about food safety/microplastics).
- Explicitly acknowledged as a small, niche market (co-host says he wouldn't personally buy one despite liking the underlying trend), but the idea "solves a founder's own problem," which is framed as generally the best way to validate a product idea.
- Better business version proposed by co-host: build a **YouTube channel** around the process — taking common meals, comparing the packaging's stated calorie counts to an actual dehydrate-and-burn measurement on camera — as engaging, family-friendly science content, with the device/product sold to the audience it builds (content-to-commerce funnel) rather than the calorimeter being sold cold to strangers.

## Discussion: Google AI Overviews / SEO disruption (context for digital-agency-style side hustles)
- Google's then-new AI-generated answer summaries (using Gemini) are discussed as a major threat to the traditional SEO-driven ecosystem that many small/medium businesses and digital marketing agencies depend on for organic traffic — Google may now answer queries directly without sending users to source websites, and (at time of recording) without clear attribution/citation of the source content.
- Framed as more disruptive to "knowledge" search queries (e.g., "best pizza recipe" -> a blogger who monetizes via a cookbook/course) than to "local services" search queries (e.g., "roof repair near me"), which the hosts believe are less likely to be replaced by AI summaries because of their inherently local, service-booking nature — though they flag this distinction as their own inexpert, on-the-fly hypothesis, not a certainty.
- Predicted two possible outcomes: (1) the "Walmart option" — large/trusted incumbents (like Google itself) simply win and small sites/creators lose traffic permanently; or (2) public/legal backlash and litigation over uncompensated use of proprietary small-business content in AI answers, potentially forcing attribution/compensation changes years down the line.
- Noted as a real-time factor affecting a specific M&A opportunity the hosts were evaluating: a UI/UX digital agency for sale at $5.5M asking price against $1.8M net profit (a "3x multiple," seven-figure EBITDA, 6 years in business, 17 employees) — hosts were wary the business's core service (custom UI/UX design/development) could soon be significantly automated by AI (citing GPT-4's multimodal demo capabilities at the time), making the seller's motivation for exiting at this specific moment a key due-diligence question before buying.
- General implication for anyone running or considering an SEO/content-marketing-dependent side hustle: the mechanics of "being found" may be shifting from ranking in organic search results to being the specific source Google's AI chooses to summarize — a fundamentally different and less controllable game, prompting the hosts to actively deprioritize new investment in SEO-dependent projects pending more clarity.

## Caveats
- Financial figures for Capital Camp's margins are the hosts' own back-of-envelope estimates, not confirmed by the organizers; the actual profit/loss of the event was disputed between the two hosts during the discussion.
- The "Salty Dog" and "dog seasoning" ideas are proposed conceptually with no real-world testing, sales data, or execution shown in this episode — purely brainstormed and unvalidated beyond a cursory search showing limited existing competition.
- The AI/SEO disruption discussion is explicitly speculative ("this isn't very well thought out," "I don't know what's going to happen") and should be read as directional thinking from mid-2024, not settled fact.
