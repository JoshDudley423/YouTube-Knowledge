# This Business Model is About to Be EVERYWHERE! Don't Miss Out!
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zmHapb6pK1c | Published: 2024-10-31

Interview with Henry O'Brien, founder of SUSO Skate Co. (Toronto/Ontario, Canada) — a mobile/pop-up roller skating rink business launched during COVID. Covers the launch, unit economics, mall partnerships, mobile-van pivot, and licensing plans.

## The business / idea
- Pop-up outdoor roller-skate rental rink, later expanded to indoor mall locations and a fully mobile "roaming roller van" that brings the rink to schools, corporate events, and municipalities.
- Origin: launched May 2021 during COVID lockdown in Toronto after noticing (a) roller skating was surging virally on TikTok (billions of views on roller-skating hashtags) with no local outdoor rental option, and (b) most indoor activities were shut down, creating pent-up demand for an outdoor experiential activity.

## Startup and first-location economics
- First location: partnered with a city-owned outdoor venue (The Bentway, Toronto) that already ran a seasonal ice-skating rink — reused its existing infrastructure (smooth outdoor figure-eight surface, a 20-ft shipping container with built-in shelving, covered ~80-90% by an overhead expressway).
- Rent: flat $4,000/month (Canadian) — arrived at via rough napkin math based on projected capacity, not a market-rate negotiation (no prior precedent existed for this kind of deal).
- Total initial investment: ~$35,000 CAD (skate inventory, protective gear, insurance, website/booking plugin via Wix).
- First season (about 3-4 months, running May-September, Wednesday-Sunday only since founders had full-time jobs): grossed ~$120,000 at 30-40% margins; sold through their entire skate inventory by the last day of the season.
- Founders did not pay themselves for the first several years — only started taking a modest paycheck about 12 months before this interview (business is ~4 seasons/4 years old at time of taping); reinvested all surplus into inventory and expansion instead.
- Insurance cost: approximately $7,000 for that first year's popup (obtained through a personal connection/broker), with mandatory signed waivers, mandatory free wrist guards, and optional paid knee pads/elbow pads/helmet ($2 each or a $5 bundle).

## Pricing / unit economics
- Pricing: $18 for the first hour of skating, $25 for two hours, $30 for three hours (few customers stay past 1-2 hours); bringing your own skates was free at the public Bentway site but is now charged for at their private indoor locations.
- Average ticket: ~$26/person, but ~90% of visits are groups (dates, friend groups), so average group spend often exceeds $100.
- Good day benchmark: $2,000-$3,000 revenue; extended weekend hours can push $3,000-$4,000/day.
- Skate cost: ~$80 USD per pair, sourced from a US wholesaler (Ruby Skates) carrying a German brand (Chaya/Kaya) manufactured in Asia — deliberately chosen as premium/photogenic (titanium trucks, bright colorways) rather than cheap old-style skates, reasoning that visually appealing gear drives more user-generated social content and justifies the price point. Attempted private-label sampling from Chinese factories but none matched the quality of their current supplier, so they haven't pursued selling skates directly.
- Staffing: 2 people can run a slow day; busier days need closer to 5; the founders self-staffed the entire first summer themselves.

## Marketing (the core differentiator of this case study)
- Near-zero paid marketing for the first ~2.5 years (paid ads started only ~18 months before this interview, after 3+ prior seasons) — relied almost entirely on organic local press and user-generated content (UGC).
- PR tactic: proactively cold-emailed/DM'd local media outlets and editors months in advance of launch — emphasized that local journalists respond much better to a "hometown," family-friendly, time-limited story than a generic pitch; local media (compared to national outlets like CNBC) is far more receptive and easier to land, especially with a concise, localized pitch. A single published article produced a direct, immediate spike in bookings — some weekends sold out purely from press coverage.
- Insight on media relations: editors are hungry for genuinely interesting local stories and are more receptive than people assume — "there aren't a lot of people doing cool stuff" pitching them.
- Influencer strategy: reached out to micro/local influencers (e.g., ~15,000-50,000 followers, often "mom" or local foodie influencers) offering free skate sessions (for themselves plus guests) in exchange for organic content, rather than paid placements — kept costs near zero; on the rare occasion influencers requested payment, rates ran $150-$250 per post.
- Deliberately created engineered scarcity/"FOMO" by limiting operating hours/days (fewer, busier sessions rather than spreading thin) — reasoning: the appeal is partly the feeling of a "happening" event/party, and a half-empty rink undercuts that perception even if it seems inefficient on paper.
- Physical location as marketing: being highly visible in a dense downtown/pedestrian area (near apartments, close to downtown core) generated substantial organic foot traffic and drive-by awareness even without media coverage.

## The "mall unlock": selling into a cost center, not a profit center
- Second location idea originated from noticing a nearby mall (owned by Cadillac Fairview, a large North American mall operator with hundreds of properties) had already let an unrelated fitness class use its vacant rooftop parking garage — prompting Henry to cold-pitch Cadillac Fairview directly about using vacant retail/parking space company-wide.
- Explicit strategic insight (host's framing, confirmed by guest): don't sell into a company's profit center (where staff are focused on protecting margin), sell into their cost center — specifically, a mall's marketing department, which already has a discretionary budget for driving foot traffic (e.g., they might already spend $5,000-$10,000 on a Santa activation at Christmas or an Easter egg hunt) — pitching the business as something the mall pays for, not something that pays the mall, flips the negotiation.
- Deal terms vary a lot by which mall contact you reach: a "specialty leasing" contact treats the vacant space as a revenue opportunity for the mall (they charge you rent, sometimes with a revenue-share kicker if you cross a monthly sales threshold, e.g. anything over $30-35K/month); a "marketing" contact instead has budget to pay YOU to activate the space, since their job is to drive foot traffic that benefits the mall's core retail tenants (e.g. Apple, Anthropologie, Lululemon).
- Mall rooftop location rent: roughly $4,500-$5,000/month (comparable to their first outdoor rink deal).
- Rooftop location required investing ~$30,000 in modular plastic sports tiles (the same material used for basketball/pickleball courts, sourced from a Canadian distributor at roughly $4/sq ft) since the mall's rooftop parking deck surface wasn't smooth enough for skating; the mall had also pre-retrofitted two shipping containers (painted, structurally prepped) that SUSO simply craned onto the rooftop and outfitted with plywood shelving — a relatively low-cost buildout given the mall's existing infrastructure investment.
- Later decided to exit the rooftop location entirely due to unmanageable weather variables (wind especially, plus rain) and the operational overhead of coordinating food vendors; sold off roughly half of the sports tiles (~4,000-5,000 sq ft) to buyers building basketball, tennis, and pickleball courts, and pivoted focus to indoor mall locations and the mobile van format instead.

## Mobile "roaming roller van" model (their current growth focus)
- Pivoted to a fully mobile format after realizing malls' marketing budgets extended beyond fixed pop-ups to one-off branded events — the van brings the rink (skates, protective gear, a branded 10x10 tent) directly to schools, corporate events, and municipalities.
- Requires only a flat, safe surface roughly the size of a basketball/tennis court, cordoned off from vehicles — has even set up in an office building's sixth floor for a corporate holiday party.
- Pricing tiers vary by client type:
  - School events: intentionally discounted to ~$15-20/student to keep it accessible, with a minimum headcount requirement; sweet spot is ~25 skaters per rotating 45-60 minute session, aiming for 60+ students across a ~3-hour visit.
  - Corporate/municipal events: flat rate of $4,950 all-inclusive (staff, logistics, travel, skate rentals, protective gear, public liability insurance, waivers) for a 5-hour event with 5 rotating ~45-minute sessions serving 125-150 skaters total. Named client examples: Crest, Fujifilm, Neutrogena, Four Seasons, Pinterest.
- Margins on the mobile/van format: 60-70% (vs. 30-40% for the original fixed pop-up format) — driven by low incremental overhead since inventory was already owned, staffing is only paid when a job is booked, and clients typically pay in full up front before the event.
- Typical crew/equipment: 2 staff, one van holding 4 racks with up to 85 pairs of skates; setup takes 25-35 minutes, breakdown ~35 minutes.
- Appeared on Dragons' Den (Canadian Shark Tank) without securing a deal; investor feedback questioned the long-term durability of vacant retail space as a channel, which reportedly helped push the pivot toward the higher-margin, more scalable mobile/event model.

## Seasonality strategy
- Deliberately modeled the pop-up calendar after Spirit Halloween's approach: run indoor pop-ups roughly August through Halloween/Christmas/Valentine's/Easter/spring break, then exit that location by end of April/early May once people want to be outdoors again — creating urgency/FOMO ("come by while we're in town") rather than trying to operate the same format year-round.
- Rationale for exiting purely outdoor/rooftop locations: too many uncontrollable weather variables (wind, rain) undermine a full season's profitability; indoor locations offer more predictable foot traffic.

## Expansion plans: licensing over franchising
- Explicitly favors a licensing model over traditional franchising, citing that typical franchise fees (his example: window-cleaning franchises like "The Pinks" charging ~$50K, or $1-2M+ for something like Starbucks/McDonald's) are inaccessible to average people.
- Stated target: package their operational knowledge/blueprint so someone can start a similar mobile roller-skate business for roughly $50,000 all-in (assuming they lease rather than buy the van) — potential vehicle for this is a paid course/digital product teaching people in other cities/states how to replicate the model (van + branding + skate inventory + protective gear), rather than selling formal franchise territories.
- Reasoning: licensing lets you still capture value (e.g., requiring licensees to buy gear through you, or run bookings through your point-of-sale system for a cut) without the legal/financial complexity and cost of franchising, while making replication far more accessible.

## Tools/vendors named
- Wix (initial website + booking plugin)
- Square (in-person point-of-sale, run on iPads with cellular data since outdoor locations lacked WiFi)
- Peek Pro (third-party online booking platform — historically 90-95% of bookings came through online pre-booking rather than walk-ins)
- Ruby Skates (US wholesaler) / Chaya (German skate brand, manufactured in Asia) — their skate supplier
- Local sports-tile distributor (Canada) for the rooftop surface

## Caveats / risks
- Weather is a major operational risk for any outdoor/rooftop format — wind on a rooftop was specifically called out as underestimated; a rainy early season can meaningfully cut into total-season profitability.
- Refund policy: officially non-refundable, but in practice offers rebooking (with an advance weather-cancellation notice) or a refund for the ~3-10% of customers per 100 who can't reschedule (e.g., out-of-town visitors) — prioritizing customer goodwill over strict policy enforcement.
- A rink/permanent format faces high capital costs (a competitor's fixed permanent rink required, per Henry, an unfathomable amount of investment) and full-time exposure to seasonality; the mobile/pop-up model avoids this by only operating in favorable conditions/seasons.
- Managing on-site food vendors alongside the skate rink turned out to consume more staff time/attention than it was worth and was discontinued.
- The category (like other "novel experience" or fitness-fad businesses — spin, Barry's Bootcamp, ski) is subject to natural ebb and flow, though the founder argues staying power comes from the enduring appeal of getting people (of any age, 8 to 80+) off screens and active together, aided uniquely today by TikTok/Instagram's amplification of the visually appealing, nostalgic aesthetic of roller skating (a dynamic that didn't exist during roller skating's earlier popularity peaks in the 1950s-70s).
