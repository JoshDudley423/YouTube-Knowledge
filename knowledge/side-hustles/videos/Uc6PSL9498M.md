# Is Lawn Leveling the Next Big Home Service Idea?
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uc6PSL9498M | Published: 2024-07-22

Interview with Brandon, owner of Central Texas Lawn Leveling (CTX Lawn Leveling / Central Texas Lawn & Landscaping), Georgetown/North Austin, TX. Real, detailed case study of a home-service niche business with concrete unit economics.

## The niche: lawn leveling / lawn revitalization
- Lawn leveling: smoothing/filling low spots in a lawn (using a sand/compost topdressing mix, "scalping" the grass down first, then dragging material to fill low areas) to get a golf-course-like, flat, manicured look — distinct from ordinary mowing.
- Origin: guest worked on an oil rig; during COVID, with more time at home, started manually leveling his own lawn (following YouTube tutorials), was dissatisfied with a local company's $900 job (no education, no guidance on scalping the grass first, used liquid aeration which he considers ineffective for compaction), and improved on it himself. Neighbors initially thought he was "killing his yard" by dumping material on it, then became customers once they saw the visual results — the same "polarizing procedure look weird → drives curiosity → drives customers" dynamic the host compares to pressure-washing businesses.
- Validation method: posted before/after photos to **Nextdoor** with a poll asking neighbors how much they'd pay per month for a lawn like that, offering **4 price tiers ($125, $150, $175, $225)** based on a rough per-service breakdown (e.g., $225 ÷ 5 planned services = $45/visit, benchmarked against a plain mow-and-blow competitor). Got **~150 responses**; most clustered around the **$175** tier, with $225 seen as too expensive — but confirmed there was a real market.

## Business evolution phases
1. **Phase 0 (self-taught)**: leveled own yard; used a soil sample to guide fertility decisions since every yard's history/material composition differs (guest notes many home builders skip putting proper base material under sod — a recurring root cause of poor lawns in the area).
2. **Phase 1 (Nextdoor validation + neighbors)**: ran the Nextdoor poll, took on a couple of neighbors' yards at low/no cost to build case studies (before/after photos), operating originally under the name "Real Cut Landscaping" (named after using a reel mower for a golf-course-style cut).
3. **Phase 2 (dedicated leveling company)**: a neighbor who saw the Nextdoor post wanted to build a "real" leveling company; they collaborated informally (guest assisted, no formal partnership was offered), then split and each built their own separate leveling business/brand.
4. **Phase 3 (scaling / equipment acquisition)**: bought specialized equipment cross-country as needed (drove to Nashville for trailers, Kansas City for a Dingo mini skid steer), built a website, ran ads, and went all-in acquiring both equipment and customers. Later, after being laid off from his day job in January, went full-time into the business.
- Later pivot: briefly ran a subscription **lawn care** (mowing) division as a source of recurring revenue, but had to shut it down (had to hand off 45 lawn-care clients to another vetted company) because operational growth in the core leveling business "broke everything" and he couldn't maintain service quality across both. In hindsight, he explicitly regrets dropping lawn care, since it would have provided steadier, less-seasonal recurring revenue; he's now seeking to partner with or acquire an existing lawn care company to rebuild that recurring layer.
- Current strategic direction: becoming a one-stop-shop covering leveling + landscaping + fertility (and eventually mowing again) via subcontractor partnerships, since "nobody really does all of it" in this space, and using leveling as a lower-margin lead-gen wedge into much larger landscaping projects (framed by the host as "leveling might end up being 20% of your business" while feeding higher-value landscaping jobs).

## Numbers / unit economics (detailed)
- **Average job ticket**: ~$2,200-2,300, with the bulk of jobs falling in the **$1,800-$2,600 range**, for homes in the **3,000-5,000 sq ft lawn** range, using roughly **7-15 cubic yards of material** per job.
- **Cost breakdown on an example $1,800 job (~4,000 sq ft, 6-8 yards of material)**: material costs ~$60/yard; delivery costs $125-$300; scalping (mowing the grass down to ~1/2 inch, bagging and hauling clippings) is treated as a **loss-leader add-on** (an extra 1.5-2 hours of labor that can't really be charged at a premium — customers are encouraged to scalp their own yard before the crew arrives to reduce this cost). Total job cost (labor + material + insurance/overhead allocation) on an $1,800 job runs **~$1,200-1,300**, i.e., roughly **$400-800 gross profit per job**, before overhead.
- Counterintuitive margin note: **smaller jobs are more profitable than bigger ones** — larger open yards are actually easier/faster to service with drag equipment, while smaller yards with flower beds, tight gates, and slopes require more manual labor (wheelbarrows, hand-leveling, can't fit drag equipment) even though they use less material and the price can't scale proportionally with yard size.
- **Insurance**: ~$8,000/year for general liability, priced based on the company's revenue (i.e., cost increases as the business grows, even with zero claims filed), required because the company is treated as a commercial operation.
- **Lead volume**: over **600 leads** through their website in a single year, concentrated **February-May** (peak ~10-11 leads/day in-season); tracked via a 4-tier Google Sheets pipeline (Responses → Appointments Booked → Quoted → Completed) to compute close rates. Achieved a **90-95% lead response/callback rate**; ~5% went entirely unaddressed due to volume.
- **Seasonality**: strongly seasonal — main season runs roughly **mid-March (after spring break) through early fall**, with the business "shutting down" toward end of September; explicitly stated that lawn leveling alone "cannot sustain a business year-round" and requires diversification (landscaping, or ideally a recurring lawn-care line) to cover the off-season.
- **Repeat/recurring business**: landscaping is the primary source of repeat revenue — successful leveling jobs (which show visible transformation and build trust) frequently convert into landscaping project requests. Grass recovery from leveling/treatment typically takes 2-6 weeks depending on grass health, sun, and rain.
- **Rebate programs**: local water authorities (e.g., Lower Colorado River Authority) and cities offer **rebates up to $600 (50% reimbursement)** for topdressing and core aeration services (not leveling specifically, since it's not yet officially categorized), because organic-matter-based lawn treatments improve water retention and reduce irrigation demand — company helps homeowners fill out and submit rebate paperwork (invoice, photos, water bill) as a value-add and word-of-mouth driver (host suggested further automating this paperwork with tools like Google Forms/automation, which the owner said he'd consider as a scaling improvement).

## Product/positioning differentiation
- Developed a proprietary topdressing material blend: **75% loam/25% coffee-grounds-and-manure mix** as an alternative to plain sand, chosen because coffee grounds provide nitrogen and have water/nutrient retention comparable to sphagnum peat moss (cited a Texas A&M study), while being cleaner (no rocks/mulch/sticks) than typical commercial biosolid-based compost, which can damage the expensive precision mowers (~$3,000 units) used for fine, close-cut lawns.
- Root cause identified repeatedly in the local market: new-build homes (~85-90% of the company's customer base are homes built within the pandemic-era building boom) frequently have inadequate base material (sometimes literally none, or bare rock) under sod — "home builder grade" lawns, jokingly called "ankle breakers" — due to builders cutting corners during the high-demand, low-interest-rate building surge. This creates a large, ongoing addressable market of homeowners with underlying yard/irrigation problems the company's leveling and core-aeration process incidentally exposes (e.g., improperly buried sprinkler lines, sprinkler heads covered by hardscaping, rocks/debris under the topsoil) — turning "we scalped/aerated your yard and discovered these other problems" into further landscaping/repair upsell opportunities.
- Uses a relationship with a professional agronomist (a personal friend with a Master's in Agronomy, pursuing a PhD) who interprets soil-test results for a per-test fee; provides customers detailed, science-based, personalized care instructions (watering, fertilizing schedule, when to start mowing) as a differentiator — customer reviews specifically cite the depth of information/education provided, something the owner says no competing lawn-care or leveling company in the area offers. Planning a "Level Up Lawn" podcast and a private Facebook community to build on this educational positioning.

## Marketing / customer acquisition
- Primary lead source: **organic inbound (SEO/Google)** — being one of only two dedicated "lawn leveling" companies in the area for a long time meant strong organic search visibility; paid ads used to a lesser degree. Local Facebook lawn/grass hobbyist groups (mentioned: "Bermuda Lawn Dominators," ~880,000 members) are a large, engaged community used informally for market research and word-of-mouth, though not a major direct lead-gen channel for this business specifically.
- TikTok/Instagram: used mainly for process/education content (loading material, scalping, delivery), not a meaningful direct lead source for this business; Facebook remains the dominant social channel because that's "where the lawn groups are."
- Willing to travel to underserved metro areas (e.g., San Antonio, New Braunfels, South Austin, even Houston) if they can bundle at least 2 jobs to justify the ~5+ hours round-trip drive/labor/fuel cost of a distant job versus a normal 30-45 minute local job.
- Market-saturation risk noted by the host (via Google Trends) but pushed back on by the owner: even in an increasingly "saturated" niche (more small competitors, including several who falsely market themselves as leveling specialists without real expertise), demand outpaces supply locally — the business had its highest-volume year yet and still couldn't service every lead. Category confusion is common: customers often conflate "lawn leveling" with "lawn grading" (drainage correction), generating adjacent landscaping-repair leads the company has learned to capture rather than turn away.

## Operational caveats and lessons
- Growth itself became the primary operational bottleneck: managing a large lead influx (calendar packed with 8-9 leads/day at peak, 35+ paid-deposit jobs booked by March) strained lead follow-up, hiring/training new crew, and logistics (weather dependency — can't work wet/muddy yards, must plan material delivery around forecasted rain).
- Explicit customer-expectation management practice: always quote lead times as "2-3 weeks plus rain delays" rather than promising fast turnaround, to avoid rushing jobs on unready (too-wet) ground, which risks creating new problems (machinery ruts).
- Owner's stated single biggest retrospective regret: dropping the recurring lawn-care/mowing line rather than building it in parallel from the start — believes it would have made the overall business meaningfully more sustainable/less seasonal (with subscription-style, broken-up pricing) despite lawn care itself also being partly seasonal.
- Owner is deliberately avoiding further capital investment in leveling equipment/capacity itself, viewing lawn leveling as an inherently niche, seasonal, competitively crowding category (cites another local company that entered and then sold all its equipment within a year) — instead redirecting growth capital into a **"material business"** (selling his proprietary compost/leveling material directly to DIY homeowners, having bought a dump truck) and into expanding the landscaping/general-contractor side of the business, rather than scaling the leveling service itself further.
- No health insurance for himself at the time of recording (cited as a real personal cost/risk of running a small trades business), despite the company holding required commercial liability insurance.
