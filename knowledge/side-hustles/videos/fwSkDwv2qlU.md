# How to Make $1M+ With a Drone ($1.5K in 30 Minutes)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fwSkDwv2qlU | Published: 2025-10-12

"HoldCo Bros" episode — Chris Koerner and Nik (Nikonomics) research and pitch the drone roof inspection business as a low-cost, low-complexity side hustle.

## The business idea
**Drone roof inspection / "roof condition documentation"**: using a drone to photograph and document roof condition for insurance companies, roofing companies, property managers, home inspectors, construction, and solar companies, instead of a human physically climbing the roof.
- Important framing/legal tip: **do not call it "inspection"** in your business name or marketing — in certain states, using that word can trigger additional licensing requirements. Industry operators instead call it "roof condition documentation."

## Market size / trend data
- Total inspection & monitoring market: ~$23 billion; drone rooftop inspection specifically is currently only ~$350 million of that, but projected to grow ~14.5-15% CAGR to ~$3.5 billion by 2035 (roughly a 10x increase over 10 years) — for comparison, normal GDP/inflation-adjusted growth is ~3%, so this niche is growing ~5x faster than the broader economy.
- Roof/storm insurance claims reached over $30 billion in 2024; a major tornado outbreak in December 2024 caused Google search interest in "roof inspection" to spike from a relative volume of 23/100 to 100/100 (all-time high, per 20-year Google Trends data).
- Structural tailwind on the insurance/inspection labor side: the insurance industry projects ~400,000 unfilled adjuster/inspector/estimator positions by 2038; roughly 50% of the current insurance workforce is expected to retire within 15 years, and less than 25% of the current workforce is under age 35 — a widening supply gap the hosts argue drone technology is positioned to fill.
- Market segmentation cited: ~40% of demand from insurance companies, ~20% from roofing companies (residential + commercial), remainder from property managers/commercial real estate, home inspection companies, construction, and solar panel installers.

## Concrete numbers
- Gross margins: 50-70% per job; net profit margins: 20-30% typical, up to 40-50% for highly efficient operators.
- Pricing by job type: standard residential visual inspection $200-400; add thermal imaging +$1,000-3,300(sic, reported inconsistently in source — treat range as approximate, likely $100-300 extra); commercial jobs $300-700; complex/industrial/high-rise $500-1,500; per-square-foot pricing 15-50 cents/sq ft; 3D mapping or AI-powered damage analysis add-ons $400-700.
- Startup cost: a capable drone (e.g., DJI Mavic 3, enterprise/thermal versions) runs $1,200-3,500; all-in startup cost including drone, insurance, and analysis software is typically under $10,000 ("a few grand on a credit card").
- Licensing: requires an FAA Part 107 remote pilot license — roughly 20-40 hours of study/prep, a proctored exam, cost cited at $175 for one operator's course. Flights near airports/controlled airspace require an additional permit application that can take up to 30 days for approval.
- Case study (Tom Powers): a former roofing inspector who fell off a roof, bought a DJI Inspire 1 Pro, got FAA Part 107 licensed, and built the business to **seven figures/year in about 4 years**. Operationally: 50-60 photos per inspection, ~15 minutes per inspection, can complete 2 inspections per drone battery, replaces roughly half of his customers' manual roof inspections. Runs a small team (a few employees, 2-3 crews). His single biggest profit driver is **storm response**: $15,000-30,000 in inspection revenue in a single day after a major storm, since one storm damages many homes in the same area at once (high volume + high efficiency from routing).
- Realistic daily capacity for a solo operator: roughly 2 roofs/day once you account for permit checks, weather/Google Maps pre-planning, on-site shooting (~2 hours per job including setup/equipment checks), and post-processing/upload/analysis — not the 10+/day some might assume.
- Customer acquisition cost benchmarks cited from other operators' Google Ads data: cost-per-click ~$70-80 for broad "roof inspection" terms; more specific/long-tail keywords (e.g., including a city name) bring cost-per-lead down to ~$30-40, with roughly a 50% close rate on those leads. A less-competitive/cheaper alternative keyword mentioned: "drone facade inspection" bids lower than "drone roof inspection" while capturing similar buyer intent.
- Facebook ad ROI reported by operators interviewed in research: roughly 3:1 to 5:1 return (every $1 spent generates $3-5 back) — noted as effective for B2B despite common assumptions that Facebook ads don't work for B2B.
- Historical pricing collapse: 8-10 years ago drone operators could charge $2,000-3,000/day; pricing has since compressed/stabilized as licensing broadened the operator pool and hardware costs fell (thermal-camera-equipped drones dropped from ~$20,000 to ~$3,000-5,000), even though licensing itself added a barrier to entry.

## Process / steps described
- **Per-job workflow**: check proximity to controlled airspace and file a permit if needed → check weather → pre-scout the roof layout via Google Maps → arrive early, do an equipment check → fly and shoot (~2 hours on site) → export/upload footage → run it through roof-analysis software → deliver report/images to client (may include light photo editing).
- **Moat/barrier to entry**: FAA Part 107 licensing plus the operational know-how (assessing roof damage, weather/airspace planning) creates enough friction to meaningfully limit casual competition, without being so burdensome that it blocks a motivated side-hustler (roughly 40 hours of study total).
- **"Land and expand" upsell strategy** once you have a client relationship:
  - Offer to shoot **social media content** (before/after roof footage) as an add-on for roofing companies — nearly free to produce since you're already up there, and most roofing companies have no in-house video capability.
  - Offer **inventory/materials management documentation** for commercial construction sites (photographing on-site building materials on a recurring — weekly/monthly/quarterly — basis) to protect against delivery disputes; positioned as a natural upsell if you're already contracted to inspect a building under construction.
  - Longer term, add **drone photography services** generally — explicitly recommended to lead with the harder-to-enter inspection business first, then expand into photography once trust is established, rather than starting with photography (which the hosts and cited industry sources describe as low-barrier/oversaturated — "easier for a photographer to learn drone craft than for a drone operator to learn photography").
  - Residential upsell modeled on a legacy example ("Uncle Clark," a family member who in the 1980s/90s photographed farmland from model airplanes and sold prints door-to-door at ~$250 each with a 60-70% take rate): after doing a roofing company's inspection, print a nice aerial photo of the homeowner's property and offer to sell it to them directly (flagged by the hosts as potentially legally/socially awkward without consent, on reflection).
- **Customer acquisition — B2B relationship/piggyback approach (primary recommended channel)**: rather than only cold-marketing, list out personal contacts who work in adjacent industries (real estate agents, general/commercial contractors, roofing companies, insurance adjusters, property/facilities management companies, solar installers, restoration/remediation companies, architecture/engineering firms, home inspectors, schools/universities, healthcare systems, retail chains, hospitality venues) and approach them directly — a single referral relationship (e.g., one property management company) can generate recurring, multi-job revenue rather than one-off sales.
- **Storm-chasing tactic**: maintain existing relationships with roofing companies so that when a storm hits (tracked via weather reports), you're already positioned to receive a surge of inspection referrals; can also work "bottoms-up" by flying proactively after a storm to identify damaged roofs yourself and selling those leads to roofing companies.
- **Paid + organic acquisition stack**: Google Ads (bid on lower-cost adjacent keywords like "drone facade inspection" rather than the more expensive "drone roof inspection"), Google Business Profile optimization (found to meaningfully move organic lead volume), programmatic SEO (city-specific landing pages), and Facebook ads (despite the common assumption these don't work for B2B).
- **Review-generation tactic** (cross-referenced from a stump-grinding operator, Tyler, mentioned in the episode): after every job, directly and immediately ask the customer to leave a Google review on the spot ("would you pull your phone out right now and do it?") — described as the single highest-leverage, lowest-cost driver of local search ranking.
- **Low-risk entry point**: before taking on your own clients, some companies pay independent operators to fulfill outsourced drone jobs, letting a beginner practice/gain paid experience before running solo jobs.

## Caveats / risks
- Metal roofs cause drone signal interference — a real operational learning curve cited by the case-study operator.
- Onlooker/confrontation risk: neighbors or bystanders sometimes challenge operators about drone use; recommended mitigation is to have a tripod-mounted phone recording the operator's own conduct at all times as documentation/liability protection, since a false report can result in losing your FAA license.
- Not a fully passive or infinitely scalable solo business — administrative overhead (permits, weather/site prep, post-processing) caps a single operator at roughly 2 jobs/day; scaling further requires hiring crews.
- The market is B2B, not B2C — the hosts explicitly note customers are businesses (roofing companies, property managers, etc.) searching for this service themselves, not homeowners, which shapes which marketing channels (Google Ads/organic, LinkedIn-style relationship building) actually work.
- Pricing per job is not high in isolation ($200-1,500) — profitability depends on operational efficiency (many jobs per day/storm event) and high margins rather than high per-job revenue.
