# How to Make Money With Tariffs While Everyone Panics
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LXB5G1zqnGg | Published: 2025-04-10

Context: recorded right after "Liberation Day" (April 2, 2025) tariff announcement — US average tariff rate reportedly jumped from 2.6% to 22%, S&P 500 lost ~$5 trillion in value, JP Morgan cited a 60% recession probability for the year. Framed as a list of opportunistic business ideas rather than macro commentary.

Ideas / opportunities discussed:

1. **Sourcing/manufacturing arbitrage research**
   - Use **Import Yeti** to reverse-engineer which factories/countries any company (large or small) sources products from — free/paid tool, not sponsored.
   - Use **Jungle Scout** to find Amazon products with high sales volume and rising trend (more demand than supply) in a chosen niche (e.g., baby goods, kitchenware).
   - Cross-reference: find hot products via Jungle Scout, then find alternate manufacturers (in non-tariffed countries) via Import Yeti.
   - Monetization paths: sell sourcing reports as a PDF to US companies already selling those products; order samples from current supplier vs. alternative and compare quality; become a **sourcing agent/broker** for US companies currently importing from tariff-hit countries (China) and take a **percentage of import value as a broker/finder's fee**.
   - Start a "reverse-engineering" newsletter (example name: "Tariff Tuesday") — every time a big brand changes manufacturer/supplier (tracked via Import Yeti), email it out. Claim: because subscribers skew toward executives/sourcing agents, ad space on such a niche B2B newsletter can be sold at **5-10x normal newsletter ad rates** even with only 100-10,000 subscribers.
   - Note: Import Yeti data has a natural 1-3 month lag (shipping/customs lead time) before new sourcing shows up.
   - Cold outreach idea: scrape all Shopify Plus store owner/exec contacts via **builtwith.com** (or pay a virtual assistant on Upwork $20-50 to do it), then email them "I'll find you better factories within 24 hours for 3 products, and only charge if you actually use who I find."
   - Specific cold-email hack: append `/collections/all?sort_by=best-selling` (or similar `sort_by=best-selling` suffix) to any Shopify store's root URL to auto-list their products best-to-worst selling — use this to name a prospect's actual top 3 sellers in a cold outreach email to prove you did your homework.

2. **Countries poised to gain manufacturing**: Vietnam and Cambodia were named as smaller Asian countries getting hit by tariffs but lacking negotiating leverage (unlike China), so manufacturing is expected to keep shifting there — implies opportunity in sourcing/importing from these specific countries.

3. **Offshore staffing/recruiting agency**: with a potentially weaker US dollar, hiring remote workers abroad stretches further.
   - Source virtual assistants via Upwork, then place them with companies for a fee.
   - Model A — **staffing agency**: mark up a worker's $7-8/hr rate by 50-100%, manage them directly, take ~50% margin off the top; more recurring revenue but more operational headache (hiring/firing).
   - Model B — **placement agency**: charge a flat fee ($500-$5,000) or 10-30% of first-year salary as a one-time placement fee; better cash flow per deal but not recurring — "only as good as your last deal."

4. **AI-driven software/digital products**: claims tariffs don't touch digital goods (SaaS, communities, info products). Recommends using AI app builders (Replit, Cursor, Bolt, Lovable, Windsurf, Gumloop) to build products fast.
   - Specific niche called out: a **"last-mile agency"** that finishes half-built Replit projects — observation that many non-technical users start apps in Replit (including via Replit's bounty program) but can't finish them (often only 50-80% done), creating demand for finishers/fixers.

5. **Tariff/supply-chain consultant**: become a fast expert using AI research tools (ChatGPT deep research, Google) and start publishing content (social posts, video). Claim: a few hours of focused reading puts you "in the top 5% of experts" given how confused/underprepared most business owners currently are; framed as high-demand/low-supply for this advisory service right now.

6. **"Buy the dip" on small businesses**: distressed sellers (blue-collar businesses, home services, laundromats, RV parks, pet crematoriums) may be panicking over tariff ripple effects even when their business isn't actually exposed. Opportunity to acquire cheaply if you're more bullish on the economy than the seller and structure deals with **seller financing** and downside protection (e.g., clawback/reversion terms if buyer defaults).

7. **Made-in-America e-commerce**: explicit recommendation to avoid starting a typical import-dependent e-commerce business right now; instead launch e-commerce selling domestically-manufactured, affordable, high-quality goods and lean hard into "Made in America" branding/marketing even when the domestic-made angle is minor, because tariffs may finally make Americans price-tolerant enough to prefer domestic goods.

Caveats/risk notes:
- Explicitly says he's not qualified to comment on macroeconomics/politics — these are pure opportunity plays, not policy predictions.
- Notes his own historical research (searching "recession" in 100 years of newspaper archives via newspapers.com) found only ~30% of recession-prediction headlines proved accurate — used as a caution against overreacting to doom predictions.
- Offshore staffing/placement business acknowledged as "very crowded" already.
- Cold outreach conversion expectation set low: "95%+ of people will never respond no matter how good your email is" — you only start working once a prospect responds.
- Historical framing used to argue recessions/tariff shocks are good times to build (GM founded 1908 after 1907 bank panic; Starbucks expanded during 1980s recession; McDonald's grew post-WWII inflation; Microsoft/Apple founded during 1970s stagflation/oil crisis; J&J thrived in Great Recession on non-discretionary goods; LinkedIn started post dot-com crash 2002; Airbnb founded 2008).
