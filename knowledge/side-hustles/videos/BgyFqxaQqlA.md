# How to Make $100k/Month From a Company Going Out Of Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BgyFqxaQqlA | Published: 2024-11-02

## Vending machine business ("Side Vending") update
- Started as a viral perfume vending machine business, expanded to a portfolio: perfume vending, motorcycle-helmet-cleaning vending machines, and cotton candy vending machines.
- Perfume vending: sold ~$100k worth over one summer after going viral on social media; then lost both salespeople in one month (sales went from weekly to zero) and had to hire 3 new salespeople.
- Cotton candy vending machines: margin is reportedly "over 100%" (cost basis extremely low relative to price); marketed to kids, highly visual/postable product.
- Helmet-cleaning vending machine: a single viral video generated 2,000+ leads.
- Cumulative sales across the vending portfolio: ~$200k by ~5 months in (started August, this recorded early November).
- Caveat raised: the whole model leans on generating viral social content to drive machine sales — both hosts agreed this is the weakest of the three ideas discussed in the episode ("kill" it) specifically because "it doesn't work unless you have some way ... to make something go viral."
- General lesson stated: businesses "always take longer than you think and cost more than you budget," and revenue projections are almost never hit exactly (either wildly beat or badly undershoot).

## Buying/monetizing a failed company's email list (The Pro's Closet case study)
- The Pro's Closet: 12-13 year old online used-bike marketplace (like "CarMax for bikes," Shopify-based), based in Denver. Raised ~$55M in VC funding.
- What went wrong: overpaid for trade-in bike inventory during the 2021-2022 bike price boom, then demand collapsed and they couldn't unload the glut of inventory; shut down in 2024.
- Scale: claimed 50,000+ bikes sold at ~$3,000 average price = ~$150M+ in bike sales alone over 12 years (plus accessories/apparel).
- The idea: an email list from a shut-down company is a wasted asset. Estimated the company likely had 300,000-400,000 email subscribers (using a rule of thumb of 3-5 email subscribers per 1 paying customer from e-commerce experience).
- Newsletter monetization math cited: roughly $0.50 per subscriber per month in revenue for a niche daily-ish newsletter (rougher $2 figure was floated but corrected down to ~$0.50, varies by niche — e.g., finance niches monetize higher).
- On a 400,000-subscriber list that would be roughly $200k/month, or a $2-3M/year newsletter business at an estimated 75% margin — smaller than the original bike business but far more profitable and easier to run.
- How to find this opportunity: reach out directly (e.g., cold LinkedIn message to the CEO) and ask to buy the email list/data when a company folds. In this case the list had already been sold to someone else by the time of outreach — so speed matters.
- Broader framing: "sell the one to sell the many" — of the ~3 million Shopify stores, an estimated ~30,000 are 8-figure+ businesses, any of which could support a companion paid newsletter using the same customer base.
- Method for finding failing/closing businesses to approach: monitor for public shutdown signals, network via CPAs who service these businesses, or post on social media that you're looking for such situations — struggling owners will often reach out in DMs if they think you can help.

## "Negotiations as a Service" / Pharmacy Benefit Manager (PBM) arbitrage idea
- Concept: help companies cut their pharmacy benefits spend by using pricing transparency from Mark Cuban's Cost Plus Drugs as leverage, then take a cut of the savings ("I only eat when you eat").
- Background: PBMs act as middlemen negotiating bulk drug pricing between employers/insurers and manufacturers; industry described as "massive" (mentioned informally as ~$90B+ scale in conversation, unconfirmed).
- Companies spend on average ~$1,000/year per employee on pharmacy benefits.
- The "formulary" is the document specifying negotiated price per drug; PBM-manufacturer pricing is typically covered by NDAs, creating information asymmetry (companies/pharmacies don't know what others pay).
- Cost Plus Drugs' model: publishes its formulary publicly and marks drugs up a flat 15%, versus an estimated industry-average markup of ~30%.
- Concrete savings example given: on $1,000/employee/year pharmacy spend, if wholesale/pharmacy cost is ~$770, the typical 30% markup employer pays ~$230 in margin; a Cost Plus-style 15% markup would mean paying only ~$115 in margin — an estimated ~$115/employee/year savings.
- At scale: a company with 1,000+ employees could save roughly $100k/year this way.
- Business model proposed: approach mid-size-to-large employers (must have enough employees to be required to offer health benefits — cited threshold is roughly 20-50 employees depending on ACA rules) and offer to renegotiate their pharmacy formulary in exchange for a percentage of the savings achieved (no fee if no savings found).
- Use Cost Plus Drugs' published pricing as a public benchmark/"stalking horse" to pressure the PBM or manufacturer into better terms — you don't need to reveal your full method, just point to public comparable pricing.
- How to get first customer: start with a local/known small business, ask how they currently handle their pharmacy benefits negotiation (they usually don't know), get familiar with the mechanics, then use LinkedIn to find HR/finance decision-makers (CFO, controller) at larger local companies and pitch around their annual benefits renegotiation window.
- Caveat: pharmacy spend is typically a small line item for large companies relative to total health costs (premiums, surgeries, primary care dominate), so it may not be a top priority even though it's an easy, low-risk win — sell it as effectively free money with no downside.
- Referenced analogous model: a company called "71 Pounds" audits FedEx shipping labels, automatically files late-delivery disputes, and keeps 30-40% of whatever it recovers for the client — a low-effort, contingency-based savings-recovery business model.
