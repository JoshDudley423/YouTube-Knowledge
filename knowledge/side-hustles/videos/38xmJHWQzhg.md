# Stop Rambling: 7 Tricks to Make People Respect You
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=38xmJHWQzhg | Published: 2026-07-22

Solo episode on communication/sales/pitching tactics rather than a specific business idea, but includes concrete real-world business data points from Koerner's own ventures and prior guests. Framing: "the businesses and founders that win are not always smarter — they are simply better at communication."

## Core content: 7 communication tactics for founders/salespeople
1. **The hook / first sentence is everything**: generic cold-outreach openers ("Hi, my name is... would love to hop on a call") get deleted in seconds, even by senior executives (cited: Best Buy's CMO). Effective openers reference something specific and personal to the recipient (a recent tweet, a podcast episode, a LinkedIn detail) — proves the sender actually paid attention. Example: a cold email opened with "you said on a recent episode your favorite business is one a high schooler could run — well I built one" got read in full and a reply within 30 minutes, leading to a podcast booking. Real example: cold-called Best Buy's CMO to pitch an iPhone-repair-in-store partnership; a mentor found via LinkedIn that she'd played basketball at Gonzaga, and opening with that fact (not the pitch) was credited as the reason the call worked.
2. **Admit a flaw fast ("Pratfall Effect")**: admitting a weakness/flaw increases trust rather than reducing it (cited as a social-psychology finding). Example: as a brand-new iPhone repair shop owner with zero repair experience, telling customers "yeah, we'll figure it out" (then learning the actual repair on YouTube in real time) built more loyal repeat customers than faking confidence would have. Example: a guest (Mike Yoder, drone deer-recovery/agricultural-spraying business) mentioning offhand that he grew up Amish generated the most-engaged clip from that entire episode.
3. **Sell with stories, not just data**: the podcast episodes that perform best are never the ones centered on a P&L read-out — they're built around one vivid, specific moment (example: a peach-stand business owner, Shannon, recounting pulling up to a gas station at 5am with her 11-year-old son to learn the business from her mentor). Framing: "investors, customers, vendors — they're buying the founder; the numbers are just the receipts."
4. **Plain language beats jargon ("the dumber it sounds, the smarter you look")**: cites University of Munich research finding that speakers using simple, clear language are rated as more competent than those using jargon (TAM, ARR, blended CAC, "vertical SaaS") — jargon triggers listener distrust ("I think this guy's hiding something"). Test: if you can't explain your business in one short, plain sentence, you don't understand it well enough yet. Examples of ideal one-line business descriptions: "We fix broken iPhones" (Phone Restore); "They put windows in shipping containers, put it on a semi-truck, and drive people through wine country" (Toasted Tours); "I sell peaches on the side of the road" (peach stand). Uses the sinking of the Swedish warship Vasa (1628 — capsized on its maiden voyage due to being top-heavy/under-ballasted despite an impressive, ornate exterior) as a metaphor for pitches that are "pretty above the waterline, hollow below."
5. **Specifics beat adjectives**: replace vague claims ("really strong growth," "scale rapidly," "the right investment") with concrete numbers, dates, dollar amounts, and proper nouns. Cited real examples used as models of this style: "We grew from 3 to 247 customers between December and January"; "we add 30 new customers a month with no paid marketing"; "I made 50 grand in my first 10 weeks" (Mike Yoder, drone/ag-spray business); "I wholesaled one self-storage deal for $360,000 of profit" (Connor Gross); "I opened 100 peach stands my first summer" (Shannon); a business Koerner personally bought for $750,000 (~5 years prior to this recording) that made "$10 million in 90 days" with no outside funding or paid marketing (unspecified which business).
6. **Vary sentence length / use silence, avoid rambling**: nervous speakers default to filling silence and over-hedging; top communicators speak in short, punchy bursts and pause deliberately (cited: Steve Jobs product-launch delivery style). In negotiation, the person doing the most talking is usually losing. Real example: while dealing with a customer client representing ~90% of his company's revenue who was making threats, Koerner sent a short, unhedged email (from a hospital, during his daughter's care) ending with "Your continued involvement in this working relationship is a greater liability to our business than the current threats you are making" — this ended the leverage dynamic; the client's own team eventually intervened and the relationship was terminated. Tactic: put your strongest line at the very end (or one strong line at the start and one at the end) of any message.
7. **End every communication with a specific recommendation, not an open question**: ending with "let me know what you think" or "open to your thoughts" signals the sender doesn't fully believe in their own ask, and creates cognitive friction for the recipient. Cited claim: people follow a specific, concrete recommendation (e.g., "let's get on a 20-minute call Thursday at 2pm, I'll send the invite tonight") roughly 60% more often than they respond to an open-ended question.

## Background business anecdotes/numbers referenced (context, not fully detailed elsewhere)
- Koerner's first business: an iPhone repair store in Tuscaloosa, AL, opened April 2011, nearly failed in its first weeks (personal-guarantee 5-year lease signed with a newborn at home). Growth tactic: ordered custom wristbands for ~$0.02 each and distributed them at college bars near campus, branded "Phone Restore" — students who broke phones while out drinking would wake up wearing the wristband and walk into the shop with it on. Business survived, expanded to 4 stores total, and was eventually sold for "about a million bucks."
- John McAfee pitch story (further detail referenced in the channel's Episode 9): Koerner guessed McAfee's email address and pitched him in person (2018) on a cryptocurrency price-prediction tool; McAfee agreed to a 25% partnership stake and active promotion; they built a community of 70,000+ people and launched a free "airdrop" token that reached a ~$30M market cap within months before the broader crypto market crash ended the venture.
- Referenced (not detailed in this transcript, but named/linked as further source material for related side-hustle numbers): Mike Yoder's drone deer-recovery/agricultural-spraying business ($50,000 in first 10 weeks); Shannon's roadside peach-stand business (100 peach stands opened in her first summer); Kevin Throop's Toasted Tours wine-tour business (see separate video note: knowledge/side-hustles/videos/2WUqdw5xtlg.md).

## Takeaway for side-hustle context
This episode is not itself a business-idea pitch — it's a meta-lesson on pitching/communicating any business, service, or ask. Its practical value for a side-hustle builder is in customer acquisition messaging (cold outreach openers, plain-language positioning, ending outreach with a specific next step) and in illustrating, via other guests' cited numbers, what "specific and provable" business claims sound like when telling your own story to customers, partners, or press.
