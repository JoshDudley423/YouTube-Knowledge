# "It's Not That Easy?" Sam Launched Overnight to Prove It Is
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcFBcfrmC2k | Published: 2025-05-28

Interview/case study with recurring guest "Sam" about launching a real DTC e-commerce brand (hummingbirdfood.co, selling branded sugar as hummingbird nectar mix) in response to a Twitter argument, plus a broader riff on "exploding topics" ecommerce/digital-product ideas and "offer vs. vehicle" strategy.

## The origin and premise
- Sam posted on Twitter about an existing Amazon seller doing an estimated **$20-30 million in lifetime sales** of pre-mixed hummingbird nectar (literally sugar water — 1 part sugar to 4 parts water — sold at ~$11 for 64oz, with preservatives added for shelf stability). Evidence cited: the Amazon listing showed "80,000 people bought this more than once," 24,000 reviews (at an estimated ~1% Amazon review rate, implying **~2.4 million total purchases**).
- An anonymous account replied "it's not that easy" to his tweet, which Sam took as a challenge; he built and launched **hummingbirdfood.co** the next morning, filming the entire process publicly (linked from his Twitter bio).

## Product and sourcing (real, run "in the garage")
- Product: nectar mix sold as plain sugar in branded bags (host is explicit about this both to the interviewer and on the website itself — "it's just sugar in a bag, branded as hummingbird nectar").
- Sourcing: buys 50-lb bags of sugar from a local grocery store (Smart & Final) for **$39.97/bag**, scoops and repackages by hand into smaller bags in his garage using a **~$20 Amazon heat sealer**.
- Packaging: sells 2-lb (actually ~1.8 lb) bags, multi-packs only (stopped selling single packs — see below).
- Mentions a longer-term sourcing plan: move to a co-packer/baking-mix manufacturer to have "just sugar in a bag" produced at scale rather than hand-packing, and possibly list on Amazon the following year once the DTC test proves the model (goal stated: "I think I can do a million dollars a year on this" at even 1% of the existing competitor's market).

## Unit economics (concrete numbers)
- **COGS**: ~$3-3.51 per unit (raw materials only — sugar + bag — not counting labor or shipping).
- **Price**: $18 for a 2-lb bag → roughly **80% gross margin** before shipping/labor.
- **Stated rule of thumb for any CPG/Shopify-via-paid-ads product**: needs **70-80% gross margin** to work as a paid-acquisition-driven business, and **AOV should be north of $50** — cites that even a $1,000 mattress-style DTC product needs a retail price around 5x its target CAC (e.g., if CAC is ~$300-400, the product needs to be priced ~$1,000+ to work).
- **Results after first ~2.5 weeks**: ~$5,000 in sales; achieved roughly a **1.5x ROAS** at best; some ad sets/days saw purchases at **~$14 cost per acquisition**, with **AOV hovering around $45-50** partly thanks to an in-cart upsell (see below).
- **Shipping problem** (the single biggest cost issue flagged): averaging **~$12/shipment** to ship ~2 lbs of sugar via boxes (single packs go in a poly bag instead of a box); this is described as the main threat to profitability ("the thing that's killing hummingbirds right now is shipping"). Discussed mitigations: negotiating with a 3PL (quoted as low as $4-5/shipment), and the host's suggestion to **drop product weight to under 16oz** to qualify for cheaper USPS First Class shipping tiers (host's specific tip: 15oz vs. 1.1lb is roughly half the shipping cost — ~$4 vs ~$8 — because 16oz is the first-class cutoff).
- **Seasonality**: explicitly a May-to-September seasonal business (confirmed by checking Amazon competitor sales patterns via Jungle Scout); expects CAC to fall further in-season with more ad optimization, and expects the whole opportunity to shrink outside that window.
- **In-cart upsell**: a $14 ebook add-on ("Hummingbird Attraction 101") with $0 COGS, converting **5-6% of checkouts**, meaningfully lifting AOV; created for essentially free using ChatGPT (see below) and paired with a free-shipping threshold to further incentivize the add-on.

## Ad/marketing tactics (Meta ads specifics)
- Platform: Meta only (Facebook + Instagram), using "Advantage+" placements (letting Facebook auto-distribute across newsfeed/stories/etc.) rather than manually restricting placements.
- Budget structure: runs **ABO (Ad Set Budget Optimization)**, not CBO — sets budget at the ad-set level so Facebook can allocate spend across multiple ad creatives within a set based on performance.
- Structure used: two parallel campaigns — a **"testing" campaign** (new creative batches, e.g., 5 creatives at $100/day) and a **"winning" campaign** (creatives that prove out get moved here to scale) — described as applying the Pareto principle (a small number of creatives dramatically outperform the rest).
- Creative production: used ChatGPT/AI image generation to create product mockup scenes (e.g., "a woman holding this bag," "this laying on a table") from a single downloaded mockup image, turning those into ad creatives cheaply. Store built with **Solo Drop** (a Shopify-store-building tool referenced elsewhere in the channel).
- The ebook upsell was written entirely with ChatGPT in about **12 minutes**: prompt approach was "write a detailed outline of 10-12 chapters," then iteratively "now write chapter one... now chapter two" within the same chat so it retains context — explicit "V1 mentality": don't polish content until the market proves it'll pay for it ("if people are actually buying it, I will take the three days to make it good... build it and they probably won't come, sell it first and you know they're coming").

## Broader "exploding topics" idea-generation session (tool + concrete idea list)
- Tool named: **Exploding Topics** (paid subscription) — surfaces technology/product search terms sorted by recent growth rate, filterable by timeframe and by "growth exploding" vs. established/branded terms.
- Brainstormed digital-product/ebook ideas live against currently-trending terms (illustrative, not validated businesses): robotic/smart lawnmower build guide, inflatable projector screen, portable neck fan, mini portable air conditioner (~200% search growth) build guide, digital planner build guide (using AI), phone cooler for gaming/streaming, wrist/dumbbell-style weight bracelets (rebranding ankle weights as wrist weights), PDRN skincare mask, eyebrow-specific serum, filtered showerhead, "mop soap," a breath-control wearable device.
- Concrete white-label/dropship path discussed for the eyebrow-serum idea: **Suppleful.com** — a supplement drop-shipping fulfillment platform offering white-label peptide/hair-serum products; stack described as: Solo Drop (store) + Suppleful (fulfillment) + Canva (branding/templates) + ChatGPT (copy) + Facebook Ads Manager (traffic) — framed as launchable "today."
- General framework: pick topics from the health/wellness or seasonal ("summer") categories on a **1-year timeframe filter** specifically to avoid picking something that's only trending due to seasonality (unless intentionally targeting a seasonal window, as with the hummingbird product itself).

## Strategic framework: "offer vs. vehicle" (the episode's most generalizable idea)
- Core distinction: an **offer** is the underlying value proposition/outcome (e.g., in B2B: "help you make more money, save time, or save money"); a **vehicle** is the specific product form that offer is delivered in — and the same offer can be sold through many different vehicles: a done-for-you service, a one-time digital product/guide, a SaaS/software subscription, a coaching program, or a community.
- CPG analogy given: creatine has been sold historically as a powder, but is now also sold as gummies, drinks, bars — same active ingredient/offer, different "vehicle"/form factor. Cited example: TryCreate.com's founder succeeded largely by changing creatine's form factor (to a ready-to-drink format) rather than inventing a new offer.
- Actionable advice: **start with the easiest/lowest-friction vehicle for an offer, not the "coolest" one** — most people default to wanting to build a SaaS/software product, which the guest argues is usually the hardest vehicle to launch and iterate on. Recommends validating an offer first as a **service** (sell it directly to a human before building any product), because customers will directly tell you what they actually want/value, and iterating a service or ebook is far cheaper/faster than iterating software. Software only becomes the better vehicle once you have enough proven customer volume (mentioned rough threshold: "at a thousand customers, software makes more sense") — at low volume (e.g., fighting for the first 10 customers), a service will make more money than a software MVP.
- Guest's own history cited as proof of this pattern: his ad-creative software business ("Creative OS") began 3 years earlier as a services agency ("Better Creatives") selling the same underlying offer (higher-converting ad creatives), which was then packaged as a one-time digital template pack, and only later became a software product — the offer never changed, only the vehicle, in increasing order of build complexity as validation accumulated.
- Related aside: guest recommends launching low-stakes **digital product Shopify stores** (zero COGS, zero shipping, "fulfillment is just sending an email") as a low-risk way to practice/learn Facebook ads mechanics, offer/positioning testing, and general DTC skills that transfer to any future business — mentions he is separately building one specifically because he's frustrated with physical shipping/fulfillment logistics in the hummingbird business.

## Explicit caveats
- The whole hummingbird venture is presented as a scrappy "V1/prove-it-works" test, not a scaled operation: no shipping software (ShipStation) or optimized packaging/pricing yet at the time of filming; inventory had run out (only 250 bags ordered) and ads were paused waiting on restock.
- Guest explicitly anticipates and preempts "hater" criticism (buying supermarket sugar, hand-packing in a garage) by pointing to the concrete result (~$5,000 in sales in 21 days) as the rebuttal — framing this as evidence that simple/unglamorous execution beats theorizing.
- Shipping cost is called out clearly as the single biggest threat to the business's core unit economics as currently run.
