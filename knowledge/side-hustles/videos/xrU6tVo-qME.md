# How Anyone Can Make $10K+/Month From the Government
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xrU6tVo-qME | Published: 2026-05-14

Interview with Natalie (@natalietheceo), who does government contracting as a side hustle/business: she bids on government contracts, subcontracts the actual work to a company that does it, and pockets the spread between what the government pays her and what she pays the subcontractor.

## The business model
- Register an LLC (any state, $100-$300 filing fee), then register free on sam.gov as a small business. That alone qualifies you to bid on government contracts — no experience, license, or track record required for contracts under $350,000 (see thresholds below).
- Find a contract (RFQ/RFP) on sam.gov for a service you can understand (e.g., painting, waste disposal, catering, landscaping).
- Call/find a subcontractor (a real company that does that work) and get a price quote from them BEFORE submitting your bid — never bid without already having someone lined up to do the work if you win.
- Submit your bid at your subcontractor's price plus your markup.
- If you win, government pays you (net 30 or net 45, sometimes faster on small purchase-card buys under $10-15K), then you pay your subcontractor. You get paid first, then pay who you owe — no need to front money.
- You must legally perform/add at least some value yourself (coordination, strategy, project management) — pure pass-through with zero involvement is illegal ("pass-through scheme"). Flying out to inspect sites, help a client find an apartment, etc. counts as her "adding value."

## Naming and positioning
- Use a generic/broad LLC name (e.g., "Natalie's Services LLC" not "Natalie's Painting LLC") so you're not pigeonholed and aren't judged on your business name — many contracts are decided purely on lowest price + being "reasonable" (decent price + followed submission requirements correctly).
- Prefer services over products when starting out — easier to negotiate net-30 payment terms with a service provider than with a manufacturer who has to front inventory/shipping.
- Under $350,000 contract value: it becomes an RFQ (request for quote) — no past performance/track record needed, just a competitive quote.
- Over $350,000: becomes an RFP (request for proposal) — you need past performance, either your own or your subcontractor's/teaming partner's (using their track record counts toward your bid).

## Concrete numbers from her deals
- First contract (hazardous waste/formalin disposal pickup, California, 5-year contract): subcontractor quoted $700/pickup, she bid $1,500/pickup, won. ~14-16 pickups/year → ~$800 profit/pickup → ~$10,000 net profit/year for 5 years (through 2029), for essentially "an hour a month" of maintenance work (submitting invoices).
- Catering contract (Idaho, feeding soldiers breakfast/lunch/dinner for 2 weeks, ~300 people/meal): subcontractor charged her $8/$10/$11 per breakfast/lunch/dinner; she charged government $10/$12/$15. Subcontractor total $41,000, she billed government $52,000 → $11,000 profit in 2 weeks.
- Landscaping contract (Colorado, 5-year, largest deal): subcontractor (a 6-person landscaping team) charged her $700,000; she billed government $962,000 → ~$262,000 gross profit over 5 years. She later found the same contract had previously been awarded at $1.6M, meaning she likely underpriced by ~$200K+ and could have charged more.
- Relocation/eminent-domain contract (Virginia, helping a woman find/apply for a new apartment after the government bought her house near the Dismal Swamp to build a parking lot): she did the work herself (no subcontractor), bid $14,000, got paid in progress payments ($5K, $5K, $4K). Required only a couple of trips to Virginia and some Google apartment searching — she estimates well over $1,000/hour effective rate.
- Current run rate: ~$10,000-$12,000/month profit across her contracts; best single month ~$24,000 (multiple contracts overlapping); landscaping-heavy months can dip to $4,000-$5,000 in off-season since revenue follows service level required.
- Win rate: early on submitted 14 bids in a month, won 1. More recently: submitted 9, won 4. First bid she ever submitted, she won.
- She estimates roughly 8-9% of government contract listings get zero bids at all — an "untapped market."

## Process / weekly routine
- Time investment to WIN a contract: a few hours/week of prospecting and paperwork (she runs a set weekly schedule: Monday = find opportunities [2 hrs], Tuesday = draft scopes of work, Wednesday = call subcontractors, Thursday = gather info/quotes, Friday = look again).
- Time investment to MAINTAIN a contract already won: about "an hour a month" total across all contracts combined — mostly just submitting invoices — provided you have reliable subcontractors.
- Most work is remote/digital (Zoom, phone, email); in-person site visits are optional (she flew to Colorado and Virginia by choice, not requirement).
- You can swap subcontractors mid-contract if they flake, but it's a hassle (may cost more) and you remain liable to the government either way — the contract is yours, not the subcontractor's.

## How to find/research contracts and pricing
- sam.gov: free, public listing of all federal government contract opportunities (also state/local/city/school/university procurement systems exist separately).
- USASpending.gov: free public database to look up what the government has previously paid for similar work (by NAICS industry code) — use this to price bids competitively/aggressively rather than guessing.
- The now-decommissioned FPDS website used to also show number of bidders on past contracts; sam.gov now lets you self-register as a "potential vendor" to see how many others are circling a given contract.
- Free advisory help: local PTAC offices and APEX Accelerator offices give free step-by-step and proposal-review help; also consult a government contracting attorney or contracting officer before structuring subcontracting relationships.
- She uses Claude (or ChatGPT) heavily: creates a "project" per solicitation, uploads all the attachments (contracts often have 15-25 attachments), and uses a custom prompt that positions the AI as an expert in government contracting + that specific industry, to quickly decide whether to bid, identify submission requirements, and draft the proposal.

## Selecting subcontractors
- Look for hunger/responsiveness: if they take days to respond or seem too busy, skip them — correlates with poor performance.
- Check reviews, website, prior clients, professionalism of communication.
- Prefer small, established businesses (a small team, e.g. 6 people) over solo operators (risk if the one person is unavailable) and over giant firms (may deprioritize a small contract).
- Communication and honesty are critical — a subcontractor should flag problems early rather than let them surface as a dispute with the government.

## Special programs / eligibility notes
- Certifications for women-owned, minority-owned (Black/Hispanic/other), veteran-owned businesses, or businesses in low-income areas ("HUBZone") can unlock set-aside contracts unavailable to others; free to apply via SBA certifications. Government has a stated goal of awarding at least 5% of contracts to women-owned businesses.
- Felons CAN run this business (they aren't the ones on-site doing the physical work; subcontractors are) — but subcontractors themselves generally cannot be felons if they must be on a government facility site.
- You can request contract modifications if the government's scope of work was incomplete/wrong — she got a Colorado contract bumped from $800K to $962K after her subcontractor flagged that additional work wasn't originally specified in the scope.
- No limit on number of contracts you can bid/win simultaneously.
- Scaling path beyond direct contracting: become a consultant helping other (larger, established) small businesses win government contracts, taking a percentage — since domain-expert businesses often don't understand the bidding process and lose to less-experienced-but-process-savvy bidders like her.

## Caveats / legal risk
- "Limitation on subcontracting" clause (per post-episode correction from a contracting expert, Natalie the guest's peer "Natalie"'s clarifications appended by host): on contracts where this clause applies, your company must perform at least 51% of the work yourself. Two compliant workarounds: (1) a formal teaming arrangement where you lease the subcontractor's employees to your company for the contract duration, or (2) both parties qualify as "similarly situated entities" (e.g., both certified small businesses under a small-business set-aside), verified via SAM.gov registration.
- A pure pass-through with no added value/strategy/coordination from you is illegal.
- Winning isn't guaranteed — expect to lose most individual bids; success is a numbers game (submit many, expect a low but improving hit rate over time).
- Formatting/submission errors can disqualify an otherwise-good bid regardless of price or capability — the government won't even evaluate a non-compliant submission.
- Markups should be based on researched historical government spending data (via USASpending.gov), not arbitrary percentages — factor in overhead and unforeseen costs.
- The government always tends to pick the lowest "reasonable" bid regardless of your experience/reputation, so overcharging because "it's the government" often backfires against experienced firms that assume they'll win on reputation alone.
