# Behind the Scenes of Accidentally Launching a Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5NmPDoNoS8A | Published: 2024-08-02

"HoldCo Bros" episode with Chris Koerner and Nik (Nikonomics). Covers a real-time case study of turning a viral video into a vending-machine distribution business, a LinkedIn/Twitter DM-filtering business idea, and a lengthy tangent on the Lou Pearlman boy-band Ponzi scheme (mostly entrepreneurship psychology, minimal actionable business substance).

## Case study: turning a viral video into a vending machine distribution business
- Origin: Chris saw a perfume vending machine product on Instagram in March, contacted the manufacturer about becoming an exclusive distributor, got a lukewarm response, shelved the idea.
- ~5 months later, he mentioned the idea on his podcast, then made a quick (5-minute) Instagram Reel about perfume vending machines purely to promote the podcast episode — it went massively viral: ~8M views on Instagram, ~3M on TikTok, his best-performing content ever on both platforms, plus a surge in YouTube subscribers.
- Viewers flooded his DMs asking "where can I buy these?" Rather than wait on the manufacturer's slow response (they offered to talk "in 5-6 days"), Chris immediately: bought the domain perfumevending.com on Namecheap, threw up a bare-bones lead-capture form, pinned an Instagram comment linking to it, changed his Instagram bio to point to the site, and set up an Instagram DM auto-reply that triggered on any DM containing the words "fragrance," "perfume," or "cologne," replying with the website link plus links to his YouTube channel and newsletter.
- Lead volume: within a day of going viral, leads came in every ~3 minutes; a week later, still every ~12 minutes. After one week: 1,000+ leads (updated mid-episode to "1,082 leads"), averaging ~8 machines of interest per lead, at $3,000/machine — framed as representing ~$24-30 million in potential machine sales.
- Automation stack used to manage the lead flow: Carrd (landing page host, but weak native form handling) piped via **Zapier** into Google Sheets, plus a second Zap to auto-email each lead a Luma link to register for a webinar and a link to book a sales call; outbound emails sent from a personal Gmail account (no dedicated ESP like Klaviyo). Setup took about an hour initially but "kept breaking" and needed ongoing fixes (accidentally triple-emailed some leads).
- Ran a webinar pitching over 100 of these leads directly, and had 30-50 sales calls booked for the following week.
- Business model options considered with the manufacturer (a small overseas manufacturer with a patent, not yet selling into North America): (1) sidestep the manufacturer entirely and source/manufacture through the host's own existing China relationships (14+ years importing hardware/electronics), (2) become a wholesale distributor — buy units directly and resell them, taking on inventory/logistics, or (3) act purely as a sales/lead-gen organization — generate demand, educate, and close sales, then hand off shipping/manufacturing/maintenance to the manufacturer. They explicitly chose to negotiate for **option 3 (agent/sales-partner model)** specifically to avoid taking on an "operationally intensive" business — securing a steep unit discount and status as an authorized reseller for Canada, Mexico, and the US.
- Explicit self-assessment: "we're middlemen ... we're okay being a middleman" (i.e., not manufacturers or inventors, just distribution/sales layer).
- Caveat at time of recording: contract still being finalized, zero units actually sold yet — framed as a real-time experiment to be updated in future episodes; close rate from the booked sales calls was unknown.
- General lesson drawn: **speed of execution on an inbound viral opportunity** (capturing leads within hours, not days) was the key differentiator; also framed as evidence you don't need to be "the most qualified" person in a niche to capitalize on demand — distribution/audience access can substitute for domain expertise.

## LinkedIn/Twitter DM-filtering business idea (unbuilt, brainstormed)
- Problem identified: high-follower-count people get flooded with generic sales-pitch DMs (example given: someone offering a "free 10-minute user interview" that's actually a setup for a 30-minute sales pitch). Chris currently has an executive assistant manually track/triage his DMs (mostly Twitter) in a spreadsheet — called a "nightmare" but valuable because occasional real opportunities (example: a genuine podcast-guest pitch nearly dismissed as spam) get missed without a human filter.
- Proposed business: offer a **concierge DM-management/filtering service** — client defines their own qualification criteria (e.g., "only surface DMs about a vetted business idea"), and the service (a human filterer, or ideally an automated/chatbot-based intent-detection layer) filters inbound DMs against that criteria and only escalates matches.
- Acknowledged technical obstacle: Twitter/LinkedIn API access and permissions make this hard to automate/scrape reliably (a prior real product, reportedly built by "Girdley," did something similar — converting Twitter DMs into email so you could reply via email — and shut down; attributed by Chris either to Twitter's API changes or the business simply not gaining enough customers).
- Proposed distribution/customer acquisition tactic for this exact product: **automated outbound DMs** targeted specifically at accounts with large follower counts (i.e., people who themselves get a lot of spam DMs) — e.g., target accounts with 7,000+ followers, skip accounts with only a few hundred, with an opening line like "This is the last DM you'll ever get" or "Are you tired of getting DMs? Let me help clean up your inbox."
- Explicitly flagged as a **niche, not mass-market opportunity** — Chris says he personally would pay for such a tool given DM volume, but doesn't believe it's a big enough market to be more than a small/niche business.

## Entrepreneurship/psychology tangent (not directly actionable, but repeated themes)
- "You don't just compound your money, you compound your learnings and experience" — every failed side hustle or bad decision is framed as a long-term asset, not wasted effort.
- Discussion of Lou Pearlman (boy-band manager, subject of Netflix documentary "Dirty Pop") as a cautionary tale: built Backstreet Boys/NSYNC through aggressive distribution work (assembling the acts, raising millions from investors, securing a BMG record deal, personally traveling everywhere with the bands) while structuring contracts so he/BMG captured the vast majority of the money; ultimately ran a Ponzi scheme (~$1B, less than a third ever recovered) across fabricated shell companies.
- Key business framework repeated approvingly: **"first-time founders focus on product, second-time founders focus on distribution"** — argument that Pearlman's real (if ethically compromised) value was distribution (assembling the talent, raising capital, and securing label/distribution deals), not the "product" (the music/performance) itself.
- General observation on founder/investor equity splits: capital-only partners who take large equity (e.g., 70/30 in their favor) for one-time capital contributions tend to create resentment over time due to "recency bias" — if the deal plays out over years, the operating partner increasingly feels the capital partner "hasn't done anything lately," even though the original investment was what made everything possible; this dynamic is described as less corrosive when the deal/relationship is short-lived (e.g., a hard-money real estate loan resolved in a few months) versus multi-year.
- Observation on fraud/Ponzi psychology: schemes like Pearlman's, Theranos, or Fyre Festival tend to escalate because the founder has previously always found a last-minute rescue (e.g., a new investor) when near collapse, reinforcing the belief that they'll always find a way out — the hosts argue you need to actually experience a real, un-rescued failure early on to develop the judgment that prevents this kind of escalating self-deception.

## Caveats
- The perfume vending machine business numbers ($24-30M in "potential" leads, 1,000+ leads) are unvalidated pipeline/interest figures at the time of recording, not actual sales or revenue — zero units had been sold when this episode was recorded.
- The LinkedIn/Twitter DM-filtering idea is purely speculative brainstorming, not a built or tested product.
