# The Most Profitable Business Everyone Overlooks
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eD2AMYAR8tA | Published: 2026-04-06

Interview with Mike Yoder (raised Amish, ex-tree care business owner), founder of Drone Deer Recovery and NuWay Ag -- covers two related drone businesses: deer recovery (the original) and agricultural spray drones (the bigger, more scalable opportunity). Combined businesses did $32M+ in revenue last year.

## Business 1: Drone Deer Recovery (original business, started 2022)
- Service: use a thermal-camera-equipped drone to locate deer that hunters shot but couldn't track down/recover (vs. hiring a tracking dog, the prior standard solution).
- Origin: bought a cheap DJI Dual drone (thermal + zoom camera) mainly to make YouTube content of his own hunting land; realized the underlying service was valuable.
- Initial customer validation/launch: brought a laptop and a video of the drone footage to a local outdoor/sportsman expo (Northeast Ohio Sportsman Show) with a bare-bones booth -- the booth became the most talked-about display at the show and drew a crowd so large the organizers asked him to move it; ran out of business cards on the spot.
- Pricing discovery: initial float price of $250-350 was corrected upward by prospective customers at the expo who said "that's not enough" (they were comparing to ~$350 tracking-dog services). Because these commenters were themselves a self-selected group who occasionally need this rare service (an unbiased sample, unlikely to become customers but knowledgeable about willingness-to-pay), Koerner notes this is unusually reliable pricing feedback compared to typical "would you buy this" market research. Launched the actual service that September at $550 if the deer is found, $450 if not -- a premium over the $350 dog-tracking alternative, justified because the drone doesn't disturb/scatter the rest of the deer on the property (a real downside of tracking dogs) and boasts a ~98% find rate.
- Additional early customer acquisition: printed flyers with before/after thermal-vs-zoom images, distributed to local bow shops and rifle ranges; word-of-mouth within the tight-knit hunting community grew from there.
- Revenue: made $50,000 in his first 10 weeks (his first deer season operating publicly).
- Regulatory note: legality was ambiguous at the time; he proactively contacted the Ohio Department of Natural Resources, got informal verbal approval as long as the drone wasn't used to locate deer for the purpose of then shooting them (only for post-shot recovery), and later met in person with skeptical local game wardens to demonstrate the equipment and build trust. Ohio has since formally updated hunting regulations to explicitly permit drone-assisted recovery.
- He was not the first in the world to do this (found a drone from a Wisconsin operator already doing it quietly) but was the first to make it public/mainstream via YouTube content -- this both helped competitors and expanded total market awareness.
- Business model evolution as competition increased and demand for his direct service work declined: built "Drone Deer Recovery" into a lead-generation/directory platform (compared to Yelp or pre-acquisition HomeAdvisor) -- independent drone operators pay a $500/year subscription to be listed on a pilot-locator map; customers search by zip code and get a phone number via text/click-to-contact, and calls are trackable. Roughly 200-300 active pilot listings at a given time (fluctuates seasonally) -- described as "low six figures a year" in membership revenue alone, plus outsized value from becoming the SEO/traffic authority in the space (network-effect moat: hard for a new directory competitor to replicate the backlinks/YouTube history/organic search ranking built up over years).
- Traffic scale: ~400,000 website visits in a single peak month during deer season (2024 data); 2.5-3 million combined views across YouTube/TikTok/Instagram.
- Business lesson/regret: failed to trademark "Drone Deer Recovery" early; by the time he tried, the phrase had become generic in his space, causing brand confusion when copycats used similar names (e.g., "Drone Deer Recovery Tennessee") -- compared explicitly to the Spikeball/Roundnet trademark-genericization problem. Ultimately his SEO dominance made the copycat confusion self-resolving, but he wishes he'd trademarked it before going viral.
- Team: 28 employees on payroll across the combined business.

## Business 2: NuWay Ag -- agricultural spray drones (the bigger opportunity)
- Core pitch: drone crop-spraying (herbicide, fungicide, pesticide, fertilizer application) as a service business, targeted especially at aspiring entrepreneurs (including former/current military members) rather than at farmers buying the equipment themselves -- most farmers currently prefer to hire an operator rather than adopt/learn/maintain the technology themselves.
- Personal results shared: made $132,000 profit in 24 days on one job; a separate job netted $65,000 profit in 8 days. First operating season overall, made $50,000 in 10 weeks -- revenue has "600x'd" since. Last year, the combined businesses (deer recovery + ag spray) did over $32 million in revenue.
- Unit economics: net profit margins around 70% (up to 85-90% on rolling/hilly terrain where competing methods struggle). Operating cost: roughly $3-3.74/acre. Pricing charged to farmers: $10-20/acre depending on terrain, competitive with existing alternatives:
  - Fixed-wing airplane spraying: ~$8/acre on flat land (e.g., Kansas).
  - Ground rig spraying: ~$6-8/acre.
  - Drone spraying on hard-to-reach/rugged/tree-obstructed acreage where airplanes and ground rigs can't operate at all: $20-25/acre, since there's effectively no competing option.
  - Drones can match or beat airplane pricing on flat land too, and offer more precise, consistent application (airplanes commonly overshoot/undershoot near obstacles like power lines; drones fly to an obstacle, turn, and return at a consistent low altitude).
  - The farmer separately buys the actual chemical/fertilizer product (e.g., from a supplier); the operator's fee is purely for application labor/equipment (based on roughly "2-gallon work" per acre as the baseline reference rate, with a small upcharge for higher-volume applications like 5 gallons/acre).
- Market size: DJI-sourced figures cited -- 99 million corn acres, 82 million soybean acres, 46 million wheat acres, 52 million pasture acres, 11 million cotton acres in the US; over a billion "acre treatments" needed annually nationwide (an acre sprayed multiple times per season counts multiple times). Current market penetration by drones estimated at only ~2.5-3%, leaving 94-97% of the addressable market untapped.
- Application frequency (recurring revenue potential): varies by crop -- corn/beans typically get one herbicide application per year (sometimes a second fungicide pass); pastureland gets sprayed roughly twice a year for a few years then drops to a 2-4 year maintenance cycle; specialty crops like onions can require weekly spraying for 6-8 consecutive weeks during the growing season; cotton (~11M US acres) is sprayed roughly weekly until harvest-ready.
- Customer acquisition method (the key practical advice): you must physically demonstrate the drone spraying on the prospective farmer's own field (e.g., spraying plain water) -- cold pitching with only a business card doesn't work since many farmers don't understand what a spray drone even is. Also effective: showing up at farm co-ops (where farmers buy seed/chemical) with the visibly-equipped trailer/rig exposed, which itself signals legitimacy and starts conversations. Close rates after an in-person demo are described as very high because you're not asking farmers to adopt a new spending category -- you're offering to redirect money they already spend on an existing alternative (airplane/ground rig) to a better/comparable-priced option. Word-of-mouth is strong: competing operators/farmers watching a drone spray a neighboring field (including farmers already using an airplane) frequently become customers themselves after seeing it in person ("wow factor").
- Equipment: primary drone referenced is the DJI T-100 (26-gallon tank capacity); operators typically fly ~12-13 acres per tank load at 2-gallon/acre application rates; battery swaps required roughly every 4-6 minutes of flight, batteries take ~8-9 minutes to recharge, so operators run a rotation of at least 3 batteries per drone to keep continuous operation going. Spraying is fully automated (GPS-guided autonomous missions) -- operators mostly manage logistics (refilling tank, swapping batteries, monitoring multiple drones like air traffic control) rather than manually piloting.
- Financing: equipment can be financed; typical buyer interest rates around 15% depending on credit.
- Regulatory/licensing path to start: (1) FAA Part 107 remote pilot's license (described as easy -- a few days of study), (2) state commercial pesticide applicator's license (also relatively easy, e.g., a 3-hour online course + test in Kentucky), and (3) an FAA Part 137 certificate required to legally dispense pesticides from the air over 55 lbs of drone weight (virtually all ag spray drones exceed 55 lbs) -- this federal certificate is described as very slow to obtain (3-4 months). To bridge that gap, NuWay Ag offers a "137 Fastpass" program letting new operators fly and earn revenue under NuWay's existing certificate/licensing within about 10 days of purchasing equipment, while their own certificate paperwork is still processing.
- Scaling model observed in the wild: some operators run multiple trailers/rigs and shift into pure management roles (dispatching operators rather than flying themselves) -- cited example: a Kansas-based operator running 10 trailers covering ~60,000 acres, largely staffed with the operator's military veteran contacts (framed as a strong fit for former/current military personnel who want outdoor, autonomous, structured work). Rough back-of-envelope math on that scale: 60,000 acres x ~$10/acre ≈ $600,000/season in revenue for that one operator's network. Yoder estimated he personally could cover the same 60,000 acres with only 4 trailers given his operating approach, versus the Kansas operator's 10 -- signaling meaningful efficiency variance between operators.
- Attrition/reliability: he reports essentially no involuntary customer churn from farmers switching to competitors; the only "lost" acreage came from farmers he sold rigs to who then started spraying their own or neighboring land themselves (a byproduct of his dual role as both service provider and equipment distributor).
- Side revenue stream: he is also a DJI drone distributor and describes himself as the #2 or #3 largest retailer of thermal drones in the US.
- Adjacent unexploited opportunity flagged: large agricultural chemical/product distributors and co-ops already have trusted farmer relationships and could easily add drone spray-application as a service line, but by and large haven't yet -- attributed to the space still being new.

## Caveats / practical notes explicitly raised
- Legal: you must have landowner permission before flying a drone over their property (can't just show up and fly uninvited, contrary to a cold-outreach idea floated by the host); FAA also requires visual line-of-sight monitoring of the drone during operation (though watching via camera monitor from an air-conditioned vehicle, as long as the drone stays within eyesight, appears to be treated as compliant in practice per the guest).
- This is physically demanding, outdoor, seasonal work ("hay while the sun shines") -- intense, long hours during a short spray season, described by Yoder as a "testosterone business" with strong outdoor/technology appeal to men in particular, which the host notes is itself a factor that can override pure price competition (farmers sometimes choose a pricier drone operator purely because the tech itself is compelling/cool to watch).
- Avoid recommending specific chemical products to farmers -- Yoder deliberately stays out of product recommendations to avoid blame if a product underperforms, sticking strictly to the application/spraying service.
- Neither business (deer recovery or ag spray) requires large starting capital relative to payoff, but the guest is explicit that most people underperform their potential in this industry by staying too small/passive (only servicing enough acres to make ends meet) rather than proactively pursuing expansion once initial demand validates the concept.
