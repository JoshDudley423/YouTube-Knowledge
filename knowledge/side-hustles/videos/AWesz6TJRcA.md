# How To Get Paid $2,500 + Monthly Checks for "Drag & Drop" AI Employees
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AWesz6TJRcA | Published: 2026-01-12

Interview with Danny, who built a voice-AI-agent side business for local service businesses (barbers, salons, etc.) starting with his own barber as a free pilot client. Concrete pricing, sales tactics, and lead-gen approach for a voice-agent agency.

## The idea and business model
- Sell AI voice agents to local, appointment-based small businesses (barbers, salons, nail/massage, home inspection, drone/exterior washing, remodeling/real estate, etc.) that replace voicemail: when the owner doesn't answer (they're busy working with hands-on clients), the AI voice agent picks up, answers FAQs, and texts a booking link or fills out a lead form.
- Niche logic explicitly stated: look for industries with a favorable "ticket size vs. number of businesses vs. approachability" tradeoff. Barbers are low-ticket but there are ~76,000 of them, are cheap/easy to approach (no gatekeeper/decision-maker to get past — you talk to the owner directly), and are a "captive audience" (a captive customer sitting in the chair for 30-plus minutes). Compare to dentists (higher ticket, but harder to reach the decision-maker) or pizza shops (~200,000) and convenience stores (~400,000) as other single-owner-operator niches AI now makes viable to sell agency services to, where it wasn't financially viable pre-AI (e.g., a convenience store owner won't pay $3,500/month for Facebook ads management, but a much cheaper voice agent pencils out).
- Framed by the host as a time-limited "Yelp moment" / "Google Business Profile moment" / early PPC-era opportunity — a window where an underused tactic is cheap/easy to execute before saturation drives costs/competition up (Danny's own background: made money 11 years ago optimizing Yelp profiles during a similarly narrow early window).

## How the agent works (technical/operational specifics)
- Built on GoHighLevel (GHL) — described as trainable "in a few clicks" by dragging in a screenshot of the business's Google Business Profile or simply linking their website; GHL's agent then automatically extracts business info (FAQs, hours, etc.) for the agent to use in conversation.
- Setup used for the barber: his existing work phone number was kept; instead of routing calls to voicemail after unanswered rings, calls route to the voice agent (this "replaces voicemail," not calls in general — the barber still gets first crack at answering if free).
- Agent conducts a natural, human-sounding conversation (GHL offers multiple voice personas; they chose a female voice) — does not proactively disclose it's AI unless directly asked. Most callers ask about hours, booking, and general business questions and don't ask if it's AI; per Danny, it doesn't matter to customers as long as their question gets answered.
- For a straightforward booking-driven business (like a barber), the agent texts the caller a direct booking link to the business's existing scheduling system rather than trying to fully complete the booking itself. Note: contrasted with a different treatment for a "medical practice" context, where the goal instead is to get the caller to book a call or talk to a live rep (implying more caution/compliance sensitivity in some verticals) — including handling subscription/membership-based service models.
- Reported real-world numbers for the pilot barber: 125 missed calls captured by the voice agent from ~November through the time of filming (roughly a month or so), especially spiking during the holiday season, with those calls resulting in actual bookings — described as converting "formerly missed calls" (FMCs) into revenue.

## Sales / go-to-market tactics (concrete and reusable)
- First client acquisition approach: do the first build completely free for someone already in your own life/circle of trust (in this case, his own barber, whom he saw weekly) — treat that person explicitly as a "lead magnet"/test dummy/case study rather than a paying customer. Payoff: the barber now refers other business owners (e.g., referred a medical-practice client) and gives Danny free haircuts in lieu of payment.
- Advice given generally: to land your very first agency client, tap your own sphere of influence for free (a family member, your own barber/mechanic/oil-change guy) — if the free pilot doesn't produce results/referrals worth talking about, that's a signal to revisit the product before trying to sell it.
- Pre-sales technique — build first, sell after: before the first client meeting, research the prospect's current website, use ChatGPT to identify concrete flaws, then build a live mockup (using a "vibe coding" tool, Replit) of an improved website in advance — show it in the first meeting rather than pitching in the abstract. Cited as reducing sales friction (buyer's confirmation bias: "if the free mockup is this good, the paid version must be even better") and reducing post-sale delivery time since much of the work is already done ("assume the sale").
- In-person live demo tactic specific to captive-audience service businesses: while getting a haircut (or during any similar sit-down service), pull out your phone, open the voice-agent platform, rename the agent's business profile to the prospect's business name in real time, then call the number live in front of them so they hear "their own" AI agent answer — a strong, immediate "wow" close. Danny has done this both for his own pitch and had his barber do it unprompted to other barbers/salon owners in a shared multi-suite salon building.
- KPI dashboarding as an upsell/retention hook: built (with a coding partner, via Replit) a small custom KPI dashboard for the barber's business showing things like best days to schedule promos, who's absorbing the most reschedules on a multi-barber team, etc. — framed as giving small, non-business-trained owners (e.g., someone trained in barbering, not business) visibility they've never had, which increases perceived value and stickiness. Explicitly recommends solving current, visible problems for the client (identified through a long discovery conversation about their goals) rather than "reinventing the wheel" with novel AI features nobody asked for.
- Discovery process: roughly a one-hour open conversation with a prospective client, focused entirely on understanding their goals/current business first, not on selling — used to identify what specific pain point (e.g., need to free up time to open more chairs/hire more barbers) is most valuable to address.
- Team structure: Danny (sales, vision, client-facing) partners with a co-founder who handles the technical/coding integration work (Replit-based builds can hit rough edges that need real engineering to finish).

## Pricing
- Barber client: first build was free, but Danny "played the what-if game" with him afterward — barber estimated he'd already paid ~$1,000 for a mediocre website elsewhere, valued the voice agent + KPI dashboard combination at easily $2,500 upfront plus roughly $200/month ongoing.
- Stated general pricing range across his broader client conversations: $1,000–$5,000 upfront depending on client sophistication/budget/stage of business, with monthly retainers in the low-to-mid hundreds (currently planning to raise the monthly fee toward $200–$500 depending on scope).
- Explicit pricing philosophy/caveat: don't charge a cash-strapped early-stage startup founder full price even if they want the service — offer free advice/guidance instead and focus paid work on clients who can actually afford and appreciate it.

## Results and caveats
- All of Danny's client pipeline so far (four to five inbound calls/week at time of filming) came from one free referral chain (the original barber), with zero ad spend or paid sponsorship — used purely as a proof of concept/showcase for prospective clients.
- Danny's own reflection on what he'd do differently: "start faster" — he argues against waiting to fully learn every skill before beginning, favoring learning by doing.
- Scalability caveat raised directly in the interview: door-to-door/one-at-a-time sales to barbers is time-intensive and arguably "not very scalable," but the host argues the high ticket price (relative to effort) and likely double-digit close rate (unconfirmed/estimated, not measured) makes the economics work regardless, especially against a large total addressable pool of businesses (76,000+ barbers alone).
- No independently audited revenue/profit totals are given for Danny's business as a whole — the numbers shared (125 missed calls, $2,500+$200/mo for one client, 4-5 inbound leads/week) are anecdotal from the business owner himself.
