# From Nothing to $20k/month with Email Newsletters: Full Tutorial
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xc2IjVqVdQY | Published: 2025-09-05

Interview with Jas (X: @CreatingJas), founder of Winnipeg Digest (winnipeg-digest.beehiiv.com), a local city newsletter/media business, on building it from zero to six figures in ~17 months and launching a Christmas-lights business ("Digest Lights") that piggybacks on it.

## The core business: local newsletter
- Winnipeg Digest covers Winnipeg, Canada (~800,000 population). Launched April 2024; by the time of filming (~17 months later) it reached 35,000 email subscribers plus 73,000 social followers (Instagram-focused), a combined reach over 100,000 Winnipeggers.
- Content strategy: "everything other than crime and politics" — local events, community updates, city bylaws/news, restaurant/hiking guides that rotate seasonally. Deliberately avoids politics (alienates half the audience) and violent crime coverage (bad vibe), occasionally mentioning crime only in neutral informational terms (e.g., "the most-wanted list was released").
- Cadence: started once/week, quickly increased to twice/week, then three times/week within about a month, once it became clear there was more local content than a single weekly edition could cover. Three times/week became the stable cadence (allows one shorter edition without complaints).
- Email length: 400-2,600 words, mostly driven by event listings each with a short description.
- Voice/tone: deliberately casual, conversational, "fifth-grade reading level," explicitly avoiding "newsletter jargon" or news-style writing — readers specifically praise the positive, conversational tone.
- Production: AI (Claude/ChatGPT) generates first drafts; two virtual assistants (one handles events research, one handles writing) do most day-to-day production; Jas does a final human review pass (~1 hour, done late at night) before sending.
- Total current time investment: ~20 hours/week (including sponsorship sales/ad production); without sponsor management, could run in ~7 hours/week. Early on, before automation/VAs, it consumed closer to 40 hours/week (his own writing plus manual video content creation).

## Subscriber growth & acquisition costs
- Subscriber acquisition cost: started around $0.15 CAD (~$0.11 USD); by time of filming had risen to roughly $1.30 CAD as the local market saturated.
- Growth rate: ~4,000-5,000 new subscribers/month early on; slowed to ~2,000/month at similar ad spend (~$3,000/month) later, indicating market saturation — he was considering shifting spend from paid ads toward referral/giveaway-driven growth once ad-driven growth plateaus.
- Primary paid channel: Facebook ads recommended as the dominant spend (his rule of thumb: "if you're going to spend $100, $90 should go to Facebook ads"). Instagram/organic content and referral-driven seasonal guides (e.g., "Top 50 things to do this winter") are supplementary but valuable acquisition channels.
- Market-size guidance: a city under 100,000-150,000 people is "too small" to sustain a viable local newsletter; a city over ~1 million is often "too much" content to cover meaningfully unless publishing 5x/week, and such large cities tend to fragment into effectively separate "different worlds" (e.g., north vs. south side) that don't share local interest — better split into two newsletters or by neighborhood/niche rather than covering the whole metro.
- First-mover advantage in an underserved market was central to Jas's low acquisition costs — Winnipeg had no competing local newsletter or even an aggregated events Instagram account before he launched. Advises anyone starting a local newsletter to prioritize a city where they'll have first-mover advantage over a city they know better but face competition in; for larger, already-saturated markets (e.g., Austin, which he says already has ~100 local newsletters), suggests instead targeting a specific suburb, neighborhood, or narrow demographic niche (e.g., "young dads in Austin," "freelancers in New York," "Williamsburg moms") rather than the whole metro.

## Monetization: subscriber value and ad pricing
- Subscriber lifetime value: at current open rates and one-ad-slot-per-issue capacity, each subscriber is worth roughly $8-$9/year if every ad slot sells out; given a very low ~0.05% unsubscribe rate, subscribers are expected to remain on the list 5+ years, implying a strong long-run LTV multiple on acquisition cost (host frames it as turning roughly $1 of acquisition spend into $40-$50 of lifetime ad revenue, with patience).
- Business model is annual, exclusive local sponsorships, NOT one-off ad sales — described as the single most important monetization lesson: sell category exclusivity (e.g., only one HVAC company sponsor per year) rather than one-time ad slots, since exclusivity ("a monopoly over 100,000 local readers") is the actual product being sold and one-off sponsorships don't build a sustainable revenue base.
- Ad pricing at time of filming: roughly $75 CPM (cost per thousand newsletter opens) for one-time/non-exclusive advertisers on a single newsletter send; roughly double that ($2,800) for a single Instagram feature/post. Annual sponsors get a much better effective rate: $12,000 for 12 monthly newsletter ad placements (one per month, deliberately capped at monthly frequency to avoid audience fatigue/oversaturation of a single sponsor).
- Revenue reported: newsletter ads alone project to about $144,000/year; combined with Instagram and other local monetization, total revenue is in the $200,000+ range (excluding the new Christmas lights venture). Monthly revenue at time of filming: $12,000-$15,000, with growth accelerating/compounding notably in the preceding 6 months.
- Best-fit sponsor types identified: high-ticket or highly-recurring-value local services — dentists, HVAC, plumbing, roofing, event venues (his single largest annual sponsor, an event venue, pays $40,000/year). Auto repair shops were cited (by the host) as an example of a category that might pay short-term but sees poor ROI and won't become long-term repeat sponsors, versus something like a bookstore that might be harder to initially convert but sees strong ROI and becomes a loyal recurring sponsor.
- Sales pitch framework for high-ticket sponsors: ask the prospect their average customer lifetime value (e.g., a dentist says $5,000/customer), then frame the ad spend against how few new customers are needed to break even (e.g., "if you spend $10,000 with us, you only need 4 new customers out of 35,000 readers to double your return").
- Deliberately avoids advertisers that would clash with the newsletter's brand/reader base (e.g., casinos, clubs) even at the cost of near-term revenue, since the majority-female, 35-65-year-old reader base isn't the target market for those and preserving trust/fit matters more long-term; also avoids using Beehiiv's built-in ad-network "boost" monetization features because those ads are irrelevant to a local audience and degrade the reader experience (hurting open rates over time).
- Best lead-gen tactic for sponsors: run an 8-question onboarding survey immediately after signup (built in Beehiiv) capturing demographics (neighborhood, homeownership status, buying/selling intent, household income) — reports an unusually high 87% survey completion rate, which becomes powerful ammunition when pitching advertisers (e.g., "75% of our audience are homeowners").
- Common actual lead source for landing bigger sponsors: young marketing coordinators at larger local businesses discover the Instagram account first (not the newsletter) and reach out wanting a feature; Jas then upsells them into the higher-value newsletter sponsorship during that call.

## Growth ceiling and the case for vertical integration
- Explicit ceiling stated: a local newsletter monetized purely via ad sales tops out around $500,000/year even in a great market with everything executed well — a real, viable outcome for someone content with that, but not "serious money" without further diversification.
- Jas's stated preference: build his own complementary local businesses on top of the newsletter's audience/brand ("vertical integration"), rather than expanding horizontally into other cities — reasoning: (1) expanding to new cities means competing for subscribers without an established first-mover cost advantage, whereas his own market's proven playbook stays cheap; (2) national/franchise-style horizontal newsletter networks (compared to competitor "6AM City") end up working with national brand advertisers who pay much lower CPMs (~$10) via lump-sum deals rather than the $75+ CPM achievable with hyper-local advertisers; (3) proving a strong "money model" (e.g., $200k newsletter + $200-500k Christmas lights) in one city may be more valuable than spreading thin across many cities.
- Vertically-integrated ad economics comparison made by the host: a podcast sponsor might pay ~$20/1,000 views, a platform-native ad unit (e.g., Spotify's own midroll ads) might pay as little as ~$6/1,000, but a business you personally own and advertise inside your own audience can capture ~$200/1,000 views-equivalent value — a 10-30x difference versus selling ad space to third parties, because you capture the full margin of the product/service sold, not just an ad fee.

## The new venture: Christmas lights installation (leasing model), piggybacked on the newsletter
- Rationale for choosing this specific business: (1) short, defined season (10 weeks) that doesn't compete for time with the core newsletter business — if it fails, he simply stops and loses little; (2) leasing model (rather than selling lights outright) creates high customer LTV — leased customers tend to stay 3-5 years, generating recurring revenue in following seasons rather than a single one-off sale; (3) natural upsell path into permanent/"jellyfish" lighting (installed under gutters, no seasonal takedown) at 4-5x the average order value of leasing seasonal lights — his plan is to pre-sell/discount that upsell during a customer's first Christmas-light season so the higher-margin sale happens the following year; (4) matches the newsletter's demographic — the primary reader base skews female, 35-65, who he believes are more often the household decision-maker for Christmas light purchases (unlike, say, HVAC/plumbing, where a customer wouldn't typically consult a lifestyle newsletter when their heat breaks); (5) leverages his own core skill (Facebook ads/marketing) rather than a business requiring door-to-door sales training he doesn't have.
- Note/counterpoint (from the host, citing a Pennsylvania home-services operator friend): permanent/jellyfish lighting can be an operational headache (one-time sale, ongoing maintenance/repair callbacks) and that operator avoids installing it unless a customer insists and won't buy otherwise — a caution against over-indexing on permanent lights as a strategy.

### Marketing/sales approach for the Christmas lights business
- Initial customer acquisition tactic: ran a Christmas-lights giveaway (inspired by a marketing tactic he'd seen referenced online) — one grand prize giveaway plus a $150-off consolation offer for every entrant; got ~300 entries. The business was initially named/branded distinctly ("Lights," no website yet) but designed to visually match Winnipeg Digest's brand colors, deliberately leaning into the newsletter's trust/audience rather than hiding the connection.
- Uses urgency/scarcity discount offers tied to early booking (e.g., "$150 off if you book before October 7th") to extend the season earlier than the industry-standard start date (~October 15th/September 15th for most competitors advertising).
- Sales technique that fixed an early conversion problem: initially tried closing deals on the spot at a "design consult" appointment, which converted poorly (only ~4 of the first 7-8 quotes closed). Switching to a structured, in-home consultative sales process (borrowed from a friend's door-to-door painting sales playbook) — walk the customer through a mockup book/booklet, present a high anchor price (e.g., quote $3,000 covering trees + full home + side of home), then reduce scope/price down to what the customer actually wants (e.g., down to $1,500), then apply a time-limited discount to close — improved conversion from roughly 4-of-8 to a projected 60-70% close rate by season's end.
- Plans to add a referral incentive (e.g., a $100 gift card per referred customer, a tactic borrowed from a Christmas-lights-industry contact on Twitter) once installations are complete and word-of-mouth outreach begins.

### Unit economics of the Christmas lights business (year one, self-operated)
- Average job size: ~$1,400.
- Hard cost (lights/materials only, excluding labor): ~$200-$300/job.
- Labor: owner-operated in year one (no labor cost counted yet); a job currently takes 3-4 hours; expected to be cut roughly in half in year two once lights are pre-cut and bulbs pre-crewed from the prior season.
- Projected future labor cost once hired out: roughly minimum wage (~$15/hour), ~3 workers x ~3 hours per job ≈ $100-$150 in labor cost per job.
- Year-over-year customer retention on the leasing model: ~85% average.
- Deliberately did the installation labor himself for the first season (despite being able to afford hiring help immediately from newsletter cash flow) specifically to learn the actual time/skill requirements before delegating — explicit principle voiced: know the operational "weeds" of a business before outsourcing/scaling away from doing the work yourself, so you can accurately judge a hired operator's performance later.
- Revenue projection: expects to safely hit $100,000 in the first 10-week season (some jobs already booked and converting before the main season even started), with an internal stretch goal of $150,000-$200,000.
- Long-term structural plan: doesn't intend to personally run the Christmas lights business long-term — plans to bring in an operator and offer them a vesting equity stake over a few years once the model is proven, rather than remain the hands-on operator.

## Broader vision: newsletter as a marketing/equity engine for other local businesses
- Longer-term ambition described: use Winnipeg Digest as a "fractional CMO" vehicle — approach other local businesses offering full marketing services (including exposure via the newsletter and his own personal social following) in exchange for an equity stake (e.g., 15-20%) rather than cash fees. Acknowledges this pitch isn't yet credible to business owners without a proven, self-owned example of delivering that kind of value, hence starting with a wholly-owned business (Christmas lights) first to prove the model before pursuing equity deals with other owners.
- Social media following is highlighted as a key unlock/differentiator for cold outreach: being a top-3 most-followed account in his city means local businesses feel almost obligated to respond to his outreach, dramatically improving reply rates versus cold email; recommends any local newsletter operator build a real social media presence alongside the email list for this reason, and to use having "two channels" (newsletter + social) as an upsell point with sponsors.
- Deliberately shifted from face-forward video content (which drove his early Instagram growth, prompted frequent public recognition) to faceless carousel-style content specifically so the media asset itself (not his personal identity) becomes a sellable/transferable business asset if he ever wants to sell it.

## Additional tactical tips mentioned
- Subject line best practices: keep under 5 words and under 40 characters; include a time/urgency cue (e.g., "this weekend"); avoid revealing the full answer in the subject line, since answering the reader's curiosity in the subject line reduces their reason to click (example cited: "New Winnipeg Costco Location" as a high-performing subject line, versus spelling out the address/date).
- Reported metrics: consistently 50%+ open rate (currently 56-60%), click-through rate 8-12% — both well above typical newsletter benchmarks.
- Caution against automated list-cleaning: an automated "clean my list" tool can wrongly purge subscribers who are actually opening emails but whose opens aren't being tracked correctly by the ESP (Beehiiv in this case) — recommends manual list cleaning infrequently (e.g., every couple months) instead of continuous automated pruning.
- List-acquisition growth-hack idea (proposed by the host, untested by Jas): approach local businesses that already collect customer emails but don't actively use the list, and offer them free newsletter ad space in exchange for access to (or inclusion in) their email list — since most small businesses collect emails passively and rarely email them, this can be a cheap subscriber acquisition channel; suggested testing via segmented cohorts (e.g., split a 20,000-person list into 4 groups of 5,000, testing different offers/angles) to measure open/engagement before committing broadly.
- Both hosts flagged an unbuilt market gap: no existing "SparkLoop"-style cross-promotion/referral network exists for local (as opposed to national) newsletters, since local newsletters in different cities have no natural cross-promotion value to each other's readers — floated as a separate potential business opportunity (an aggregated local-lead-referral network, e.g., letting national brands like Kohl's offer an opt-in incentive across many local newsletters at once).
- Best-performing ad creative formula (Facebook/Instagram, video preferred over static image): drone footage of a recognizable local landmark, headline text "Struggling to find things to do in [City]?", subhead "[Newsletter Name] sends you 50+ events happening in the city every week" — described as essentially the entire winning formula.

## Caveats
- The "easy $10-15k/subs" growth stage is, per Jas, not actually the hard part of this business — the harder, more failure-prone part is convincing local businesses to buy into a newsletter-advertising concept they've likely never used before; anyone can build the audience with basic Facebook ads, but converting that audience into paying sponsors is where most local newsletter operators struggle.
- Pure ad-sales monetization caps out around $500,000/year even under ideal conditions — a real ceiling for anyone treating this purely as an ad-sales business rather than pursuing vertical integration or other add-on ventures.
- Subscriber acquisition costs rise as a local market saturates (his own cost roughly rose ~9x from launch to the time of filming), meaning the earliest movers in a given city capture the cheapest growth.
