## Update: Perfume vending machine business (context, not a new idea)
- After a prior video about perfume vending machines went viral, they became the exclusive North American distributor for the manufacturer.
- First full month (August): sold 19 machines for $72,000 total revenue.
- Margins: initially ~35% gross margin (blended), because add-ons (card reader, bill reader, coin reader, stand, TV) were being passed through at cost with no markup — fixing that pricing was expected to raise blended margin to ~45%.
- Back-of-envelope gross profit for the month: ~$23,000-$24,000 before paying a sales rep and other expenses.
- Operational headaches flagged: manufacturer invoices in Euros while they sell in fixed USD pricing, creating currency-fluctuation exposure (~10%+ swings) with no resolved policy yet (unclear whether to reprice quarterly, monthly, or absorb it); over 2,500 inbound leads with a broken lead-tracking workflow (data split across Otter AI and HubSpot, no integration between them); hired a second sales rep to help manage volume.
- Key demand-side lesson: it's much easier to sell a vending machine to an end user who will place it on property they already own/operate (e.g., a gas station or gym owner) than to an "entrepreneur" buyer who wants to place it on someone else's property, because the latter requires them to do location research/vetting first, which adds friction and kills deals ("friction equals time, time equals dead deals").
- Buyers frequently want to talk to existing owners for expected-earnings validation, but at the time of recording there were no other US owners to refer them to (only European owners, who often don't speak English) — a real bootstrapping problem for a brand-new distribution business.

## Idea 1: "Car Fair" — used car marketplace without owning inventory
- Modeled on an existing real business, Sierra Auto Fair (Sacramento/NorCal, held in a community college parking lot).
- Concept: rent an otherwise-idle large parking lot (e.g., a high school or community college, unused on weekends) and run a weekend pop-up marketplace connecting private-party car sellers and buyers — you never touch inventory or facilitate payment.
- Sierra Auto Fair's actual mechanics: sellers pay ~$149 to reserve a numbered parking spot, fill out a form, scan a QR code, park the car, and leave keys with an attendant; they return ~2 days later to collect the car (or proceeds). Buyers attend for free, browse, and test-drive if the seller left keys. The organizer does NOT handle the purchase transaction (cash, cashier's check, etc. is between buyer and seller) — they only monetize the seller's parking-spot fee, though they do track which cars sold for testimonials/marketing.
- Identified as a "three-sided marketplace" (buyers, sellers, and the venue) — harder to balance than a typical two-sided marketplace; risk of mismatched supply/demand (e.g., 100 cars but 5 buyers, or vice versa) is called out as a real operational/embarrassment risk.
- Liability/legal concerns raised (unresolved in the conversation): permitting/approval needed from the city or venue to "conduct commerce," and unclear liability exposure to the organizer if a buyer/seller scam occurs.
- Best-fit categories for this model: higher-ticket physical goods people want to inspect before buying (cars, furniture, electronics) — not lower-value flea-market/craft-fair type goods.

### Concrete launch playbook proposed
1. Pre-sell the supply side first: get an initial cohort of ~20 sellers to pay in advance for a spot (e.g., discount first 20 sign-ups to $99 from a normal $199 price), planned ~2 months out, to lock in "real" committed cars before doing any demand marketing.
2. Alternative/refundable-deposit tactic to guarantee no-shows don't happen: charge sellers $100-200 upfront with a full refund conditioned on actually showing up (not on selling the car) — ensures commitment without truly costing the seller money.
3. Only accept cars that are likely to actually sell (e.g., popular models like Honda Civic, Ford F-150) rather than allowing anything, since the point is transactions, not a car show.
4. Build the demand side via organic Facebook groups/Marketplace posting (same playbook used for their home-service businesses), or paid Facebook ads; expect the first event to be a money-losing customer-acquisition investment (~"a couple thousand dollars") that becomes self-sustaining/word-of-mouth in subsequent years, similar to how Sierra Auto Fair likely grew organically pre-internet.
5. Direct-outreach variant: scrape Facebook Marketplace / AutoTrader.com for new private-party listings of high-demand vehicles; message sellers offering to bring multiple interested buyers to one time slot at the fair (creates a bidding/FOMO dynamic: "you have 4 people interested in your F-150 — bring it Saturday at 1pm and let them compete").
6. Distribution partnership idea: pay to place a coupon/ad inside local high school fundraiser coupon booklets (piggybacking on students already selling to friends/family in the community) as a way to reach a hyperlocal buyer/seller base.
7. Monetization layering (stacking revenue streams beyond the seller fee): partner with 3 local auto body shops/mechanics to sponsor the event in exchange for offering on-site paid inspections (e.g., $50/car quick diagnostic) — this also generates repair-shop leads for follow-on work; similarly recruit local used-car dealerships, banks/credit unions (private-party auto financing is a real product), and moving companies as sponsors, since attendees are in a "high-intent, cash-in-hand" life-transition moment.
8. Lead-monetization idea (flagged, with an ethical caveat baked into the banter): collect buyer registrant contact info (name/email/phone) and resell those leads to interested businesses (dealerships, detailing shops, repair shops) — explicitly NOT extending this to collecting sensitive data like SSNs/credit cards, which the hosts joke about as an identity-theft risk to avoid.
9. Format can be adapted to other high-ticket private-party good categories with the same playbook (example given: an "Apple electronics fair" restricted to iPads/MacBooks/iPhones of certain generations, with Geek Squad-style on-site diagnostics as the sponsor/inspection angle).

### Adjacent land-monetization variant (rejected as too risky)
- A real example cited: a business in Huntsville, AL runs an unattended 3-acre gravel lot next to a highway where people pay ~$200/month to park a car with a "for sale" sign; buyers just show up and call the listed number, no attendant needed.
- Proposed variant: lease ~20 parking spots at the edge of a large retail parking lot (e.g., Walmart, Hobby Lobby) — the least desirable spots for the retailer (far from the store) are the most desirable for a used-car-lot sublease (close to the road) — and rent them out at ~$500/month total for people to park cars for sale.
- Rejected/de-prioritized due to liability concerns (unattended cars at a third party's property being broken into/stolen, and the property owner's own liability exposure likely makes them unwilling to sublease regardless of insurance) — the fixed-time "fair" format was preferred because liability is bounded to a specific attended window (e.g., 10am-2pm) rather than 24/7 unattended exposure.

## Idea 3: Exploiting viral/hype cycles for a fast build-and-sell exit (Nikita Bier playbook)
- Case study: Nikita Bier's apps TBH (anonymous compliments app for high schoolers — paywalled the ability to see who complimented you) and later Gas (2022 relaunch of essentially the same mechanic after his Facebook non-compete lapsed).
- TBH: launched hyper-targeted at 3 specific Georgia high schools via a private Instagram account that pre-built a following before launch (friend-requested every student at the target schools, teased a launch date) — sold to Facebook for $100M in 2017.
- Gas (2022 relaunch): launched Oct 2022, hit #1 in the App Store within ~10 days, 1M daily paying active users by Oct 21, $1M in sales by Oct 23, $5M in sales by Dec 1; sold to Discord by Jan 17 (~4 months after launch) for an undisclosed sum, estimated 8-9 figures.
- Key mechanic: geofencing/exclusivity — the app was only visible in the App Store to users within a few minutes of a specific target high school, creating "this app is just for us" exclusivity that fueled organic virality.
- Framed as the same underlying pattern as prior physical-good fads (pet rocks in the 80s, Tamagotchis in the 90s, Pokémon in the 2000s) — a novelty product achieving hyper-fast viral scale, then monetizing/selling before the trend fades, just applied to whatever the current dominant technology/platform is (apps, then possibly AI/chatbots next).
- Idea floated (unbuilt, brainstormed live): an AI chatbot persona built to go viral with a teen/young-adult audience via comedic "roasting" content (not fully fleshed out; hosts note comedic/novelty value alone is hard to monetize/retain versus something with lasting utility, e.g. the TBH paywall-behind-curiosity mechanic).
- Distribution precedent cited: college-cheating-tool Chrome extensions/apps (auto-filling test answers) that used deliberately "leaked"-looking TikTok videos (filmed to look like an accidental classmate recording) as guerrilla marketing — several clones reportedly hit $100K+ MRR quickly purely from this virality-engineered content style.

### Real case study: college "class of [year]" Instagram roommate-finder pages
- A specific low-tech, no-code side hustle recounted via a cold email from a follower (Sam Aldridge): college-bound students follow an Instagram page named after their incoming college class (e.g., "UGA Class of 2025") to find/announce themselves to future classmates and find roommates before ever meeting in person.
- Mechanic: student DMs the page their photo/info to be posted; page charges via Venmo (started at $4, raised to $8 within the first year, later $12) with reportedly little price resistance — essentially a one-time "get featured" fee.
- Scale/economics estimate (per the source's email): one college's page had ~5,000+ posts total, implying ~$30,000-$50,000 in cumulative revenue since inception for that single page; with 1,000-2,000 students posting per admissions cycle at ~$4-8 each, that's roughly $4,000-$16,000 per cycle for "barely any work," and an estimated $500K-$1M aggregate opportunity across all such pages nationally (hundreds of colleges, each with its own class page).
- The operator replicated this exact model for a different school (Purdue) simply by noticing it didn't already have one.
- Eventual disruption: a VC-funded startup, MeetYourClass.com, productized/scaled the same mechanic and reportedly grew to 400,000+ students on a dedicated roommate-finder app — illustrating the "make $100K in a short window before it gets copied/VC-funded" dynamic common to these trend-riding hustles.
- Suggested way to replicate/extend this pattern: pick a list of all US high schools or colleges sorted by enrollment size, or niche down further (e.g., by major, or specific business school), and run the same targeted-Instagram-page playbook — hosts note this category is generally "too small" for VC interest, which is exactly why it can remain a viable solo/side-hustle opportunity longer.

## Caveats / risks
- The car fair idea's three-sided marketplace structure is explicitly called harder to balance than a typical two-sided online marketplace, with real risk of embarrassing supply/demand mismatches at a live, in-person event.
- Legal/liability uncertainty (permitting, city approval, organizer liability for buyer/seller disputes or scams) is flagged as unresolved and a genuine open risk for anyone replicating the car-fair model.
- The land-sublease-at-a-retailer variant was explicitly rejected as too liability-heavy versus the bounded-time "fair" format.
- Trend-riding viral plays (Nikita Bier-style) are explicitly framed as high-reward but a "window in time" opportunity — the college roommate-finder example shows how quickly a bootstrapped niche hustle gets copied and outcompeted once a funded startup notices it.
- Perfume vending machine distributorship update illustrates real early-stage operational risk in a fast-scaling reseller business: currency exposure, broken lead-tracking systems, and no ability to reference existing US customers for buyer confidence, despite strong headline revenue.
