# How to Start a Business? Don't Wait for Permission.
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sYF9lrIGDn4 | Published: 2025-05-22

Chris Koerner speaking to a homeschool class of ~20 high schoolers about his entrepreneurial history and general startup advice/Q&A. Mostly mindset/history, but contains several concrete business examples and specific no-capital-needed tactics, captured below.

- Concrete childhood side-hustle example: at age ~10-11, living across from a golf course, collected golf balls that flew into his yard, washed them, and sold them "3 for a dollar" via a hand-painted plywood sign by the mailbox on a busy road -- made hundreds of dollars over a summer with zero marketing beyond the sign, purely from foot/car traffic.
- Textbook resale business ("Textbook Crook," in college): observed that college bookstores buy back a $200 textbook for as little as $20 after one semester (even unused/uncracked); built a website for students to buy and sell textbooks directly to each other, cutting out the bookstore markup/spread.
- iPhone repair store (2010, his "first real business"): iPhone had launched 3 years earlier and screens were breaking often; started a repair shop with zero repair knowledge (learned to fix each phone on YouTube while the customer waited) and zero capital of his own -- raised $20,000 startup capital from a personal contact who trusted him (after being turned down by his boss and other contacts). Grew this to 4 stores over ~2 years, then sold to a competitor.
- iPhone parts/screen recycling business ("LCD Cycle," started the day after selling the repair stores): paid iPhone repair shops (like his former one) $3 per broken/scrapped screen, then shipped those broken screens to a Chinese manufacturer to be remanufactured (new glass applied) and resold as parts back into the repair-shop supply chain. Idea originated from a cold call by a Chinese company that wanted to buy his own store's broken screens -- he essentially copied that business model himself. Ran for 9 years and grew fast; prompted his relocation from Alabama to Texas for more central shipping logistics.
- Tree trimming business (current, still running): a marketing/lead-generation business, not a hands-on operator business -- "we don't own a chainsaw, we don't own any equipment," they subcontract all actual tree work out to existing tree trimming crews and focus purely on finding/acquiring customers. Explicitly matches his stated business pattern: "marketing heavy, operations light."
- Zero-capital service business template (explained live to the class): pick a simple task (window washing, weed pulling), knock on a neighbor's door, offer a free quote, collect payment up front (example: $250 quoted for window washing), spend a small portion of that (~$50) at Home Depot on supplies needed for that specific job, keep the rest as profit ($200), and every job after the first is pure profit since supplies are already owned. Point: you don't need starting capital for labor/service businesses that require no inventory or expensive equipment.
- Kids' Facebook Marketplace reselling business (family example): he pays his own kids 40% commission to sell unwanted household items on Facebook Marketplace, but they must do the entire process themselves -- take photos, write the listing (can use AI to generate the description), research pricing by checking comparable sold listings, post video first (said to increase engagement/traction), respond quickly to inquiries, and handle the uncomfortable part of meeting a stranger in person to exchange the item for cash.
- Market-validation-before-building tactic (explicitly recommended as his top tip): test real demand via Facebook Marketplace or a simple ad before spending any money building out a business. Concrete example: before buying any equipmentfor the tree trimming business, he posted a Facebook Marketplace ad offering tree trimming services with zero equipment/crew in place -- got inbound requests to quote jobs within 5 minutes, which confirmed real demand before committing capital to gear.
- General framing/principle repeated throughout: "sell it before you have it" -- the single biggest piece of advice given; attempt to get even $1 of revenue from a stranger (not friend/family) before doing "advanced procrastination" tasks like building a website, filing an LLC, writing a business plan, or buying a domain, which create an illusion of productivity without testing actual demand.
- Door-to-door sales as a skill-building recommendation: framed as one of the best ways to develop selling ability because every door is an independent live test (of tone, opening line, body language, etc.) -- with enough repetitions (his own missionary experience involved being rejected an estimated 40,000-50,000 times over 2 years knocking doors in Hungary) you develop a reliable, dialed-in sales approach. Also recommends jobs like waiting tables, mowing lawns, and fast food work (In-N-Out, Chick-fil-A) as good formative jobs for interpersonal/sales skills and saving capital.
- Business partnership guidance: recommends starting solo before taking on a partner. A 50/50 equity split should only be used when both partners are working full-time, side-by-side, in person, with no other jobs/side hustles -- otherwise use an unequal split (90/10, 80/20, investor/operator structure) to reflect unequal contribution/commitment. Look for a partner whose strengths cover your weaknesses (and vice versa) who is equally excited about the specific idea.
- Self-described business-building pattern across his ventures: strong at the "0 to 1" startup/launch phase (marketing, sales, idea generation) and admittedly weak at "1 to 10" operations/scaling (cites diagnosed ADHD, poor attention to detail) -- his strategy is to exit/step back from day-to-day operations of a business as soon as it shows traction, rather than try to personally run it long-term.
- On lawn care specifically (a business he personally tried and abandoned in college, "Clean Cuts Lawn Care"): flags lawn mowing as a low-ticket, price-competitive service (hard to charge a premium, competes against people willing to underprice) -- for similar physical effort/labor, a higher-ticket adjacent home service like tree trimming or landscaping ($3,000/job) is a better business than lawn mowing ($40/job) for the same amount of "out in the sun" work.
- Overall business track record disclosed: has started over 75 businesses in ~15 years, most never generating revenue, some failing/abandoned, some moderately or very successful; currently holds ownership stakes (1%-100%) in ~12 active businesses and employs ~20 people combined across them. One notable failure disclosed: lost a real estate investment (buying mobile home/RV parks) due to partnering with people he "didn't get to know well enough."
- Reading recommendations given to the students: The 4-Hour Workweek (Tim Ferriss), The Three Signs of a Miserable Job (business parable), Shoe Dog by Phil Knight (Nike founder memoir), and the "clean version" of Can't Hurt Me by David Goggins.

Caveats/risks:
- This episode is largely personal history and general mindset advice for teenagers rather than a specific replicable business model with numbers/margins -- unlike most other episodes in this batch, there are no concrete revenue/margin figures for the current businesses discussed (tree trimming, etc.).
- The iPhone repair store origin story explicitly involved learning to fix phones on YouTube in real time while charging customers for the repair -- presented candidly as "not smart" from a competence standpoint, even though it worked out; a real quality/liability risk for anyone replicating this approach literally.
- The mobile home/RV park investment loss is cited as a cautionary example specifically about partnering with people you don't know well -- a due-diligence gap, not a flaw in the business model itself.
- Business partnership advice (50/50 only when both partners are full-time/in-person/all-in) is presented as a strong personal opinion/rule of thumb from experience, not a universal legal or financial principle.
