# Noah Kagan Joins: Side Hustles & Entrepreneurship
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tz61vKhpglA | Published: 2024-05-06

Live call-in Q&A (Episode 12), co-hosted with Noah Kagan (AppSumo co-founder, author of "Million Dollar Weekend"). Chris and Noah take questions from callers running real, named businesses and workshop growth/marketing ideas live.

## Segment: Dental "second opinion" website (My Dental Advocate)
- Caller (a dentist) runs mydentaladvocate.com: patients upload X-rays and a questionnaire, get a professional second opinion on a dental treatment quote within 48 hours for $47.
- Traffic: ~30,000-40,000 monthly pageviews, driven mostly by SEO content (product review blog posts), not by people searching for "online second opinion" specifically -- meaning traffic and intent are mismatched.
- Revenue: Amazon Affiliate income from the product-review content brings in $2,000-3,000/month (more than the core service); the actual paid consult service only converts 2-5 consults/month; hosting/software costs ~$100-200/month; no paid ad spend currently.
- Context: caller notes dental costs can run into the thousands (e.g., a real quote cited: $515 consult + $1,698 root canal, before insurance) -- creating real demand for an independent second opinion.
- Advice from Noah: consider turning the underlying idea into a multi-vertical platform (dental + doctor + other verticals) rather than staying dentist-specific; alternative idea -- drop the free/low-cost question model entirely and charge $100/year for unlimited consult questions, reasoning that free access undervalues the service and a flat annual fee could work well for higher-usage households (e.g., families with multiple kids/annual dental visits).
- Since the affiliate/content side was actually outperforming the core paid service, advice was to double down on what's already working (the content/affiliate revenue) and reduce focus on/possibly drop what isn't converting, rather than force a weak revenue line.
- Distribution ideas: partner directly with dentists for referrals (position as "customer advocate," not a dentist competitor, to keep dentists comfortable referring); use a scraping/outreach tool (named: Outscraper) to find and pitch dentist partnerships, potentially with revenue share; put ROI-focused testimonials (e.g., "paid $47, saved $4,700," ideally with video) prominently at the top of the landing page since video testimonials convert well; consider partnering with dental insurance companies since a good second opinion can reduce their payout costs too.
- Caveat raised: the purchase decision (getting a dental quote) is highly time-sensitive and happens in the moment/in the dentist's office -- most patients won't stop to Google a second-opinion service mid-visit, which limits how much pure SEO traffic converts to the core paid service.

## Segment: Lead generation for a charitable-giving services company selling to wealth advisors (UI Charitable Advisors / donor-advised funds)
- Caller's company: helps independent wealth management firms set up and manage Donor Advised Funds (DAFs) for their high-net-worth clients, competing indirectly with Schwab/Fidelity/Vanguard's DAF offerings (those firms reportedly manage ~$40 billion in DAFs; caller's company manages $350 million with a team of 12 -- described as small but fast-growing).
- Differentiation: unlike big custodians offering only a few mutual funds inside a DAF, this company allows more flexible, complex, in-kind donations (real estate, private business interests, crypto, fine art/collectibles) and lets the client's existing advisor keep managing/investing the DAF assets (and keep charging their normal advisory fee on those assets) rather than losing that relationship to a captive custodian fund.
- Revenue model: asset-based fee starting at 0.6% (60 basis points) on the first $500,000 in a DAF, sliding down to ~10 basis points for larger accounts -- so a $1M DAF generates roughly $6,000/year in gross revenue, and accounts can persist for 5-10+ years, making acquisition economics attractive even with a high-touch sales process.
- Best-fit customer: high-net-worth individuals (roughly $500,000+ in a DAF) where the flexible/creative philanthropic structuring (e.g., interest-free loans to charities, non-cash asset donations) delivers meaningfully more value than a generic custodian DAF; less compelling for smaller accounts.
- Sales dynamics: long sales cycle, highly seasonal (most DAF contributions happen in Q4 as clients finalize annual tax planning with their advisors in Sept-Oct), and existing customer acquisition channels are (1) integration/technology partnerships (hoped to become the biggest channel eventually), (2) industry conference attendance, (3) referrals from existing advisor relationships, (4) cold outreach.
- Advice from Noah: prioritize doing more of whatever is already generating the best customers (mainly referrals) rather than chasing new channels -- specifically, exhaust "referrals of referrals" before trying new tactics.
- Geographic/cultural growth angle: caller's company is based in Provo, Utah (high concentration of religiously/charitably-minded people); Noah suggested leaning into that regional identity for cheap, memorable outreach stunts (referencing Brex's early growth hack of sending champagne bottles to target companies' offices -- suggested sending Diet Coke to prospects as a Utah-flavored, cheaper, funnier equivalent) and "dominating" the Utah market first as a beachhead given the high local density of charitably-active people.
- Other ideas floated: identify and target where the same prospect demographic already congregates for adjacent activities (e.g., charity auctions) as an awareness channel; run a targeted "did you know you could save $X in taxes" outreach campaign to a curated list of likely prospects (Noah's parallel: AppSumo emails potential partners a specific dollar figure, e.g., "can we send you a $100,000 check," to get replies) rather than generic outreach.

## Segment: Mortgage loans for self-employed entrepreneurs (bank-statement loan product)
- Caller sells a mortgage product specifically for self-employed/entrepreneur borrowers, using 12-24 months of bank statement revenue instead of tax-return-reported income to qualify borrowers (addresses the common problem where heavy tax write-offs make self-employed income look artificially low to traditional lenders).
- Pricing: interest rate roughly 1 percentage point higher than a conventional/VA loan; fees comparable to standard loan products.
- Prior customer acquisition experience (different loan type, VA loans): direct mail campaigns.
- Advice: partner with home-service/business "micro-influencers" (e.g., someone with ~10,000 followers in the home-service niche) to refer self-employed customers who need this loan type, potentially via referral/affiliate fees (caller was unsure whether referral fees are legally permitted for mortgage products and needs to check regulations); target existing high-trust networks and communities for self-employed/solopreneur people (named examples: Dynamite Circle, Hampton, EO/YPO-style entrepreneur organizations, and "Ocho"/now "CarryMoney.com," a platform serving self-employed people with Solo 401k/SEP IRA-type retirement products, as a place where the target customer already congregates); run targeted ads on mortgage-comparison sites like LendingTree.com so self-employed users who get rejected/discouraged by standard lenders see a specifically-branded "self-employed mortgage guy" offer.
- Branding advice (from Chris, referencing his own experience): build a personal/brand identity narrowly tied to the specific niche (e.g., "the self-employed mortgage guy") rather than a generic personal name, so prospects extend positive assumed credibility before even reading any content -- parallels Chris's own early growth tactic of branding himself as "the mobile home park guy" on Twitter rather than under his own name, to stand out in an uncrowded niche.

## Segment: Blue-collar worker (oil refinery operator) looking for a low-risk side hustle
- Caller: works a rotating shift schedule as an oil refinery operator, wants to build a side income with an eventual path to replacing his job; has some combination of available cash and "sweat equity" but is unsure what idea to pursue.
- Chris's stated framework: most business ideas can work ("even the pet rock worked") -- the deciding factors are less about picking a clever idea and more about (1) whether the idea is one you'll actually stick with, and (2) how you'll distribute/sell it.
- Caller initially considered general home services (inspired by an uncle's plumbing business) but decided against it: watching that uncle run everything himself (including all bookkeeping, no delegation) while also managing a marriage and kids showed the stress/all-consuming nature of certain home-service businesses, plus licensing/training barriers.
- Caller's actual idea, which he was already excited about: buying a woodworking/engraving machine to make custom wood signs (e.g., decorative "Live Edge" bar signs, Airbnb-style decor signs) from his garage as a side hustle, inspired by a carpenter uncle.
- Advice given: buy the woodworking equipment used (e.g., via Facebook Marketplace) rather than new, both to save money and to de-risk -- used tools resell for close to what you paid, so you can exit cheaply if it doesn't work out; buy a simple phone tripod and film the entire process from day one (even if never posted) because that content archive becomes valuable later for scaling distribution (explicitly: "that's how you take a business from $30,000/year to $3 million/year").
- Suggested initial validation approach: make 5-10 sample pieces, post them on personal social media (not to sell, just to solicit feedback on which designs people like/would pay for) before committing further -- personal networks who give feedback often become first buyers; also explicitly flagged as low-risk since unsold inventory/equipment can be resold.
- Suggested sales channels: Facebook Marketplace, local Facebook groups, Etsy, eBay, Shopify.
- Distribution idea specific to this niche: DM short-term rental (Airbnb) hosts offering free custom decor signs in exchange for a small QR code/branding on the piece directing back to the maker (free marketing for the maker, free decor for the host) -- caution given to create a separate/new Airbnb account for this outreach since Airbnb can ban personal accounts for excessive unsolicited messaging; short-term rental host communities on Reddit and Facebook were also suggested as outreach targets.
- General caveat raised: converting a hobby into a business sometimes causes people to stop enjoying the hobby -- worth being aware of going in, though it doesn't always happen.
