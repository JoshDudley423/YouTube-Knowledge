# Why You're Overthinking Your First Product (Do This Instead)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pMtOUpkRC8w | Published: 2025-04-14

Conversation between Chris Koerner and Sam Thompson (unlimitedcontent.com), riffing on business ideas. Part 2 of a two-part episode.

## Ideas discussed

**"$1 deep research" wrapper**
- Pitch: sell one-off access to AI deep-research tools (e.g., OpenAI's Deep Research) to people who won't pay $200/month for it.
- Mechanism: run it through your own paid account/API (e.g., via Zapier), charge the customer per use (~$1/query, 10,000-word report with sources).
- Model options: pay-per-use reload (charge $5, draw down balance, auto-recharge like OpenAI API credits) vs. subscription.
- Rationale: cites Sam Altman's public suggestion that ChatGPT's $20/mo plan should convert into usable credits toward Deep Research/Operator, since ~95% of ChatGPT users are free, ~5% pay $20/mo, and only a fraction of those pay $200/mo — implies a large underserved middle market.
- Value-add for founder: reading user prompts at scale reveals what people actually want (research/product ideas), though privacy rules may restrict this.
- Bonus angle: pair with existing "digital product" funnels (e.g., sell a sourdough recipe book, then upsell "your AI assistant for this exact topic," which is really just an unbranded ChatGPT wrapper).

**Simple/cheap "super assistant" ChatGPT wrapper (no niche)**
- Idea: instead of narrow single-use-case AI wrapper apps, launch a generic, dead-simple, mobile-native ChatGPT interface aimed at casual/non-technical users who find $20/month unjustified for light use.
- Charge ~$9-10/month (about half of ChatGPT Plus).
- No guardrails — let users ask literally anything (cooking, car PSI, sports trivia, etc.); this is intentional, most users will self-select into one main use case anyway.
- Distribution: same paid-ad playbook as niche "guide" apps — run separate downloadable app listings/ad creatives per persona (e.g., "best personal training AI," "life assistant AI") that all funnel to the same underlying generic assistant, just re-contextualized in marketing.
- Reference comp: AIPRM, a ChatGPT plugin that helps with prompting — cited as an example of a simple value-add layer on top of ChatGPT.

**Apparel / merch brand with a resonant slogan**
- Framework: brand = "resonance marketing" — people buy because they identify with a message, not because of product features. Hats/shirts are the easiest medium for this.
- Case study: "God Is Dope" apparel brand (Atlanta) — one T-shirt design, given away free/sold at cost ($5), went organically viral, hit an estimated $30-40M/year in revenue by year 2-3. Attributed success to 4 factors: affordable, good design, polarizing/resonant message, mass-market appeal, plus strong distribution/marketing follow-through.
- Other cited comps: Dad Gang (8-figure hat brand built on viral "dad pride" messaging), Rough Shot Supply ("these are the good old days" hats — narrowed a catalog of ~8 hat designs down to one winning design that became ~93% of sales, illustrating an extreme Pareto/power-law split, "95/5 not 80/20").
- Concrete test process: design 8+ slogan/logo variants, run $100 in Facebook ads behind each, see which gets clicks/conversions, kill the other 7-9 regardless of any stray sale; double down on the winner.
- Execution tools: Canva for design, PodPartner for print-on-demand (cited as cheaper/better quality than Printful/Printify).
- Two-tier product idea floated: same slogan on a $25 shirt (slogan only) vs a $15 shirt (slogan + ".com" appended, cheaper because it doubles as brand advertising) — panelists were split on whether adding the .com actually helps; also proposed adding a QR code to the shirt that could double as an affiliate-tracking link for the wearer.
- Facebook ads guidance: don't over-target — use "Advantage+"/broad targeting and let the ad creative itself do the targeting (people self-select by whether the creative resonates); run broad, US-only, ~$100/day.

**Never Touch Plastic marketplace (dead idea, cited as example of what NOT to do)**
- Concept: niche marketplace (Etsy-style, via a marketplace-as-a-service tool like Sharetribe) for products/materials that have "never touched plastic," targeting a small but growing anti-microplastics audience.
- Explicit caveat from Koerner: marketplaces are one of the hardest business models to start — "the biggest boulder to get rolling" — even though a winning marketplace can be excellent once it hits critical mass. He personally avoids revenue models based on taking a % of GMV; prefers subscription-for-marketplace-access models instead.
- Fallback idea using the same domain: skip the marketplace, just sell a t-shirt or drop-shipped glass Tupperware branded "never touch plastic."

## General principles repeated in this episode
- "If your gut reaction is that a market is oversaturated, that's the market you need to go to — that's the big market." Oversaturation signals real demand.
- People underestimate willingness to pay; "people will pay for anything — the question is are you good enough to get it in front of their face."
- Advice for first-time entrepreneurs: stop planning/ideating in your head; spend $100 on ads and let real click/conversion data guide the next move rather than cycling through hypothetical objections.
- Suggested "big four" evergreen ad/content categories with built-in demand: money, health/weight loss, food, relationships/sex.
- A pizza-shop example: when evaluating an existing small business for sale (or margin opportunity), a fast/cheap validation test is running geo-targeted Facebook ads (e.g., "free pizza, give us your number") to gauge cost-per-lead before committing capital.

## Caveats
- "$19 digital product via Facebook ads" is explicitly framed as a *starting point* for learning ad/funnel skills, not necessarily the end-state business.
- Marketplace models: hard to start, avoid % of GMV revenue share per Koerner's stated preference.
- No hard revenue/cost numbers given for the AI wrapper ideas — they're speculative/riffed, not validated case studies (unlike the God Is Dope example, which is real).
