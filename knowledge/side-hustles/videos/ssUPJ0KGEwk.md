# How to Print Money With Google Sheets + AI
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ssUPJ0KGEwk | Published: 2024-11-09

HoldCo Bros brainstorm episode (Chris Koerner + Nik). Three ideas discussed and rated "marry/date/kill."

## Idea 1 (rated "marry" by both hosts): AI-powered data cleaning/enrichment via Google Sheets
- Tool: **"GPT for Sheets"** — a Google Workspace Marketplace add-on (by Talarian) that lets you write `=GPT("<prompt>", <cell>)` formulas directly in a spreadsheet, dragged down a column like any formula.
- Cost: described as "dirt cheap"/"a fraction of a fraction of a penny per row" for simple prompts; cost scales up with longer prompts/outputs across thousands of rows. Recommends getting your own OpenAI API key ($20/month ChatGPT account gets you API access) and plugging it into the add-on to control/reduce cost rather than using the add-on's default billing.
- Use case A — lead/newsletter data cleanup: standardize messy free-text form submissions (inconsistent capitalization, gibberish, people typing a question instead of their name) into clean first names in bulk; prompt logic: normalize capitalization, leave blank if no name detected, handle "all caps" and gibberish inputs, drop the value if the AI can't confidently identify a name.
- Use case B — inferring names from email addresses: prompt the model to guess a person's first name from just their email address (e.g., "smithjohn@gmail.com" → John), only outputting if it can do so with reasonable (~70%) confidence, else leave blank. Named alternative/competing paid tools for this specific enrichment task: **Norbert** (and similar competitors), which cross-reference LinkedIn and other sources to append names to emails, reportedly accurate roughly 30-50% of the time.
- Why it matters: personalizing outbound email (first name instead of generic "partner"/"howdy") reportedly improves deliverability signals to email carriers because it makes each message appear unique/non-bulk. On their signup form, requiring a first name field outright cost them an estimated 20-30% of subscribers, hence backfilling names post-hoc via AI instead of gating signup on it.
- Use case C — qualitative screening/scoring at scale: applied to a large batch of ~30,000 "co-founder applications" (long-form typeform submissions) where the team wanted to score applicants on entrepreneurial signal (e.g., "did they actually start something, even a lemonade stand") while explicitly zero-weighting GPA, school prestige, and graduation date — prompted as a custom scoring rubric run down the whole dataset (score 1-10).
- Use case D — competitive/market research: use a web-scraping tool like **Outscraper** to pull all 1-star (or 5-star) Google/Yelp-style reviews for a niche of local businesses (example given: nail salons), dump into Sheets, then use GPT for Sheets to extract the most common complaint/praise words across all reviews — used to identify unmet needs (e.g., a repeated complaint) that could seed a new product or service pitch to that niche.
- Proposed monetization path for a data-cleaning service business built on this: start by cleaning a prospect's messy data for free, then explicitly ask "what would you pay for this," and keep raising your price for new customers until you hit real pushback (the "Brandon Doyle method" of pricing).

## Idea 2 (rated "date"/"kill" — unbuilt, speculative): social-proof-driven local dating events
- Problem framed: modern online dating (Tinder/Bumble/Hinge-style apps) lacks the "social proof" layer that used to come from mutual friends vouching for a match (unlike LinkedIn recommendations). Online dating industry cited at ~$9B/year revenue, projected to reach ~$13B by 2031 (~7% CAGR).
- Concept: build a local, niche-specific (singles-focused) event or meetup business that uses dating apps only as a funnel into in-person group activities, rather than 1:1 dates — rationale being group settings are lower-pressure and let people self-select before committing to one-on-one time.
- Concrete format floated: rent the back room of a restaurant/bar for a private party (30-50 people); admission is gated — attendees must be on a first date with someone they matched with on a dating app (never met in person before) and pay a cover charge (mentioned $20-40/door) that also includes food/drinks provided by the venue (so the operator doesn't have to run a kitchen or bar). Gamify with activities like speed dating, improv, or karaoke; could send an attendee list/profiles in advance so people can pre-identify who they want to meet (similar to a curated/exclusive guest-list party model, referencing Nick Gray-style event curation).
- Adjacent unbuilt growth-hack anecdote (not something the hosts built, cited as an example of aggressive tactics in this space): a bar/nightclub owner allegedly created a fake attractive-woman dating profile and set up dozens/hundreds of "dates" with men at his venue as a customer-acquisition stunt — drove real foot traffic even though the men got "ghosted."
- Generalization: this same "gate an in-person meetup behind a niche/shared context" model could apply to any niche audience, not just dating (e.g., SMB owners, disc golf players).
- Verdict: both hosts rated this idea "date" (interesting, not their top pick) or lower — flagged as logistically hard to spin up and admittedly outside their own experience (neither has run a dating product).

## Idea 3 (rated "kill" by both, but seen as a fun low-stakes side project, not a serious income idea): "treasure hunting" resale content channel — "Water Finds"
- Inspiration/comp: a social-media creator known as "the Dumpster Dive King" (10M+ followers) posts GoPro dumpster-diving videos with a "treasure hunt" payoff structure (viewers wait to see what he finds); he resells found items on his own site, **lostgoods.com** (described as ugly but a smart brand name), though most of his actual sales are estimated to flow through eBay/Facebook Marketplace/Etsy rather than his own site (his own site framed as only ~5-10% of sales). Comparable creator niche cited: people building content businesses around finding and reselling lost golf balls.
- The pitch: a similar model built around wading through creeks/ponds near disc golf courses to find lost discs (some worth $10-30, occasionally "hundreds of dollars"), then filming the process for social content and reselling found discs online. Mentions a commercial "metal/plastic detector" style device that beeps near plastic to make finding discs easier/faster, as a possible tool to source or even resell.
- Monetization layers proposed: (1) content/ad revenue from the social channel itself, (2) reselling found items via a branded storefront, (3) potentially selling the plastic-detection tool itself.
- Speaker had already tried starting this: bought domain **waterfinds.com**, never launched it, let the domain lapse; it's now listed as a "premium domain" for ~$10,000 (speaker considers that price inflated/not worth it).
- Speaker's own assessment: not a $10M business, but could plausibly be a "seven-figure business" even without a social following, though he never executed it.

## Caveats
- Idea 2 (dating events) and idea 3 (treasure-hunt reselling) are explicitly untested — no revenue, user, or launch data exists for either; both hosts flag them as fun/interesting but not validated businesses.
- Both hosts note dating-events idea is risky/uncertain territory neither has direct experience in ("we could sound like idiots right now").
- GPT for Sheets costs can scale up meaningfully with longer prompts/outputs run across large datasets — cost isn't literally free at scale, and using your own OpenAI API key materially reduces cost versus the add-on's default.
