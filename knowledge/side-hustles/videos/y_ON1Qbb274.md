# He Turned $400 Into $2.5M Using AI (No Coding)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y_ON1Qbb274 | Published: 2026-03-29

Interview with Jon Cheney, founder of the General AI Proficiency Institute (Gen AIPI) — an AI implementation/consulting business for small-to-midsize companies, built via "vibe coding" (building software by describing it to AI, no traditional coding) with no coding background.

## The business / idea
- Core offering: AI consulting/implementation for businesses that don't have a CTO or in-house AI expertise — helping them adopt AI tools and automate manual processes.
- Sells via a 3-part "STE framework" on sales calls: Strategy (big-picture: how AI will affect their industry/competitors, positioning the CEO to stay ahead), Transformation (identify specific hires/tasks they were about to pay for and show how AI can do it for a fraction of the cost, tying cost directly to ROI), Education/Training (train staff, from CEO to secretary, to think and work differently with AI — framed as unlocking 10-20% more productivity from existing headcount).
- Target customer: businesses roughly $10M-$30M+ annual revenue (his stated floor — under $10M is "hard to sell to... can't get enough bang for your buck"); average customer revenue ~$30M. Early customers were "messy middle" businesses without a CTO: painting company, pool construction company, masonry company, plus larger names (Tony Robbins, Dean Graziosi organizations).
- Business model evolved from one-time engagements to recurring revenue: started as a one-time $15,000 in-person "assessment + training" package, evolved into a "fractional Chief AI Officer" retainer ($15,000-$25,000+/month), then further evolved into an ongoing systems-installation-and-management model (installing and maintaining AI systems for marketing, ops, etc.) to avoid being easily replaceable as a mere consultant.
- Pricing evolved: initially by headcount/hours (e.g., $10,000/month = 12-15 hours of work; $25,000/month = 50-60 hours), later shifted to a system-based/value-based pricing model tied to ongoing management of installed AI systems.

## Origin story and numbers
- Built the MVP (an "AI IQ test"/assessment site with upsells to paid AI courses/certification) in ~3 days (~35 hours, 10-12 hrs/day) for about $400 total, including domain, email, Stripe setup, and AI credits — using Replit (a "vibe coding" tool) after seeing a LinkedIn post about it.
- Contrast: he'd gotten a $105,000 quote from a Ukraine-based dev shop to build an unrelated app (a travel social network) with traditional coding; dragging that same proposal into Replit produced a working build in ~20 minutes, which is what led him to pivot into "AI implementation as a business" rather than build the travel app.
- Launched the IQ-test site late one weekend; got ~150 email signups but zero purchases — root cause was a broken Stripe integration (fixed in 5 minutes), but he didn't wait to test the direct-to-consumer funnel further.
- Pivoted immediately to cold outreach to businesses instead of consumer funnel: messaged people he loosely knew who ran companies; after about 6 messages, one (with a few hundred employees) agreed to a call and bought a $15,000 assessment-and-training package on the spot.
- Within the next ~6 weeks, reached ~$180,000 in sales this way.
- Revenue trajectory: ~$1M by 6 months in, solo (no employees — just AI tools as his "team": Replit as developer, ChatGPT as project manager). First $1M was near 100% profit (only real cost was his time; got free Replit credits after public exposure — the Replit CEO mentioned him on the Joe Rogan podcast, leading to referral clients like Tony Robbins/Dean Graziosi).
- Year-one total: $2.5M revenue, 50%+ net profit (over $1M net), with 5 full-time employees by the end of year one; projecting ~15 employees and ~$7-8M revenue (annual recurring) by end of year two.
- He states the business would likely command a 6-7x revenue multiple if sold (vs. 10-20x for a "pure" SaaS), implying a rough $15M valuation on $2.5M revenue.
- Cash-flow positive from day 5 of operation.
- Comparison point: he made almost as much in this business in 1 year as he did over 7-8 years running a prior venture-funded company that raised $13M — with none of the investor obligation/pressure of VC funding.

## Tools named
- Replit (vibe-coding/app-building platform — his primary dev tool)
- ChatGPT and Grok (used as "team members": project management, cross-checking each other's outputs since single-model hallucination was a bigger issue at the time)
- Stripe (payments)
- Slack, integrated with "OpenClaw" (internal name "Jarvis") — an AI agent that logs team activity/updates into their custom-built CRM/client dashboard automatically
- Zapier — pitched in this video (sponsor segment) as now supporting full AI agents (plain-English task instructions, browsing, reading/writing across 8,000+ apps like Google Sheets/Notion/HubSpot, built-in guardrails) — used as an example of low-cost automation (e.g., a lead-qualification/enrichment/Slack-notification agent) that can replace tasks otherwise done by a hired employee
- Manus — recommended over ChatGPT for deeper prospecting research (e.g., "find me 20 CEOs of X-type businesses in this area with phone numbers")
- Custom-built internal CRM and client dashboards (self-built rather than off-the-shelf Salesforce/HubSpot)

## Getting first customers (his explicit advice)
- No shortcut: you have to reach out and talk to people directly (cold messaging/calling).
- Use an AI research tool (he suggests Manus) to generate a prospect list (name, company, phone number) for a target niche/geography, then call them.
- Post publicly and often about what you're building/doing on personal social media (Facebook, LinkedIn) even with a small following — he frames a 300-person friend network as containing ~30 business owners (assuming ~10% of adults are business owners) who will see you positioned as more AI-savvy than they are, which is a low bar since most people barely use ChatGPT.
- Non-work social posts (e.g., about hobbies like kayaking, piano) generate connection and inbound interest too — people connect on a shared interest first, then discover the business angle.
- Referral-sourced calls close much faster/easier than cold calls (prospects are pre-sold); he recommends going for the close quickly in those cases rather than laboring through the full STE pitch.
- Local/personal connection matters even for a fundamentally remote business — feeling "connected" (same hometown, shared hobby, referral) drives conversion more than geographic proximity itself.

## Sales tactics (STE framework in detail)
- Strategy: paint a picture of how AI will reshape their industry and competitive position; position it as a CEO-level concern ("if I miss the boat, it's my fault").
- Transformation: ask what roles they've been wanting to hire for or what they wish they could do better; reframe the cost of a new $8,000/month hire against a comparably capable automated system (illustrative example given: $25/month to run something that replaces an $8,000/month hire), which anchors the ROI before ever quoting a price.
- Education/Training: pitch additional productivity gains (e.g., "what if your existing 60 employees were 20% more productive / your revenue grew 10%?") to expand the deal's perceived value beyond the initial automation, making a $15,000+/month retainer feel like a "no-brainer" relative to a $2-3M annual impact framed against a $20-30M business.
- Deliberately drops a little technical detail/jargon mid-call (not too much) to signal competence ("we can go into Replit, API that in, build a dashboard") without going so deep it invites do-it-yourself objections or loses the prospect.
- On discovery calls: ask "are you using AI right now?" — almost everyone says no or "just using ChatGPT like Google," which sets up the pitch that merely being on the call already puts them ahead of peers.

## Philosophy on scaling / avoiding a services trap
- Explicit warning: don't turn this into "being a dev shop" if you're not a developer/architect — building and then indefinitely maintaining custom code for clients creates a support burden and commoditizes you as a replaceable contractor.
- Better model: teach/enable client staff to build their own AI solutions ("teach a man to fish") rather than doing everything for them — his best customers are internal staff (e.g., director of operations) who vibe-code their own tools with his coaching. This keeps him valuable as a continuing guide (always something new to teach as AI advances) rather than a one-off deliverable, and clients "like you better" and retain you longer when you've enabled their own people's growth.
- In live training sessions, start with something fun/low-stakes (e.g., vibe-coding a simple game like Flappy Bird) to build confidence and engagement before tackling real business problems — mirrors teaching an instrument by starting with a favorite simple song.
- Anecdote: after his course, one attendee's adult son used AI to "automate himself out of his own desk job" in ~2 days, disclosed it to his boss instead of hiding it, and was immediately promoted to teach AI to the rest of the company — used as an illustration that becoming the in-house "AI guru" fast is achievable and career-advancing rather than risky.

## Caveats / risk framing
- He explicitly counter-advises against chasing a bigger, venture-backable idea over a proven, self-funded cash-flowing business: shared an email he sent someone else estimating only a 1-6% chance a flashier VC-backed idea produces "life-changing" money, versus 95% odds an already-working five-figure/month business keeps producing steady income — and noted that even a successful "big exit" can net founders little to nothing after investor liquidation preferences (e.g., raise $13M, sell for $12M, founders get $0).
- Self-funded/bootstrapped approach let him retain full control (no investor pressure to "keep working," could take vacation whenever) versus his prior VC-backed company experience.
- Early product-market mismatch risk: his original direct-to-consumer funnel (AI "IQ test" + paid courses) got traffic but zero purchases due to a technical (Stripe) bug he didn't immediately catch — a reminder to fully test purchase funnels before assuming a lack of demand.
- Selling AI-driven automation to replace planned hires only works because most target businesses (sub-$30M revenue, no CTO) are unaware how far AI tooling has advanced — the pitch leans on this information gap ("stop assuming other people know more about AI than they actually do").
