# 5 Genius AI Business Ideas
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hZg4TgwX_64 | Published: 2025-02-01

This is part 2 of an interview with guest James Camp. Despite the title, this segment covers a gambling-addiction rehab business concept and general business-building philosophy/tactics rather than AI ideas specifically.

## Sports-betting/gambling addiction rehab business (guest James Camp's idea)
- Premise: sports betting is rapidly expanding (state-by-state legalization, expected eventual federal legalization) and gambling addiction, especially among young men, is rising — comparable to how COVID pushed "degenerate gambler" behavior into stock trading (Robinhood) and crypto.
- Tier 1 (low-end/recurring revenue): paid online or in-person communities/newsletters for people getting clean from gambling addiction, priced at $50-$200/month.
- Tier 2 (high-end): a "high-end passages rehab" (in-person rehab facility) targeting gambling addiction specifically.
- Tier 3 (the "weird"/esoteric idea, unbuilt, explicitly caveated): casinos are required by their licensing terms (cited example: Las Vegas) to allocate a portion of revenue to gambling-addiction treatment/subsidy funds each year. The proposed model: run marketing/ad funnels (similar to asynchronous telemedicine funnels like Hims/Hers/Roman for ED or hair loss) where a prospective gambling-addiction patient fills out an online questionnaire; a licensed addiction specialist reviews it asynchronously (no live doctor conversation) and approves treatment; if approved, treatment (cited example cost: $33,000) is subsidized/paid for by casino-mandated addiction funds rather than the patient. The funnel operator/marketer would earn a margin (cited estimate: ~$3,000/customer) while spending only ~$100 to acquire each customer via ads.
- Ethical caveat raised explicitly by the guest himself: this model's incentives could mirror the private prison system — if you profit from acquiring "patients" via a subsidized flywheel, you may be more incentivized to perpetuate the underlying problem (gambling access/addiction) than to actually cure it, especially since the same casino industry funding the "cure" is also lobbying to expand legalized gambling. No solution proposed, just flagged as a real structural risk.
- No cost/revenue validation given for the funnel-based model — it's described as a hypothetical/aspirational idea, not something built or tested.

## General business philosophy and cold-outreach tactics (from guest James Camp's background)
- Personal claim: sold $88,000 at a single bottle-service table in one night while working as a nightclub promoter (used as an anecdote about hustle, not a repeatable business model).
- Claim about market saturation: "saturation is a myth" for the $1-2M/year revenue tier — asserts virtually any niche business/industry can reach $1-2M/year given 8 billion people worldwide (cited example: a course teaching handstands reportedly earning $70,000/month). Reaching $100M+ is described as genuinely hard and luck-dependent, but $1-2M is framed as a "formula," not luck.
- Advice on copying business models: rather than "copy but differentiate," his stronger claim is to copy an existing, already-validated business model as closely as possible rather than trying to improve on it — the risk of "improving" it is you might make it worse; ask "has literally everyone on Earth already heard of this" before assuming a market is saturated.
- "Midwit curve" framing applied to business execution: both very unsophisticated ("caveman") and very sophisticated ("Jedi") operators tend to just copy a working model directly and execute; it's the middle ("midwit") group that overthinks, insists on differentiating, and gets stuck rationalizing why something won't work.
- Concrete example of a simple, replicable local business cited: a Dallas woman's "porch pumpkin" decorating business — charges $500-$2,500 per install (with pumpkins running an additional ~$1,500), described as a ~$1M/year business with ~70% margins built on roughly 1,500 customers across the Houston/Dallas metro area (a market of ~17M people) — used to argue you need only a tiny fraction of a large local market to hit seven figures, and that public mockery of a viral idea ("this is so stupid, this will never work") doesn't mean it doesn't work.
- Cold outreach specifics (from the guest's own past business selling $100,000-$200,000 investor-relations/ad packages to cannabis-industry public companies): approach was manually researching each public company's IR contact, building a custom-branded deck (pre-Canva era) for each prospect, cold-emailing it, following up, and meeting prospects in person at industry conferences to build rapport — sales cycles ran ~6 months, much longer than a typical "one-call close" expectation.
- Explicit claim: cold email and cold calling do work when done as low-volume, individually customized outreach (e.g., ~20 genuinely tailored emails/day) rather than mass-blasted spam (e.g., 50,000 emails/month) — the latter creates deliverability/spam problems the former avoids.
- Networking/relationship tactic for cutting through inbound noise: if everyone is pitching a given service (e.g., short-form video editing) via Instagram/Twitter DM, don't compete in that same crowded channel — find people who already value the service but aren't being pitched it constantly, and/or reach them through a different channel (cold email instead of DM, or a platform like Stir), and consider low-cost pattern-interrupts (e.g., sending a $0.01-$1 PayPal payment with a note attached) to stand out.
- General principle repeated: "the harder way is usually the better way" — doing unscalable, high-effort things (in-person meetings, hand-built sales collateral, direct outreach) up front outperforms optimizing for scale/convenience too early; worry about scaling a business only once you have more demand than you can serve, not before you have any clients.
- Mindset/psychology point (not a tactic, but stated as foundational): belief that a goal is achievable is treated as a practical precondition for pursuing it seriously — used to explain why the same "obvious" tactics (courses, cold outreach, execution) succeed for people who fully commit ("just do it") and fail for those who intellectually agree but don't act.

## Caveats/limits
- No concrete numbers, launch costs, or step-by-step plan are given for the gambling-rehab business idea itself — it remains a speculative concept, explicitly flagged by the guest as ethically fraught (perverse incentives) and operationally complex (would require navigating medical licensing, insurance/casino subsidy relationships, and regulatory approval for an asynchronous telehealth-style funnel).
