# Launch a $50k Monthly Software Biz with No Code
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mYdUk5tM1PM | Published: 2024-11-23

Brainstorming session (Chris Koerner + Nik, "HoldCo Bros" series, part 2) covering two business concepts built around website-change-monitoring tools and a tax-strategy service for entrepreneurs, ending with a "marry/date/kill" evaluation.

## Idea 1: Website-change-monitoring SaaS (built on Distill.io)
- Core tool referenced: distill.io — a service that notifies you when content on a specific webpage changes. Costs roughly $10/month (per-business use) or can be a cheap consumer plan.
- General framework: find any business/use case where being notified of a webpage change *first* creates value or an "arbitrage" opportunity, then wrap that monitoring into a simple, easy-to-use SaaS product for that specific audience.
- Consumer-facing spin-off idea: a ticket-price-drop notification tool built on top of Distill (e.g., monitoring sports ticket prices), positioned like "Scott's Cheap Flights" (~$70/year) — a low-cost "insurance policy" subscription that pays for itself if it catches even one big price drop. Suggested pricing as low as ~$10/year for a stripped-down version.
- Other monitoring use cases discussed (mostly as untested brainstorm, not validated businesses):
  - E-commerce back-in-stock alerts for niche Shopify stores that don't already offer this feature natively (most stores restock quietly with no fast customer notification).
  - Financial/trading edge: monitor company investor-relations pages for the moment a 10-K/10-Q filing is published, to get first-mover advantage reading the disclosure before others.
  - M&A/deal-sourcing: monitor target acquisition companies' websites for changes in pricing, leadership team, or address as an early signal something is happening at the company.
  - Distill's own use-case list mentioned as inspiration: legislative/bill tracking, budget/grant tracking, medical/health job postings, patent/legal tracking, real estate zoning-law changes (e.g., a small SaaS tracking zoning law changes specifically in one county), competitive/investigative monitoring, cybersecurity change detection.
  - Competitive conversion-rate research: monitor 10-20 competitors' purchase/checkout pages for design or copy changes over time — since companies rarely change high-leverage sales pages unless data shows it's working, watching what changes get made across many competitors reveals what's converting better industry-wide.
- **Most-favored concrete application (this is the idea both hosts ultimately liked best): car dealership sales-tracking reports.**
  - Insight: car dealerships have large, flexible marketing budgets and are known to spend heavily/experimentally on any tool that might give them an edge (validated by one host's friend who sells software into that industry).
  - Pitch: use Distill (~$10/month cost to you) to track how many of a specific car model/trim each nearby competitor dealership sold, and deliver the dealership owner a **daily report of every competitor's sales within a 30-mile radius** — actionable for real-time pricing decisions (e.g., mark up a model that's sold out everywhere nearby, discount a model that's oversupplied in the local market).
  - Go-to-market/distribution tactic: build and start sending the reports *before* getting a signed agreement or payment — install the tracking (~10 minutes of setup) and email dealerships the free daily reports for 30 days unsolicited, then have a pricing conversation once they've seen the value and are hooked on reading them daily.
  - Real estate suggested as an analogous vertical (tracking local home sales) but flagged as a harder sell — real estate lacks the same dedicated, flexible marketing-budget line item that car dealerships have ("more stodgy" buyers).
- Related tactical technique (unprompted competitive-research method, described via a past personal example): reverse-engineer which asset type is most in-demand on a rental marketplace (used with RVshare) by searching current availability for "this weekend" vs. "far in the future" across categories (Class A/B/C RVs) — a category with similar total listings but far higher booking/rental rates relative to availability indicates the best type of unit to buy for a rental business. Same technique applies to Airbnb property types. The host used this research to correctly predict (per a candid conversation with an RVshare employee) that Class C RVs were the most successful category to buy for rental income.
- General idea-sourcing framework emphasized: when you find an interesting tool/platform, cold-email the company directly and ask how customers are creatively using it or making money with it — can surface unexpected business angles.

## Idea 2: "Tax Sherpa" — dedicated tax-strategy advisor for entrepreneurs (aka "silver tsunami"/CPA concept)
- Premise: CPAs typically only interact with entrepreneurs once or twice a year around filing deadlines and mostly handle bookkeeping/filing/compliance — proactive tax *strategy* optimization is usually neglected. There's room for a role focused purely on maximizing legal tax strategies for entrepreneurs, distinct from bookkeeping, filing, or fractional CFO work.
- Concrete tax-strategy levers this role would advise on (examples given in the episode):
  - Optimizing entrepreneur salary levels to satisfy IRS reasonable-compensation rules without over-paying into taxable income.
  - 401(k) contribution strategy for business owners: individual contribution limits up to $23,000/year cited in the transcript (numbers may reflect the filming year), plus employer match; if a spouse is also employed in the business, paying them a salary lets the household double the sheltered/matched contribution amount (example given: getting to roughly $46,000-$92,000 moved into tax-deferred savings for a two-income household in the business).
  - IRA contributions (separate from 401k, independent of business, contribution limit cited around $7,500/year).
  - Paying children through the business for actual work performed (legal under child labor rules when structured correctly) and having them also contribute to retirement accounts.
  - Maximizing legitimate write-offs: vehicle expenses, a portion of mortgage interest, phone/internet, meals, and correctly depreciating real estate — each governed by specific rules.
- Positioning: this role sits between the entrepreneur and their existing CPA — acting as the client's advocate/analyst who proactively surfaces deductions and strategies, while the CPA retains final sign-off/filing authority. Explicit advice: "stay in your lane" — don't do bookkeeping or actual tax filing, just strategy/advocacy, since there's already plenty of margin in that narrower lane.
- Suggested tooling: don't assume you need to be a CPA yourself — use ChatGPT plus publicly available tax information to identify applicable strategies and points of leverage; positioned as accessible to build without formal credentials (though the value is still real — potentially saving clients tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars per year).
- Suggested pricing model: flat fee (e.g., $10,000) or contingency-based (e.g., 25-30% of whatever tax savings you identify) — framed as easily measurable against last year's tax return.
- Distribution/go-to-market ideas discussed:
  - Partner directly with existing tax/bookkeeping firms to get referred into their client base, rather than trying to acquire entrepreneur clients cold.
  - Build a personal brand as "the Twitter/Instagram/YouTube tax guy" publishing tax-strategy content for entrepreneurs (acknowledged some accounts like this already exist, but there's room to lean further into the specific "not-yet-rich-enough-for-a-staff-CPA" niche).
  - Revenue-share the referral relationship with the CPA (e.g., give the referring CPA a cut, such as 10% of savings, out of your own take) to incentivize them to introduce you to clients.
- Key caveat/risk raised: fiduciary CPAs may be uncomfortable receiving referral kickbacks without disclosure to the client. Proposed structural fix: position the tax-strategy service as a formal "advisory" arm the CPA firm offers (not a hidden kickback) — client payment still flows through the CPA/agency, CPA retains final approval over deductions, and the CPA's compensation for referring/overseeing is transparent and built into the offering rather than a backroom incentive. This keeps the CPA comfortable putting their name behind recommendations.

## Host evaluation ("marry, date, kill" of the three ideas discussed, including a "Reddit growth hack" and "business in a box" idea from earlier in the episode not detailed in this transcript)
- Both hosts independently "married" the car-dealership Distill.io tracking-report idea specifically (not the broader website-monitoring concept) — seen as the most concrete, fundable, and immediately actionable idea of the episode.
- Both "dated" (liked, saw potential in, but wouldn't commit to) the Reddit-growth-hack idea, viewed as underused: more business owners/capitalists "should own their own subreddit" to build and monetize community.
- Both "killed" the generic "business in a box" idea as too abstract to execute against.
- One host explicitly stated he personally wants to go build the car-dealership sales-tracking idea and invited viewers to do so and report back results.
