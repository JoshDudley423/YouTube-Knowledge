# I Found a Perfect Business That Anyone Can Start
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aUacHsbJzXQ | Published: 2025-01-16

- Business idea: sell firewood sourced essentially free from tree trimming companies, selling year-round via Facebook Marketplace with no website needed. Host frames it as a near-100% gross-margin service business with very low startup cost.
- Sourcing wood for free: tree trimming companies normally pay a dump/landfill fee to dispose of cut wood (oak, pine, ash, etc.) — pitch them: "you're paying the dump to take this, give it to me for free instead and I'll sell it as firewood." Framed as a win-win with zero cost to the tree company.
- Alternate free sourcing method (for seasonal/off-cycle sourcing when tree crews aren't cutting): check your municipality's bulk trash pickup schedule (often one or two set days/month) and drive around the night before/morning of to collect large oak logs (need at least ~10 inches diameter to be useful; thin branches aren't worth it) left curbside for pickup.
- Startup costs / structure demonstrated: host funded a log splitter (~$1,600) and provided land (his own 3 acres, no rent charged) for two teenage operators (do the manual labor: cutting/splitting/stacking) while he sourced customers and mentored — a repeatable "you supply capital + customer acquisition, they supply labor" partnership structure for minors/first-time entrepreneurs.
- Fallback/downside protection: if firewood doesn't sell, the log splitter itself can be rented out to tree trimming companies, homeowners, or land clearers for $70–100/day — described as a way to keep the business "low floor, high ceiling."
- Firewood measurement and pricing (North Texas figures given): a "cord" = 4ft x 4ft x 8ft (explain to customers as roughly "two truck beds" since most don't know cord dimensions). Full cord sells for $450–500; half cord for $250–300 (smaller units command higher per-stick pricing). A single large tree (e.g. one American elm, cut to spec) yields roughly 250–350 "sticks" ≈ half a cord ≈ $250–300 of product from one tree.
- Wood prep specifics: logs must be cut to ~16 inches long (14–18" acceptable) for stacking to fit cord dimensions consistently — tree crews need to be explicitly trained to cut to this length, since they normally cut based on what's easy to carry, not firewood dimensions. Wood is stacked 3–4 sticks at a time in alternating directions (helps airflow/drying, prevents mold in wet conditions). Using pallets (48"x40") as a stacking base helps hit accurate cord dimensions.
- Seasoning (drying) wood: natural seasoning takes 3–12 months (faster in hot climates); can accelerate with portable kilns attached to shipping containers (~$20,000–25,000) or fixed kilns (can cost hundreds of thousands but season wood in as little as a week) — described as the natural progression path as a firewood business scales from backyard to industrial.
- Market research method before launching (repeatable for any Facebook-Marketplace-dependent local business): pick your target city plus 2 comparable "benchmark" cities of similar size/demographics; search the product category (e.g. "firewood [city]") on Facebook Marketplace with a tight radius, count only listings with the city in the title (ignore irrelevant/out-of-area results), and divide population by listing count to get a "competitors per capita" ratio. In this case: Plano (290k people) = 14 listings (1 per 20,700 people); Frisco (225k) = 21 listings (1 per 10,700); McKinney (215k) = 28 listings (1 per 7,600). Host's rule of thumb from his tree-trimming business research: ~1 competitor per 10,000 people is a typical benchmark ratio; use this to estimate expected competitor count in any market by dividing its population by 10,000.
- Local-market knowledge matters for interpreting the numbers: e.g., Plano (fewer competitors) is more urban/built-out with smaller lots, so naively it looks "underserved," but McKinney has more rural/vacant acreage that actually supports more firewood businesses — a lower listing count doesn't automatically mean better opportunity without understanding why.
- Underused marketing channel: roadside/drive-by visibility. Estimate value using CPM logic: for a busy road (e.g. 23,000 cars/day), billboard-equivalent value is roughly $20 per 1,000 cars/day = ~$460–500/day of "free" advertising if you can display your business there (e.g., parking a branded vehicle streetside, using magnetic vehicle signage which costs ~80% less than a full vehicle wrap but gets ~half the visual effect; full wraps cost roughly $1,000–3,000+). Use your state DOT's traffic-count website/tool to find actual daily traffic counts for any specific street.
- Facebook Marketplace listing best practices (stated as universal across any marketplace: eBay, Airbnb, etc.):
  1. **Photos/video**: use the maximum number of photos/videos the platform allows (the platform's cap reflects the point of diminishing returns) — lead with a video since Facebook Marketplace's algorithm favors and prioritizes video-led listings (stops the scroll).
  2. **Description**: pack in every relevant keyword people might search (e.g., all wood species names — hickory, oak, ash, elm — even ones you don't currently sell) to maximize search visibility.
  3. **Responsiveness**: fastest response wins; most competitors are slow, so quick replies are a real competitive edge given Facebook's impatient audience.
- Do NOT put your phone number directly in a Facebook listing — Facebook detects and suppresses listings that try to route users off-platform (even with disguised formatting), reducing visibility. Instead, drive people to message you on Facebook and enable full notifications; staying on-platform is rewarded by the algorithm with more distribution.
- List actual prices in your listing rather than "message for price" — reduces total inquiry volume but raises the intent/quality of leads who do reach out.
- Naming/positioning example: the teens' business was named "College Fund Firewood" (breaking the host's usual short/catchy naming rule deliberately) to sell the underlying story (funding two named real high-schoolers' college funds) directly in the business name — used real names/photos in the listing (not stock images) to build authenticity and trust, framed as leaning into a "help real people" narrative rather than competing as a generic business.
- Underused channel: local Facebook Groups (not Marketplace) — e.g., city-specific groups (foodie groups, buy/sell/trade, job-hunting groups). Strategy: join relevant groups and post engagement-bait questions unrelated to your business (e.g., "best tacos in [city]?") to build karma; after roughly a week of engagement, Facebook's algorithm tends to tag active posters as "top contributor," after which you can post more overtly promotional content (telling your real story, e.g., "I'm a firewood business trying to save for college") without appearing spammy, benefiting from both earned trust and algorithmic favor.
- Caveats: firewood must be split with the grain (splitting against the grain, as demonstrated in the video, is called out as the "irresponsible" way to do it); wood sourcing via curbside pickup is inconsistent/seasonal and requires ongoing scouting; running a business reliant on tree-trimming partners requires actively training those crews on your exact cut-length specs or the wood won't stack into standard cord measurements.
