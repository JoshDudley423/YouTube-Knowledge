# A Secret Growth Hack to Steal Free Customers
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rd2STdr_Wp8 | Published: 2024-10-21

Interview with Edward Sturm (growth marketer, @buildinpublic). Main idea: take over abandoned Meetup.com groups. Secondary ideas: unbundling SaaS features with AI, "curious learner bias" content strategy, bottom-of-funnel SEO.

## Idea: Take over abandoned Meetup groups for free leads
- Meetup.com charges organizers ~$172/year (or more if paying monthly, roughly 2x the monthly-equivalent rate) to run a group with 50+ members.
- Many organizers get bored of hosting and stop paying, so they "step down"; Meetup emails every member of the group asking if anyone wants to take over as organizer -- for $50-$100/effective cost (or free, just take over the paid role) you inherit access to a group that may have thousands to tens of thousands of members.
- Once you take over, the original creator cannot reclaim it (confirmed by Chris's own experience accidentally taking over a group).
- Newer Meetup versions may surface "groups needing organizers" directly, even to non-members.
- Tactic to maximize odds: join as many meetup groups (any niche) as possible -- costs nothing to join, only costs money to organize -- and wait for the "organizer stepping down" email to arrive for one of them.
- Once you control a group, you can: message all members directly, rebrand the group name/niche (e.g., rename a general "AI curiosity" meetup to "NYC Generative AI Meetup" to pivot into a target niche like private equity), and co-host other people's events/meetups by lending your member list.
- Co-hosting monetization: event organizers (Meetup, Luma, etc.) want more attendees, so they'll accept an offer to send your members to their event in exchange for a company blurb + backlink (often a "do-follow" backlink, which passes SEO "link juice," vs. "no-follow" which doesn't) -- no cash payment involved.
- Stated application: private equity (PE) firms are described as very price-insensitive for basic digital marketing/research work (one anecdote: a PE firm reportedly paid $50,000 for an SEO analysis that would take ~15 minutes) -- but PE deals require real relationships/connections, so the strategy is to join finance-adjacent meetups, wait for one to need an organizer, take it over, rebrand it into a PE-focused meetup, then use it to build those connections via co-hosted events.
- Scalable via virtual assistant: a VA can run outreach using a script message to event hosts offering to send your members for a marketing blurb, letting this be operated remotely (workable "even while traveling through Europe").
- Caveat/nuance: it's unclear whether it costs literally nothing to join a meetup group or whether newer Meetup UI shows "needs organizer" listings to non-members too -- speakers weren't fully certain of current platform behavior.

## Idea: "Curious learner bias" content strategy
- Concept (credited to Sean Puri via the "My First Million" podcast): you don't need to be an expert to capture attention/opportunity in a niche -- you just need to be the most visibly passionate/curious learner, documenting what you learn daily.
- Method: pick a niche, learn something new every day, post a short video about what you learned that day.
- Why it works: learner-perspective content is naturally more relatable/less "cluttered with nuance" than expert content, so it can outperform expert content with audiences who are also beginners (or experts wanting a refresher on fundamentals).
- Example given: a friend growing a Formula 1 content channel has no driver's license, has never watched a full F1 race, doesn't code, and isn't faceless -- but is now a large F1 creator by posting ~5 videos/day (has hired 2 editors) purely from documenting his learning.
- Cited pattern: creators who don't fully understand a topic but have a better "hook" can dramatically outperform more accurate/expert content in views -- framed as "energy transference," not an endorsement of misinformation.
- Suggested application: someone learning cold email/email marketing could post a 60-second daily video of one lesson learned (e.g., "subject lines matter," "shorter subject lines convert better") to build an audience before launching a paid agency/service -- by the time they're ready to sell, they already have a trusting audience.

## Idea: Unbundling large SaaS features with AI
- Premise: identify one specific, heavily-used feature inside a large multi-feature SaaS product (e.g., HubSpot, Pipedrive) and build a standalone tool that does just that one thing.
- Example (validated in the wild): "Fathom" has a feature showing sales reps their talk-time percentage on a call; Edward tweeted a similar idea ("Stop Talking" Zoom plugin) independently -- an existing bundled feature is treated as market validation, not a deterrent.
- Suggested discovery method (from Chris, not fully executed by either): scrape reviews of a large SaaS tool and run them through an LLM (ChatGPT) to identify the specific feature/widget customers repeatedly rave about, distinct from the software's core purpose.
- Build approach: use AI coding tools (Cursor as an AI code editor + Claude as the underlying model + Replit as a dev environment with live previews) to build a single-feature app quickly (cited: someone built a background-removal Chrome extension in 20 minutes using this stack) -- no coding experience required. No-code tools like Bubble are an alternative (cited: Jason Levin built a "hottest memes for marketing" SaaS in Bubble).
- Monetization/growth loop after building: use "bottom of funnel" SEO (see below) to drive users; let users' feature requests guide further development; iterate.
- Example of a tiny-niche tool making real money: stag.io, "literally just a timer app," has SEO landing pages for verticals (sports, studios, education, gyms) and reportedly earns "tens of thousands of dollars MRR."
- Caveat raised in conversation: someone will always say "everyone can build that for free, why would you pay for it" -- the response given is that market size is effectively unlimited (internet = ~8 billion potential customers) so competing/crowded ideas can still support many winners (cited: Calendly still growing despite Google Calendar's competing scheduling feature).

## Idea/technique: Bottom-of-funnel SEO
- Definition: targeting search keywords from people who already know what solution they want but not which brand/company to buy from (as opposed to "top of funnel" educational/informational searches).
- Example: Edward searched "embed Instagram feed on website," found a company offering it free with their logo watermark, and paid $60/year to remove the watermark -- described as "cash in hand" conversion behavior typical of bottom-of-funnel searchers.
- Execution: write landing pages (400-600 words, reusable sections) targeting these specific bottom-of-funnel phrases; avoid AI-generated text for this because "Google catches you eventually."
- Context/caveat: top-of-funnel informational keywords (e.g., "how does X work," general how-to/definition queries) have shifted heavily toward Reddit and Quora in Google rankings because Google devalued AI-spam-filled websites in favor of user-generated, voted/commented content -- Reddit reportedly went from ~70-80th to 3rd most-clicked site from Google search in about a year.
- YouTube Shorts as an SEO/ranking hack: because Shorts are user-generated content with a voting mechanism (likes/comments), putting a target keyword at the start of the title, description, and using the video's own transcript as the description lets creators rank for that keyword "in a day." Suggested format: "[Competitor] + alternative" videos to capture competitor-comparison searches.
