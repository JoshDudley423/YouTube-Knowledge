# Which Side Hustle to Choose?
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kDgd536-Yfo | Published: 2024-03-15

Live call-in format (Twitter Spaces "Koerner's Corner," episode 5). Chris Koerner takes seven caller questions. Notes below organized by caller/topic.

## 1. Wedding venue (quasi wedding-venue/gift-shop/garden combo)
- Idea: buy a property with a few acres, run it primarily as a wedding venue with ancillary community classes, a garden, and a small gift shop; wedding revenue expected to be the primary/flagship income source, other elements more "passion project."
- Caller's spouse currently runs a remote cleaning business doing ~$20k/month topline at 20-30% net margin; plan is to sell that business (for "at least a couple hundred" thousand) in 1-2 years to fund the venue.
- Recommended process to validate before buying:
  - Use Outscraper (Google Maps scraping tool) to pull every wedding venue within a 100-mile radius of the target city.
  - Find 2-3 comparable-sized/demographic metro areas elsewhere in the country, run the same Outscraper search, and compute a baseline ratio (e.g., weddings-per-year vs. number of venues, derived from population share of ~1M total US weddings/year).
  - Call around to venues in both the target market and comparison markets to find out actual booking lead times (e.g., booked out 3 months vs. 9 months ahead) to gauge under/oversaturation, not just venue counts.
  - Check TheKnot.com — many venues sync calendars there, useful for checking booking density.
- Recommendation: buy an existing building below replacement cost rather than building new; pick a property that could pivot to another use (e.g., Airbnb) if the wedding-venue plan doesn't work out.
- Plan the business to be profitable on wedding revenue alone; treat classes/garden/gift shop as upside, not load-bearing.
- Caveat: don't assume "ours will be better" without market experience — Koerner's own experience with a 3PL logistics business was that after years in the weeds, competitors' seemingly bad practices often turn out to exist for real operational reasons.

## 2. Buying a vending-machine-location-finder business (via Acquire.com)
- Business for sale: $16,000 asking price. Model: customer pays $500 upfront for a location search; business finds and secures a vending-machine location; customer pays another $500 on placement; guarantee/refund if no location found within 30 days.
- Competitor model (lower-touch): sells a list of 200-300 leads + cold-call/email scripts for ~$200-300, vs. this business's roughly $1,000 (for one location, less per-unit if multiple) full-service model.
- Concerns raised: seller hasn't been actively working it (launching another venture), so growth has stalled; described as similar to "buying a job" since each sale requires ~2-3 days of direct labor to find a location.
- Recommended a de-risking play before buying: reach out to vending-industry influencers/creators (named: Quinn Miller) to set up referral/kickback agreements (e.g., split the $1,000 fee) before committing capital — the buy side of the marketplace (finding people who want to buy leads/locations) is harder than the sell side (finding vending locations is comparatively straightforward via Google Maps/Outscraper).
- Framed as a geographically-driven lead marketplace, analogous to Thumbtack selling the same lead to multiple home-service pros in different cities — big scale potential if buyer acquisition is solved.

## 3. AI/LLM tool for law firms (PDF Q&A product, "DocuSage")
- Origin story: built internally at a Chick-fil-A franchise to let employees ask an internal chatbot HR policy questions (uploaded policy manuals/training materials to ChatGPT) but had data-security/proprietary-info concerns, so built a dedicated tool with a co-founder.
- Product: upload large PDFs (now multiple at once) and query them via an LLM backend; ~30 beta users, roughly 30% lawyers (inbound via Twitter, worried about ChatGPT hallucinating fake case law), some academics (grad/PhD researchers), rest general business owners.
- Branding used a "Harry Potter/Deathly Hallows" theme (cloak of invisibility = data security/privacy, resurrection stone/Elder Wand = AI's ability to surface/control information).
- Advice given: lean into the legal niche specifically rather than staying a generic "read my PDF" tool (that positioning looks commoditized/like competitors already in space, e.g. "read my pdf" style tools); niche within a niche (compare to Koerner's own AI billing tool built specifically for skilled nursing facilities) can still be a massive market.
- Advice: leave front-end branding open-ended but point features at the legal market; ship 3-4 concrete features derived from feedback from the 10 lawyer beta users (e.g., upload past case files and query them); add friction to document upload (require lawyers to send docs to you rather than self-serve upload) to project expertise/value — analogy to artificially delaying a 20-second iPhone screen repair to seem more valuable.
- Pricing advice: start low (~$100/month) to capture market share/get embedded in daily workflow, then raise prices once usage is habitual.
- Aside: recommended using Claude over the OpenAI API, citing better results from their own trial and error.

## 4. Which side hustle to prioritize (caller with multiple side income streams)
- Caller's profile: $98k salary + up to $30k bonus at day job; side income: 4,000-person email list (50% open rate, 5% click-through), ~30k LinkedIn followers, ~a few thousand X followers, sponsored posts (~$600/mo), newsletter ads (~$200/mo), a paid community (~$200/mo), consulting (~$1,500/mo), plus separate solopreneur consulting.
- Advice: don't try to do everything at once — go all-in on the community specifically, since $200/month revenue from 40,000+ combined followers signals under-monetization (either underpricing or under-delivering value).
- Framed as "Planet Fitness vs. Equinox" pricing/value models: cheap price + low commitment/low engagement (Planet Fitness) vs. high price ($500/mo) that forces both operator and members to invest more effort and get more value (Equinox) — pick one model deliberately rather than a low-price/low-value hybrid that doesn't work.

## 5. Paid-ads freelancer wanting more clients (one dentist client currently)
- Caller is a former professional photographer transitioning into Meta/Google ads freelancing, has Meta/Google certifications, one dentist client (family friend).
- Advice: dentists are one of the best niches to sell agency services into — they're highly willing to pay for growth (sometimes to the point of being oversaturated with agencies) because they want passive/outside income beyond dentistry.
- Concrete process: get exact revenue-attribution numbers from the existing dentist client as a case study; ask that client for referrals to other dentist friends; offer 2-3 of those referred dentists free ad management for 30 days (they only pay ad spend) by copy-pasting/adapting the working campaign and swapping geography; once proven, start charging; niche down to dentists only. Customer acquisition channel suggested: dentist-focused Facebook groups, via group moderators.

## 6. Twitter/X Spaces — worth it for audience growth?
- Koerner's own experience running "Koerner's Corner" as a weekly Spaces show: listener counts declined week over week (90 → 80 → 60 → 45 → 44 concurrent) though the companion podcast is growing well; describes Spaces' long-term viability as still an open question ("jury's out").
- Observation: video Spaces dramatically outperform audio-only (cites roughly 10x: 50 concurrent audio listeners vs. 500 for video) but video Spaces were buggy/unreliable when tested.
- General platform-growth point: audiences rarely transfer well across platforms — a newsletter with a 60% open rate converted only a "fraction of a percent" of its list into Spotify/YouTube followers. Building a real multi-platform presence (cites Cody Sanchez as an example who did it successfully) generally requires a full-time content team and paid ads, not incidental cross-posting.
- Different platforms suit different creator styles (e.g., X favors writers/introverts over short-form video).

## 7. Starting a virtual-assistant (VA) placement agency
- Two competing VA agency business models discussed:
  1. Placement fee model: charge ~35% of the VA's annual salary upfront (roughly $3-4k) — bigger one-time payment, harder sell to small business owners.
  2. Staffing/markup model (like a temp agency): pay the VA e.g. $5/hr, charge the client $8/hr, keep the spread — easier sell (no big upfront cost) but requires ongoing "handholding" of VAs.
- Recommended model 2 for an easier sale; recommended outsourcing the interviewing/hiring/VA-management backend to an existing provider (named: John Matzner) and focusing entirely on front-end customer acquisition, effectively becoming a lead-gen layer on top of an existing fulfillment provider.
- Tactical suggestion: build multiple cheap templated landing pages testing different vertical-specific offers ("VA for piano tutors," "VA for graphic designers," etc.), drive paid traffic to see what resonates, then go all-in on the best-performing niche. Suggested using SEMrush or Moz to find underutilized/undermonetized Google Ads keywords to guide which niches/landing pages to build.
- Advice on niche selection generally: pick niches with a clearly quantifiable value proposition (e.g., a fractional CFO's output is objectively verifiable — the numbers/reports either got delivered or didn't) over nebulous/creative ones (e.g., fractional CMO, generic marketing, or generic "virtual assistant") where it's hard to prove ROI. Suggested checking a site like SupportShepherd.com's category dropdown order as a signal for which VA/outsourcing services sell best (bookkeeping and graphic design were cited as top sellers because output is easy to verify).
