# The Most Underrated Side Hustle You Can Start With $0
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E9baszgY3J8 | Published: 2026-08-09

Interview with Braden Harman (flippingcouches.com), who started an estate sale company in Benton, Arkansas (2017) with almost no capital, grew it to a $30-40K/month gross business, then discovered it worked even better as a lead-generation funnel for off-market real estate deals (60 property deals since 2018).

## The estate sale business model
- An estate sale company contracts with a homeowner (or heirs) to liquidate the entire contents of a house — literally everything gets priced and offered for sale, sometimes including vehicles and occasionally the house itself. Trigger is almost always urgency to empty the house so it can be sold.
- The "4 D's" driving demand, in order of frequency: **D**eceased (most common), **D**ivorce and **D**ownsizing (roughly tied for second), **D**ebt (foreclosure/abandonment situations) — downsizers (e.g., a parent moving to assisted living) tend to be the most price-sensitive; everyone else typically just wants the house cleared fast and isn't very price-sensitive.
- Fee structure: no upfront fee — 100% contingent on a percentage of gross sales. Industry average is **35%**; can go as low as under 35% for large/high-value estates (cars, jewelry) with more competition for the job, or as high as 45-50% for small/low-value ("junky") estates that barely meet a company's minimum size threshold. Marketing costs (e.g., listing fees) are passed through to the client. Companies often set a minimum dollar guarantee (e.g., "35% or a $2,000 minimum") to protect against a rained-out or poorly-attended sale.
- Payment timing: company collects all money directly (cash, card, check) at the point of sale — no chasing down payment from customers. Client typically waits ~2 weeks for the company to reconcile and pay out their share, which gives the business a favorable (float-positive) cash conversion cycle.

## Startup capital and first numbers
- Startup requirements: virtually none — a price gun (from Amazon), masking tape, a phone or basic point-of-sale system, folding tables (borrow from a church, buy ~$50 Costco Lifetime tables, or use the client's own tables/counters), and a few friends/family to help staff it. Can be started and run an entire sale profitably within a week or two with $0 committed capital.
- Path in: founder started with weekend yard-sale flipping (buying collectibles/vintage items to resell in a small $-per-month antique-store booth), pivoted to estate sales when yard sales dried up in winter.
- Broke in via reciprocity, not competition: approached the top local husband-and-wife estate sale company, offered to volunteer, was instead hired at minimum wage for a few sales — this taught operations (staffing needs: cashier, floor "wanderers" watching for theft, furniture-loading help; pricing speed/accuracy; standard sale-day discount structure — day 1 full price, day 2 ~20-25% off, day 3 50% off, or a compressed 2-day version for smaller/HOA-restricted sales) and, critically, led that company to start referring him their smaller/unprofitable leads (existing companies are reportedly happy to have a trustworthy overflow partner rather than turn business away). Reasoning given for why an established competitor helped a new entrant: demand for estate liquidation is large and structurally growing (cites Cerulean Associates estimate of $124 trillion in US wealth transfer over the next 20 years, ~20% of it real estate) — more clients exist than there are estate sale companies to serve them in most markets.
- Additional lead source used from the start: bringing coffee/donuts to local real estate broker office meetings and giving a 10-minute pitch — described as an unusually receptive audience (brokers get referral fees or unload difficult listings faster when the contents are cleared), unlike cold outreach to most other office types.
- First independent sale: a 3-bed/2-bath "smoker's house" from a real-estate-investor friend who let them keep 100% of proceeds just to have the house cleared — grossed $5,000-$6,000, took ~40 hours of labor between two people (an effective ~$150/hour rate on cash economics with near-zero cost basis); by the time of the interview the same job could be priced and staged in about a day (~1/5th the time) with experience.
- Unsold remainder (industry rule of thumb: a good sale moves 70-85% of contents) is typically consigned to a local auction house, which usually also takes ~35% but guarantees everything sells (often bundled at low per-item prices) — cost to the estate sale company is just labor to transport it.

## Where the money is in a house
- No universal 80/20 pattern — revenue mix is estate-specific; called out the garage/"man cave" as frequently the single biggest revenue center (tools, lawn equipment, beer memorabilia) despite being the most tedious and uncomfortable to price (no AC). Specific surprising collectible categories mentioned: vintage boat motors, vintage grills (e.g., PK Grill, an Arkansas-made aluminum grill that holds value at ~$200 even 20-30 years old), mid-century modern furniture, vintage Star Wars toys/collectibles (an unopened 1980s Star Wars hoard drew buyers who flew in from out of state), guns, jewelry, coins.
- Marketing only needs photos/descriptions of the standout/high-value items (not every item, unlike Facebook Marketplace individual listings) — the "surface area" principle: listing several distinct rare/niche items (Star Wars memorabilia, a boat, vintage clothing) each draws a different buyer persona to the sale, and those buyers often purchase unrelated additional items once there.
- Suggested AI-assisted valuation workflow (not currently used by this operator, but raised by the host): photograph unfamiliar items and ask Claude/ChatGPT which items in the batch look potentially valuable/interesting to identify what merits closer research or expert appraisal, rather than needing decades of trained-eye experience.

## Growth trajectory and the warehouse-sale innovation
- Revenue trajectory: ~$5K/month gross average year 1, ~$10K/month year 2, $20-30K/month gross year 3, peaking around $30-40K/month gross at the point the founder stepped back — first full-time job quit came ~6-7 months in, after landing a $30,000 single estate sale.
- Key scaling unlock: got informal access to an 8,000 sq ft section of a 24,000 sq ft family-connected warehouse (~$400/weekend rent) and began consolidating many small/marginal estates (previously not worth a standalone in-house sale) into combined warehouse sales — went from single sales of $1-2K to combined weekend sales of $20-30K gross with up to 30 different estates ("lots") represented simultaneously, drawing more total foot traffic through cross-pollination (each shopper came for a different estate's items but browsed everything).
- Operational note on warehouse consolidation: each estate is tracked as a numbered "lot" on the cash register/POS so proceeds can be reconciled per-client at day's end — described as simple in practice (a lot number plus a price per sale).
- Trade-off acknowledged: identical items reportedly sell for **more** in an actual home setting than in a warehouse (buyers can picture furniture in a real room, akin to home staging psychology) — but the warehouse compensates with parking capacity (no neighborhood parking constraints) and volume/reach across combined estates.
- Typical single-house estate sale attendance: 300-400 people/day in a well-marketed ~2,000 sq ft house (more on discount day 3), but can be as low as 30-40/day with bad photos, weak items, or bad weather; the single best sale cited drew an estimated 3,000-4,000 people over the weekend (a 4,000 sq ft upscale home with the seller's professional event-planning inventory across many niche categories, grossing ~$30,000).
- Lead capture idea raised in-conversation (not confirmed as something they built out fully): use a paper "bid sheet" system to defuse in-person haggling (customers submit a written offer instead of negotiating live, which also avoids setting a precedent overheard by other shoppers) — this can double as an email/phone list capture mechanism for future sale announcements and paid "VIP preview" upsells (e.g., $50 for early access the night before a big sale).

## Solo/part-time earnings potential
- Stated a realistic, no-employee, weekends-only estimate: an individual actively networking with real estate agents, elder-law attorneys, and other estate sale companies for overflow leads can make an extra **$1,000-$4,000/month** working only weekends.
- Reported found-cash/valuable-item incidents (an operational hazard/perk category): $1,200 found in a wallet inside a Dutch oven in an oven; a full jewelry chest; illegal drugs occasionally found (not sellable, obviously); items like social security cards, personal photos, and cash are separated out and returned to (or a shred/disposal instruction obtained from) the client rather than sold.
- Ethical/consistency note on staff buying items themselves: policies vary by company; some strictly forbid buying from a client's own estate to avoid conflict of interest (may ask to buy only unsold leftovers after the sale), others allow first-dibs purchases at full price with client permission when a niche collectible surfaces.

## The bigger discovery: estate sales as an off-market real estate deal funnel
- After running estate sales, the founder noticed he was "first through the door" of houses about to be sold — uniquely positioned to identify distressed/underpriced properties before they hit the market.
- First real-estate deal happened almost accidentally: a client with too little to justify an estate sale (~$1,000-2,000 of contents) was offered a direct buyout of the house + remaining contents; founder pulled the old carpet (skipped a quoted $40,000 renovation entirely), relisted a $70,000 purchase for $150,000, and it went under contract within a week — this converted him fully into real estate.
- Scaled this into a dedicated deal-sourcing channel: contacted other local estate sale companies and asked them to refer any house that "won't sell for whatever reason" or needs to move fast; also used Deal Machine to send postcards to distressed-looking houses spotted incidentally while running sales in a neighborhood, and built relationships with realtors who send him properties they can't otherwise sell.
- Track record: 60 real estate deals since 2018 (8 years) — roughly half wholesaled (assigned the contract to another buyer without closing, netting between $1,000 and $30,000+ per deal via double-close), ~20 bought/renovated/flipped, and a handful retained as short-term/mid-term rentals (mid-term via Furnished Finder, e.g., renting to relocated Buc-ee's construction workers and travel nurses for 3+ months at a time, described as lower-maintenance than short-term Airbnb-style turnover). Funded deals via hard money and local lenders; never lost money on a deal outright, though the smallest wholesale profit was ~$1,000.
- States he has never encountered another real estate investor sourcing deals this way, despite attending BiggerPockets conferences and being active in REIAs (Real Estate Investor Associations) in three states — calls it "the best kept secret in real estate."
- Caveat given by the interviewer directly: acknowledges this strategy will likely not be widely copied despite being shared publicly, because it's unscalable, physically demanding ("sweaty"), slow, and doesn't fit the current trend toward AI/software-based side hustles — most viewers won't follow through even knowing the playbook.

## Ways to get started (three tiers mentioned)
1. **Fully independent/DIY**: no franchise fee, learn from books/YouTube, handle all marketing and lead sourcing yourself — recommended as a part-time/side-hustle starting point rather than jumping in full-time.
2. **Franchise** (three major players named: Blue Moon, Caring Transitions, Grasons): $50,000-$75,000 upfront investment plus ongoing revenue-share, but provides instant local authority, built-in lead flow, training, marketing materials, and a website.
3. **Hybrid/"business-in-a-box"** (guest is an affiliate of EstateSaleBiz.com): a few hundred to ~$1,000 one-time fee for a done-for-you website plus training, no franchise fee and no ongoing royalty, but you still do your own lead generation and marketing.

## Caveats
- The estate sale business is explicitly framed by the founder as no longer his main profit center — he has "eased out" of running sales personally and now treats it primarily as a real-estate lead funnel, occasionally consulting.
- Founder's overall real-estate profits are described only in rough terms ("six-figure income, seven figures of additional net worth" over 8 years, per the host's summary which the guest agreed with) — not independently verified.
- A blindside risk flagged for buy-and-hold real estate specifically: rising interest rates (cites his own experience going from 3-4% to 6-8% on commercial loans) significantly changed deal economics; also emphasizes always getting a professional home inspection and licensed-contractor opinion rather than relying on a seller's claims or your own read of a property's condition.
