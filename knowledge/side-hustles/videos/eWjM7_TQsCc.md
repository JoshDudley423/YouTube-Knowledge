# How to Make Millions Selling Stupid Impulse Products
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eWjM7_TQsCc | Published: 2024-09-13

Brainstorm conversation between Chris Koerner and Nik ("HoldCo Bros," part 2) -- mostly speculative idea generation, not real business case studies (unlike other episodes). Ideas range from product concepts to a viral-content-driven retail and influencer-investment model.

## Idea 1: Trampoline safety products
- Problem observed: backyard trampolines with side walls act like sails and can become airborne in strong wind (personal anecdote: two of the host's trampolines blew onto a neighbor's vacant land).
- Product concept A: a simple visual "wind flag" attached to the trampoline's sidewall that visibly waves/signals when wind exceeds a dangerous threshold (e.g., ~20mph) as a warning to get kids off/inside; possibly bundled with a tie-down kit, priced around $50-100. Marketing angle: short-form video of trampolines going airborne to sell the fear/safety angle.
- Product concept B (preferred, "white whale" framing): a cheap subscription app ($1/month or so) that pushes weather alerts (high wind, hail, lightning) to parents so they can act proactively rather than needing a physical flag. Suggested go-to-market: rather than selling to individual consumers, license/sell the app (or pay a small licensing fee per download) to a trampoline manufacturer (e.g., premium brand Springfree) so they can differentiate their product as "tech-enabled" -- framed as a "sell to one [decision maker] to sell to a million [consumers]" strategy; could also be licensed exclusively to one manufacturer or broadly across multiple outdoor kids' product companies.
- Product concept C (physical/mechanical, more speculative): a mechanism that either disengages the trampoline's spring/mat system or causes the safety walls to collapse under high wind pressure so it can no longer catch wind like a sail -- acknowledged as technically unproven/hand-wavy.
- Adjacent real business mentioned: trampolines.com sells in-ground trampolines (not susceptible to wind lift) plus installation; their actual bottleneck isn't sales but finding reliable local installers nationwide -- flagged as a potential opportunity to build/broker an installer network as an add-on service, or refer overflow install demand from swing-set/playset/treehouse installers into that channel.
- General principle drawn from this segment: identify things parents will pay for purely for peace of mind, even when the underlying risk is statistically rare -- and that broader framework ("what will parents pay for peace of mind") is offered as a reusable lens for finding other product ideas.

## Idea 2: Tornado-safety mattress handles
- Problem: standard tornado safety advice ("get in the bathtub/put a mattress over you") is impractical because mattresses are heavy and hard to maneuver.
- Product concept: add simple carrying-strap handles to the underside of a mattress (also useful for movers generally), patent the design, and license it to mattress manufacturers for a per-unit royalty (e.g., $5/mattress).
- Cost objection raised: mattress manufacturing is high-volume, low-margin; small per-unit cost additions (like 6 extra inches of material in an airline-seatbelt analogy) can scale into large unacceptable costs for a manufacturer, making them resistant to add this as standard equipment.
- Alternative go-to-market to avoid needing manufacturer buy-in: sell an aftermarket, Velcro-attached retrofit "handle" product directly to consumers (compared to As-Seen-On-TV/Shark Tank-style products) as part of a "tornado kit" -- avoids needing to convince a mattress manufacturer at all, and can double as a demand-validation exercise before approaching manufacturers for a licensing deal (e.g., "we've proven demand, it'll cost you ~$7/mattress, give us an exclusive").

## Idea 3: Viral-trend-tracking community/tool (speculative, unbuilt)
- Observation: several existing businesses/personal brands (Nikita Bir-style plays, "Hawk Tuah girl," "founder mode," Koerner's own perfume vending venture) show that individuals/companies who catch a viral moment extremely early can build a real business or windfall around it.
- Proposed idea: build a paid community or tool that surfaces early-stage viral content signals (posts trending unusually fast, e.g., "300k views in the first hour" for a normally-smaller creator) faster than general social feeds do, so subscribers can act on emerging trends before saturation.
- Discussed build approaches: (a) a pure software/API approach that aggregates existing niche Discord/Telegram/WhatsApp/Twitter groups that already informally track virality (compared to crypto "pump and dump" Discords where early members profit most), or (b) a crowdsourced human-curated model where recognized "good spotters" submit trends they notice, potentially with a reputation/"social credit" scoring system rewarding contributors whose early calls actually go viral (could even offer equity/rev-share to top-performing contributors).
- Explicit caveat: hosts note they've tried and churned off existing "trending topic" tools (e.g., mentions an app called "Break the Web" and generic trend-tracking SaaS products) because they surface generic mainstream trends (e.g., Olympics) rather than genuinely niche/early signals -- they argue the hard part is the human pattern-recognition of an experienced, platform-obsessed user, not just an algorithmic view-count threshold.

## Idea 4: "Brookstone of the 21st century" -- physical retail for viral Alibaba/AliExpress impulse products
- Observation: certain Instagram accounts (cited: an account by "Mick Dozon," ~2.1M followers) post videos of novelty products sourced from Alibaba/AliExpress (examples given: a hands-free canopy - 17M views, a floating pool cooler, a shoe/boot removal tool, an "instant trailer lift" tool - 41M views) that go viral purely on visual appeal, despite being objectively unnecessary/impulse items.
- Proposed business: open a small-footprint (under 1,000 sq ft) physical retail store -- explicitly modeled on the now-largely-defunct Brookstone mall chain -- stocked with ~20-30 of the highest-performing viral products (selected by view/comment counts on social video), with a screen out front looping demo videos. Framed as high-margin, impulse-driven, and well-suited to mall/high-foot-traffic physical retail specifically because people want to physically test/play with products before buying (cites Brookstone's massage chairs as the classic example of this behavior).
- Suggested lower-capital variant: run this as a temporary pop-up shop rather than a full lease -- pop-ups are typically placed in high-income, high-traffic retail areas in spaces that are currently vacant but expected to be leased again soon, allowing short-term/month-to-month leases (sometimes at a premium rate) without long-term commitment; useful both as a genuine store format or as a way to test a concept before signing a long-term lease. Cited real-world comparable: a temporary pop-up "candy museum" concept the host's family visited in Dallas.
- Suggested even-lower-capital variant: no inventory carrying at all -- stock the physical space only with display samples; customers try items in person, then purchase is fulfilled via drop-shipping/an Amazon seller partnership so it arrives at their home (e.g., same-day in a market with nearby Amazon fulfillment centers, such as Dallas or LA) rather than walking out with the product immediately. Tradeoff acknowledged: some percentage of customers will back out once told they won't get the item immediately (unknown drop-off rate, estimated conversationally as possibly single-digit to ~10%), but this is offset by eliminating large inventory carrying costs -- useful as a way to A/B test real demand data before committing capital to a fully-stocked store.
- Suggested underlying monetization structure: rather than just running an affiliate-link storefront (as the Instagram account owner mentioned appears to do, via an Amazon storefront), build a Shopify-based drop-shipped store and offer that creator a larger revenue cut than a standard affiliate link in exchange for routing purchases directly through the new storefront.
- Broader real-estate framing: applies this same no-inventory "showroom" concept as a potential answer to struggling malls with excess vacant space (cites Peloton's retail stores closing as an example of available small-footprint space, ~800 sq ft) -- malls could lease space cheaply to category-specific showrooms (e.g., golf equipment, pickleball paddles) that carry no inventory, letting shoppers try products in person while purchases ship from a centralized fulfillment center, reducing the mall's need for tenants to carry Foot-Locker-style backroom inventory.

## Idea 5: Investing in/building products around under-monetized niche influencers (the most substantive idea)
- Observed pattern: an Instagram creator ("Clean Girl," a woman posting cleaning/before-after content, e.g. cleaning gravestones) grew a large following and then launched her own white-labeled cleaning product (goingcleangirl.com referenced) -- hypothesis is this reflects a broader playbook: take a generic, already-manufactured product, put strong branding/packaging on it, and pair it with a creator whose organic content format (before/after transformation videos) already reliably goes viral, rather than developing a novel product from scratch.
- Extended idea -- proactive micro-influencer investment/incubation model: instead of paying an established influencer a one-off sponsorship fee, invest early in a micro-influencer (sub-10k followers) who fits a strong "product-audience fit" profile, fund them (e.g., $50,000) to keep growing their existing content/audience without touching their current account, then later co-develop and launch a product with them, giving the creator meaningful equity (framed examples: 15-49%) in the resulting business rather than a flat sponsorship check. Rationale: this removes the leverage a creator would otherwise gain once they're big enough to negotiate top sponsorship rates, and aligns incentives since the brand's own investment directly grows the audience that will buy the future product.
- Referenced adjacent large-scale executor of a similar (later-stage) model: Chernin Group's ~$17.5M investment in Kevin Espiritu (Epic Gardening, an 8-figure-revenue YouTube/media business), where Chernin takes ~20% equity in whatever ventures Espiritu launches off the back of his audience/brand -- described as a "private equity" version of the idea (betting on an already-proven creator) versus the proposed "venture" version (betting earlier on unproven micro-influencers, expecting most bets to fail but a couple to be large wins, similar to venture portfolio math -- "eight of them are not going to hit, but two of them are just going to crush").
- Concrete comparable outcome cited to justify the thesis: a female creator whose Instagram following formed around her own hair-loss/thinning struggles later launched a hair-loss cream product and reportedly hit eight figures in revenue within 3 months, attributed to near-perfect product-audience fit (~80% of her existing followers reportedly shared the exact problem the product solved). The hosts propose reverse-engineering this pattern deliberately: find creators whose audience already has strong, specific, monetizable pain points (example given: an "Accutane competitor" and creators discussing acne/skin issues) who haven't yet thought to launch a product themselves, and offer to fund/manufacture/launch it for them in exchange for equity while making them the face of the brand.
- Speculative idea about how larger companies could/might already exploit this: a major consumer brand could fund an influencer's account growth without immediately attaching sponsored-product disclosure requirements, only monetizing/disclosing once the audience is large enough -- flagged as more plausible coming from an entrepreneurial individual or small tech-style startup than a traditional large CPG company like Procter & Gamble.
- Connects to a broader observation from the hosts' past episodes: the profitability of pairing visually compelling, already-viral content formats (before/after transformations, home-service visuals like pressure washing/lawn care) with a product or business built specifically to be marketed that way, rather than the other way around.

## Caveats
- Most ideas in this episode are explicitly labeled speculative brainstorming ("I have an idea that is a stupid idea"), not validated business models -- unlike other episodes in this channel that profile real operators and real numbers.
- Physical/mechanical product ideas (trampoline wall collapse mechanism, mattress handles) are acknowledged by the hosts themselves as technically uncertain or potentially cost-prohibitive at manufacturing scale.
- The viral-trend-tracking community idea is explicitly noted as something the hosts looked for and could not find a satisfying existing tool for -- presented as an open opportunity, not a proven concept.
