# How To Start a Mobile Detailing Business with $500 (Everything You Need To Know)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGf27oVmgYE | Published: 2025-10-03

Interview with Joel Cruz, 21, owner of "Clean Cruise" mobile car detailing (Farmers Branch/Riverway, Texas area, business name shown on screen as "F Cruz"/Clean Cruise). Started January 1 after losing a restaurant server job; 8 months in at time of filming.

## The business idea
- Mobile car detailing (interior/exterior cleaning), done at customer's location, targeting a mix of general customers and increasingly higher-end/exotic car owners.
- Started with equipment bought incrementally, no formal storefront; operates out of his father's truck.

## Startup costs and numbers
- Total equipment investment over time: ~$4,000, but built up gradually, not spent up front.
- Minimum viable starter kit named explicitly: a pressure washer + a vacuum. A $100-$200 pressure washer is stated as sufficient and still what he uses 8 months in (i.e., no need to overspend on equipment early).
- Stated realistic starting budget for someone beginning today: $500.
- Current revenue: roughly $5,000/month, working ~40 hours/week, before hiring any employees.
- Goal stated: scale to $10,000/month, which he acknowledges will require hiring help.

## Pricing examples
- Monthly recurring customer (car in decent condition): $100/service.
- One-time detail on a relatively clean 2-seat coupe (customer's Porsche 911): $80, took 1-2 hours — framed by the host as a great deal for the customer (who might otherwise get quotes of $200-$300) while still netting Joel roughly $80/hour.
- Self-described pricing position: "middle" of the local market, not the cheapest.

## Customer acquisition
- Initial tactic: printed generic business cards (via a basic print service) and left them on parked cars, specifically targeting nicer/well-maintained vehicles in high-end areas (malls in the North Park/Highland Park/Preston Hollow area of the Dallas market). Placed cards on ~250-300 cars over about a week.
- Initial response rate: very low — only 2 responses from ~250-300 cards (about 1%): one direct job, one phone call that didn't convert.
- That single early job (a $100/month recurring customer in an affluent neighborhood) generated organic word-of-mouth exposure: a neighbor saw the work being done from the street, later approached Joel in person and became a high-value customer (owns a Porsche 911), and that customer subsequently referred multiple additional high-net-worth clients.
- After 8 months of building a track record (before/after photos, testimonials, case studies, a bit of a local reputation), his current card-to-job conversion rate is roughly 10% (i.e., about 1 job per 10 cards carded) — a 10x improvement attributed directly to having reputation/social proof to point to, versus 1% with zero reputation at launch.
- Also listed on Thumbtack: pays roughly $8-$10 per lead for interior-only jobs, up to ~$125 per lead for full-service jobs; closes roughly half of Thumbtack leads, yielding an effective customer acquisition cost around $30 via that channel.
- Beyond cards and Thumbtack, growth has been driven by word-of-mouth/referrals from friends, family, and satisfied clients as he built a reputation for reliability.
- No website at time of filming (host explicitly flags this as something he should fix).

## Targeting strategy
- Deliberately targets higher-end vehicles for card placement and marketing focus: exotic/luxury cars, trucks (4x4s, diesels), and larger SUVs (Escalades, Yukons) — while still accepting other types of jobs.
- Rationale: well-maintained/expensive vehicles' owners are more likely to pay for regular detailing and to become recurring high-value customers.

## Caveats / lessons learned
- "Don't take every job" — learned to screen out problematic clients based on vibe/attitude during the initial interaction.
- A correlation noted between an unusually messy/trashed car interior (despite the owner downplaying it, e.g., "it's not that bad") and being a difficult client overall — treated as a warning sign worth heeding before accepting a job.
- Least favorite part of the job: laundering the microfiber towels (ironically, not the physically hard work of detailing cars in 100°F heat).
- Most rewarding part cited: the relationships/people met through the work, not the cars themselves.
