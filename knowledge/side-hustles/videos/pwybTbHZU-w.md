# How to Get Your First Service Clients Paying Tens of Thousands
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwybTbHZU-w | Published: 2026-01-14

Interview with Jameson Haslam, who left a Wall Street/private equity career to found West Coast Decks, a Southern California deck-building company.

## The business idea and why decks specifically
- Deliberately chose a high average-order-value, low-decision-count service. Heuristic: the ratio of order value to number of client decisions determines how easy it is to avoid mistakes/dissatisfaction — a deck has ~3-5 real decisions (shape, material/brand/color, railing type), so you can do a "one-meeting close" for a $50,000 job in about an hour.
- Contrasted with custom home building (400-500 decisions, much higher risk of dissatisfaction) — similar framework to "binary outcome" home services discussed elsewhere on the show (tree removal = 1 decision, done).
- Decks are "intimidating enough" to deter general handymen from competing, creating a moat of complexity/project-management skill without requiring extreme specialization.

## Concrete numbers
- First month in business: closed $60,000 (3 jobs — one small, two medium) despite zero prior deck-building experience, pricing deliberately low as a loss-leader to build social proof.
- Spent roughly $1,500 on lead-service marketing to close that first $60k.
- First year: revenue "just under $1 million."
- 4 years in as of filming; expects "something north of $4M and less than $8M" for the current year.
- Lead costs (Southern California, ~5 years prior): $60/lead for deck-repair leads, $120/lead for deck-build leads (via Angie's List/HomeAdvisor at the time).
- Organic/referral-driven revenue: ~$400k in year one and ~$600k in year two came from Instagram-driven referrals from adjacent trades (mostly landscapers) — attributed as "free"/organic. Total organic-driven revenue to date: "7 or 8 million dollars," with the only paid marketing being that first month of Angie's/HomeAdvisor plus a couple of Yelp promotions.
- Pricing/margin rule of thumb: bid every job at ~50% gross margin (i.e., price = 2x cost of labor + materials); this is what nets out to ~15% net margin after inevitable bid misses/accidents/scope issues. "If you do more than 15% net you're under-investing in something; less than that, you need help with something."
- Notes the "death zone" in small business growth is roughly $3M-$8M revenue — enough complexity to need elite management/leadership talent, but not yet enough profit margin to comfortably afford it.

## Concrete steps / playbook to launch
1. **Name and brand simply**: name = service area + service (e.g., "Dallas Decks," not "Chris's Construction Co.") so it's unambiguous and easy to find via Google search.
2. **Cheap professional branding hack** (~$2,000-$3,000 total, described as producing a "7/10 agency-quality result" for a fraction of agency cost):
   - Browse Pinterest/Behance/Dribbble for brand aesthetics you like from *unrelated* industries (e.g., D2C shampoo branding) rather than searching your own niche directly.
   - Post a job on Upwork/Fiverr: "$200 for 20 design concepts for my brand [name], inspired by [reference]." Hire ~5 different freelancers this way to get ~100 concept permutations for ~$1,000 total.
   - Pick 2-3 favorites, then hire one designer (Upwork, often overseas — e.g., Ukraine/Vietnam) for under $1,000 to produce a full brand kit: logo, typography, color palette, uniforms.
3. **Set up all your digital "billboards" identically**: website, Google Business Profile, Yelp, Houzz, Better Business Bureau, Chamber of Commerce, Instagram — same phone number, same address, same brand across all.
4. **Get initial leads from paid lead-gen services** before you qualify for Google Local Services Ads (LSA often requires licensing/history you won't have yet): Thumbtack, TaskRabbit, Angie (merged Angie's List/HomeAdvisor).
5. **Speed to lead is non-negotiable**: respond to every lead within 30 seconds; do a qualifying call/site visit within a day; deliver a proposal within another day (or same day). Explicit statement: "If you cannot do that, do not do this business — do not do any entrepreneurial thing at all."
6. **Hire your first VA above local market wage**: e.g., pay $12-15/hr to someone in the Philippines/Central/South America rather than the local US wage (~$20/hr) — you get someone far more experienced (master's degree, 5-10 years work experience) at 2-3x the going overseas rate, which nets roughly 50x ROI versus a comparable local hire, because your first VA needs judgment, not just following a rigid script (you don't yet have a "plug and play" system).
7. **Build the informal referral ecosystem**: personally meet material suppliers (lumber yards, product sales reps) and introduce yourself — they will refer business. Be highly visible on Instagram/Facebook specifically to build cross-trade referral relationships (e.g., roofers whose storm-damage jobs also need deck repair refer to a visible/active deck company) — described as most effective for B2B/adjacent-trade referral networking, not direct B2C ad targeting.
8. Get early social proof any way possible before you have real customer reviews — ask friends, family, former coworkers, even a former teacher for character-based testimonials while you build a track record.

## Tools/platforms named
- Angie's List / HomeAdvisor (merged into "Angie"), Thumbtack, TaskRabbit — paid lead services.
- Upwork, Fiverr — for design work and hiring VAs.
- Google LSA (Local Services Ads) — longer-term lead channel, gated by licensing/track record.
- HighLevel (sponsor mention) — all-in-one CRM/funnel/scheduling/automation platform with a "missed call text back" auto-responder feature; can be white-labeled and resold to clients for recurring revenue.

## Caveats / risks
- Explicit warning against the common contractor belief that "word of mouth/good work alone is enough" — paid advertising does work; "you're just doing it wrong" if it seems not to.
- California specifically cited as an unusually difficult regulatory/business environment (subcontractor management is more complex there than in other states).
- Early pricing is intentionally a loss-leader to build social proof — acceptable only if you have cash reserves to absorb it; not a strategy for a fully bootstrapped operator with no buffer.
- Owner needs to be "all in" (full commitment/no backup plan) per Jameson's personal approach — he explicitly contrasts this with Koerner's usual "test on the side first" advice; frames it as necessary for himself specifically because he doesn't believe halfway effort will work for him.
- Margin math only works if you rigorously hold the line on scope/cost during execution (enforcing subcontractor agreements, negotiating supplier terms, fighting for change orders) — the "asymmetric downside" of service/construction businesses means costs only ever go up from initial estimate, never down, unless client requests added scope.
