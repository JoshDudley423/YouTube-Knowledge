# Can't Code? The Best Way to Start Making Money Online
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4IETgMZy1o0 | Published: 2025-09-15

Interview with Rob Walling — bootstrapped SaaS founder (built and sold Drip, an email/marketing automation company, in 2016 while sending ~50M emails/month), runs TinySeed (a bootstrapped-startup accelerator/fund, $45M deployed across ~204 companies) and MicroConf, and has invested in 220+ small software startups. Focus: the "Stairstep Method" for non-technical people to build toward a software/service business without funding, plus a framework for validating and pricing SaaS ideas.

## The "Stairstep Method" (core framework)
- Thesis: don't start by building a SaaS — that's "step three" and is genuinely hard, especially non-technical. Instead build something small first that "buys out your time from your day job."
- Step one: a simple, low-complexity product/service in a tight niche that may cap out at $5,000-$10,000/month — that's fine, because it's below the radar of big competitors and less dependent on highly competitive traffic channels. Target: **~$10,000/month**, roughly the point most people can quit their day job — after that, "you have infinite time" to attack a bigger idea.
- Rob's own path: freelanced nights/weekends (while working construction) to save capital, then bought a small one-time-purchase software product (not even a SaaS, 2005-2008) and grew a couple of these to ~$4,000/month combined, then bought/grew an SEO keyword tool in 2011 from $1,500/month to $30,000/month running it solo, and used capital + experience from those to self-fund building Drip (spent $200,000 of his own money) rather than raising venture money.
- Case study — Castos founder (podcast hosting SaaS): started a productized service called "Podcast Motor" (podcast editing/production, tiered pricing $500-$5,000+/month depending on episode length/count/video-vs-audio) while working a medtech sales day job; grew it to ~$30,000/month over a couple years using Upwork-sourced editors and project managers, doing the sales himself; then acquired a small WordPress plugin in the same space and eventually turned that into "Castos," now a 7-figure SaaS company.
- Case study — "Dave Rodenba" (friend of Rob's): bought and revamped a WordPress plugin (a Craigslist-style classifieds plugin) from $500/month to $3,000-4,000/month, then repeated with a second and third plugin (one was a job-board plugin); combined the three to ~$20,000/month, quit his consulting day job, hired out support/development, freeing ~35-40 hours/week to then build a SaaS with "infinite runway."
- Explicit caveat: this approach takes longer than jumping straight into a VC-style SaaS attempt, but has a much higher chance of success; Rob frames the "overnight success" stories heard on podcasts as survivorship bias, and breaks success into hard work + luck + skill (you can't control luck, but you can control the other two).

## App/marketplace "add-on" businesses as a Step-One vehicle
- Concrete tactic: build small add-on apps/plugins for existing platform marketplaces (Shopify App Store, HubSpot App Marketplace, Zoom App Marketplace, Heroku add-ons, Chrome Web Store, WordPress plugin directory, etc.) — Rob's team maintains "a list of 75 app marketplaces like that" for this exact purpose (searchable via "microconf app marketplaces").
- Why these work as a starting point: they're typically only hundreds (sometimes low thousands) of lines of code — genuinely vibe-codable — and almost all customer acquisition comes from optimizing your listing within the marketplace's own app-store search/ranking (i.e., you skip needing to learn broad marketing, domain/hosting setup, support infrastructure, etc. that a full standalone SaaS requires).
- How to find supply/demand imbalances within these marketplaces: look at search volume/demand for a given feature category vs. how many existing apps serve it (e.g., "8,000 people searching for X type of Zoom app but only 3 apps serve it" vs. a saturated category with 30 competing apps). No dedicated tool for this cross-marketplace research exists today (unlike Ahrefs for Google SEO) — Rob suggests this itself could be a content/community business opportunity for someone willing to manually research and share findings across all 75 marketplaces.
- Rob's own current course on this exact subject: "SaaS Launchpad" (does not go into per-marketplace tactical depth because the specifics change too fast).

## What is/isn't "vibe-codable"
- Analogy given: vibe coding is like building a simple outhouse or tool shed with no construction experience — doable. Building a two-bedroom house (i.e., a complex, scalable SaaS) is not something you can wing without real expertise.
- Vibe-coding/no-code is appropriate for: single-feature tools, low-throughput/low-user-count internal tools (Rob's own team has built ~3 internal line-of-business apps in Airtable this way), or apps serving only a handful of users where UX/reliability doesn't need to be polished.
- Not appropriate for: high-throughput, must-scale products (example: Drip was sending 50M emails/month at exit — "you could not vibe code that, it would not work") or anything needing tens of thousands of concurrent users with production-grade reliability.
- With AI-assisted coding, Rob estimates the "200 hours to build an MVP" stage of his validation framework (see below) can sometimes shrink to as little as a few days — his recommended response isn't to skip validation, but to use the freed-up time to test many more concepts (e.g., 20 ideas instead of 1) rather than assuming AI will run the whole business autonomously.

## Idea validation: the "2/20/200" framework
1. **2 hours** (often less, ~20-30 min) per idea: online research only — SEO keyword tools, Reddit threads, (Quora flagged as now too spammy to be useful) to gauge whether anyone is actually searching for/complaining about this problem. Can screen 5-10 ideas this way.
2. **20 hours** (spread over ~a month, e.g. 5 hrs/week) for your top pick(s): (a) put up a landing page (tool recommended: Carrd, ~$50/year for 25 sites; also mentions Unbounce/LeadPages) with an email capture and drive some traffic to it (organic posts, podcast mentions, or paid ads) to see if anyone opts in; landing pages alone are noisy signal (no signups doesn't tell you *why*), so (b) follow up with actual conversations — cold outreach on LinkedIn offering to pay someone's consulting rate for an hour of their time, or emailing people who did opt into the landing page to learn their actual budget/need.
3. **200 hours**: build an MVP — Rob notes this number is less fixed now that AI/no-code tools can compress it dramatically, creating "a decision point" that didn't used to exist: if you can get a working app into, say, the Shopify App Store in ~3 days, it may not be worth doing the earlier 2/20-hour stages at all.

## SaaS pricing/growth strategy guidance
- Pick a lane deliberately: either (a) a low-price ($20-$100/month), higher-churn "lifestyle business" that you can build relatively quickly and don't expect to scale into millions, or (b) price at $750-$1,000+/month minimum if you want the business to be able to afford real customer acquisition spend.
- Reasoning: at ~$20/month average revenue per account, only about 4 of "20 known B2B SaaS marketing channels" (referenced from Rob's book "SaaS Playbook") are financially viable (e.g., built-in virality, founder-driven content/SEO). At $1,000/month ARPA, ~14 of the 20 channels become affordable; at $2,500/month, essentially all 20 (including in-person events/trade shows) become viable, because you can afford real CAC.
- Three market-shape categories Rob uses: **horizontal SaaS** (any business can use it — e.g. Notion, QuickBooks) — harder to grow/bootstrap due to intense competition and market saturation, tends to plateau; **vertical SaaS** (built for one specific industry, e.g. gym/dojo software) — typically less competition, and often shows lower churn / net-negative churn; **"orthogonal" SaaS** (Rob's own coined term) — a horizontally-usable tool sold to a specific buyer role/title regardless of industry (example: "CodeSubmit," a developer-hiring assessment tool bought by heads of recruiting/engineering across any company) — knowing the exact buyer title is treated as a real advantage similar to a vertical focus.
- Suggested go-to-market pattern ("zoom-in pivot"): launch horizontally to any customer in a broad category (e.g., a generic CRM marketed to all home service businesses), then observe which specific vertical shows disproportionately low churn or over-represents in your signups (e.g., discovering foundation-repair companies convert/retain unusually well), and re-brand/re-market specifically to that vertical once the data is clear. Contrasted with the opposite motion, a "zoom-out pivot" (start narrow — e.g. realtors — then expand to adjacent verticals like mortgage brokers/lawyers once product-market fit is proven). Rob has seen both directions work, and has also seen companies intentionally fire/turn away an underperforming vertical (example: a company decided auto mechanics were their worst-fit customer type and stopped serving them).

## "Customer pain vs. competitor pain" framework
- Nearly every viable market forces a trade-off between two kinds of pain: **customer pain** (serving a less technical audience — e.g. realtors, construction workers — that needs heavy sales hand-holding, onboarding, and ongoing support) vs. **competitor pain** (entering a crowded, highly competitive space, as Rob did with Drip against ~498 competing marketing-automation tools).
- Businesses with *both* kinds of pain (crowded market + hard-to-support non-technical customers) are the worst combination and should generally be avoided/not invested in.
- A rare "neither" case can look like genuine product-market fit / a "blue ocean" — cited historical examples: early iPhone/mobile-app gold rush, early crypto/Web3, and (per Rob) currently early AI-native products — but Rob explicitly warns not to hold out searching for this perfect gap: "don't not start a business because you can't find that perfect thing." He references an "18 criteria" checklist for evaluating SaaS ideas (from his own writing) and says he's never seen a real business satisfy all 18 — perfectionism/need for certainty is described as fundamentally anti-entrepreneurial.

## Data-driven customer discovery example (host's own anecdote, not Rob's)
- Chris Koerner's own iPhone-repair-parts wholesale business: in 2012, before launching, he scraped Google Maps for a full CSV list of every iPhone repair store in the US (~21,000 stores), manually deduplicated it (multi-monitor, no automation), and used that proprietary database to cold-call stores selling wholesale parts — no SaaS, just phone outreach. Reported results: $30,000 revenue month 1, $150,000 by month 3, growing into an eight-figure business — attributed to ~3 days of unglamorous data-aggregation work that gave him a market map nobody else had.

## Mindset notes
- Rob: most people fail not from lack of ability but from (a) waiting to be told what to do (a learned habit from school systems) rather than making decisions under incomplete information, (b) not believing an opportunity is possible because no one told them or they haven't seen someone else do it, and (c) being unwilling to put in unglamorous grinding hours (e.g., Rob describes spending 40 hours manually researching something that "should" have taken an hour, and treating that extra labor as his actual competitive edge).
- Framing on "the grind": both hosts argue you don't need to enjoy every moment of the work itself — enjoying the anticipated payoff/goal is enough to sustain the effort, though ideally you find some genuine satisfaction in the process itself.
