# This Kid Found the Fastest Way to Make Money With AI Apps
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oaXnY1YExjE | Published: 2026-07-19

Interview with George (19-year-old, college wrestler at St. John's/formerly), who built ~$225,000 in revenue over 9 months creating AI-powered mobile apps with no coding experience, using the "vibe coding" app builder Rork. Extremely dense, tactical playbook for niche mobile app monetization.

## Background / how he got started
- High school: ran multiple failed/small ventures (several YouTube channels, a laser-pointer dropshipping store, a nootropics supplement pill brand made via contract manufacturing/co-packing).
- Built his first app, "Fight AI" (MMA training analysis: upload a sparring/training video, get a breakdown of strengths/weaknesses/form), using Rork — an AI app builder specifically for native mobile (Swift/iOS) apps, akin to Replit but for mobile.
- Priced Fight AI at $10/month or $60/year. Promoted via a brand-new Instagram account posting a few casual videos — got 30,000-40,000 views on a brand-new account with zero existing followers, converting to ~$500 MRR at first, then growth via sponsoring niche "meme page" influencers.
- Inspiration: a high school friend, Zach, had built a fitness app that was acquired by MyFitnessPal — seeing a peer succeed was a direct trigger to go "all in" on apps instead of the supplement business (which had zero margin on paid ads).
- Started full-time on this the summer after high school graduation (~July 2025), abandoning a planned start at St. John's mentally (though he did still enroll).

## Case study: WrestleAI (his primary revenue driver)
- Rebuilt Fight AI into a wrestling-specific version after user comments showed people didn't trust an all-sports app; partnered with wrestling influencer Kaden Henshaw (one of the biggest wrestling content creators) as a 50/50 co-founder.
- Landing the partnership: cold-outreach chain — had a VA send DMs/emails through loose connections, followed up persistently (17 emails + 4 DMs), eventually got Kaden on a call, showed live conversion-rate data and a built MVP, and proposed a 50/50 partnership rather than a simple paid sponsorship — rationale given: "I was nothing without this guy" (his audience/credibility was the missing ingredient). Built the full MVP in one week of continuous prompting to seal the deal.
- Competitive incident: an existing (weaker) web-based competitor with a USA Wrestling partnership threatened legal action and tried to pressure them into an NDA/partnership; George refused to sign anything and instead directly asked Kaden if he wanted to work with them — Kaden said no, cementing the partnership. The competitor's product never gained traction and its domain (wrestle.com) is defunct.
- Launch: released WrestleAI Sept 26, ~2 weeks before wrestling season, doing ~$8,000 in its first week. Pricing: same as Fight AI ($10/mo, $60/yr), with year subscribers gated behind free-trial access (i.e., only annual plans get the free trial, pushing more customers to commit annually) — later added an "abandoned yearly" discount offer triggered when a user tries to exit the annual paywall screen.
- Monthly revenue trajectory: ~$17K (October, month 1) -> ~$19K (November) -> ~$30K (December) -> ~$40K (January, wrestling season peak, coinciding with conference championships hype) -> ~$28-30K (February, high-school-season-ending decline) -> ~$20K (March) -> ~$15K (April) -> stabilized around a "floor" of ~$10K/month (largely renewal revenue from existing annual subscribers, since new-yearly acquisition drops off in the off-season). Revenue = recurring (monthly or annual subscriptions), not one-time.
- Seasonality driver of the revenue curve: high-school wrestlers make up the largest user segment (bigger than college); once high school seasons end (~late February) and athletes "quit" for the year, new subscriber acquisition drops sharply even though existing renewal revenue persists.
- Cash-flow constraint: Apple holds payout revenue for roughly 60 days, so despite $17K in month-one revenue, he only had ~$1,000 in actual cash to reinvest in growth during the critical early period — forced disciplined, low-cost tactics until the first payout hit in December.
- Cost structure: AI/token costs were low relative to revenue — most expensive month (January) cost only ~$2,000 in AI model usage (used top-tier OpenAI models, e.g., GPT-4/4o-class). Key retention insight: the core "AI video analysis" feature (upload a match, get feedback) only drives real engagement for about the first 5 uses before users hit diminishing returns (repeat the same errors/feedback) — so they added supplementary sticky features (calorie tracker with photo-based food logging via third-party nutrition APIs/barcode databases, a weight-cut tracker tied to an uploaded match schedule/calendar, a "stance and motion" solo-drilling mode with voice call-outs for shots/sprawls, and a "practice mode" that teaches and evaluates a move) specifically to reduce churn once the core feature's novelty wore off.
- Analysis feature design philosophy: deliberately kept feedback focused on broad fundamentals (e.g., "your head was down on that double leg shot") rather than hyper-technical critique, reasoning that overly technical feedback "goes over the head" of ~90% of the wrestling audience — breadth of applicability beats depth for a mass-market feature.
- Growth channel (organic influencer marketing) — the core playbook: after the founding influencer relationship (Kaden) gave credibility, George DM'd other wrestling content creators from a branded account with a scripted pitch ("paid promo for WrestleAI" + one sentence about what the app does + compliment on their content). Response rates were high specifically because wrestling is a small, tight-knit niche and Kaden's involvement lent trust.
- Compensation model: paid influencers per-video, mostly on a flat CPM basis starting around $2/1,000 views (later up to $5+ CPM for proven top performers), paid out ~1 week after posting regardless of how long the video continues accumulating views afterward — meaning the *effective* CPM often dropped well below $1 once slow-burn/evergreen views accumulated over following weeks/months.
- First influencer deal example: 6 videos for a flat $400 (~$70/video) with creator Corey Zeeter. First video underperformed expectations for subtlety (mentioned the app 30-40 seconds in, after a separate sponsor mention) but still hit 500K views and generated over $5,000 in revenue — illustrating that even an imperfectly-executed early sponsorship can pay off massively and is worth accepting unprofitability on initial deals since successful creators refer other creators and build brand trust within the niche.
- Scaled up to roughly 10-20 sponsored creators at peak, later consolidated down to 6-7 trusted, consistently high-performing partners.
- Bottleneck: ran out of relevant influencers to sign in the wrestling niche (only "a couple hundred" wrestling content creators exist total) — floated (but hadn't yet executed) the idea of coaching a big-name-but-content-weak athlete (someone with large follower counts from athletic accolades but poor video skills) by having them submit raw footage for the team to edit and hand back for posting, to access their reach without needing them to be good at content creation themselves.
- Internal tooling: built a custom internal dashboard tracking metrics, creator contracts, and payouts, integrated with PostHog and a Claude-based "chat with your data" assistant (via MCP) to query business metrics (P&L, daily numbers, etc.) conversationally.
- Fraud/attribution note: didn't have hard fake-view detection tooling — relied on intuition/pattern recognition (e.g., emoji-heavy comment sections as a bot signal) and would drop influencers whose views weren't translating into actual sales.
- Partnership financial structure: profits distributed 50/50 to a parent LLC structure — George's own parent holding company holds a stake in each app's individual entity, with influencer/co-founder partners (like Kaden) holding the other stake in that specific app's LLC; monthly cash distributions began once each app was consistently profitable and it made sense to actually withdraw cash rather than reinvest it all.

## Two newer apps (launched roughly simultaneously, ~45 days before this interview): a boxing analysis app and a fitness/"abs rating" app
- Both scaled to $10,000/month within 45 days using the same influencer playbook *plus* Meta (Facebook/Instagram) paid ads — paid ads made growth roughly 10x faster since it removed the need to saturate an entire niche with organic influencer deals.
- Lifetime revenue at time of filming: boxing app ~$23,000, fitness/abs app ~$15,000; combined with WrestleAI's earlier numbers, total revenue across all apps reached ~$225,000 over 9 months.
- Operating model for these newer apps: George funds/directs but delegates day-to-day execution to a trusted junior partner/operator per app (a person he can give clear direction to and trust to execute), enabling him to run multiple apps with only a few hours per week of his own direct time (he was also working a separate full-time job during this period).
- Fitness/"abs rating" app origin: pitched to him unsolicited by another teenager on Twitter who wanted to niche a crowded "workout analysis" category down to just abs (take a photo of your abs, get an AI-generated rating/score). George was initially skeptical (thought it resembled low-quality "looksmaxing" apps) but backed it due to the pitcher's evident passion/conviction — comparing it to how much money has historically been made selling ab-focused fitness gear via infomercials, i.e., a proven demand category regardless of app-store crowding.
- Marketing-creative learning (fitness app): ads showing just the numeric rating/score got fast downloads and fast churn (high initial spike but low retention/low LTV); ads showing the *workout* generated by the app (i.e., demonstrating the app's actual utility, not just the gimmick) converted more slowly but drove much lower churn — direct parallel drawn to email-newsletter growth (a high open-rate list with low click-through is worse than a lower open-rate list with higher click-through; vanity top-of-funnel metrics can mask poor downstream quality).
- Apple App Store review incident: the fitness app's co-founder publicly bragged on Twitter/X about their unusually high (~20% vs. an industry-average ~5%) download-to-paid conversion rate, and in doing so posted a screenshot revealing their paywall's pricing hierarchy (a weekly price displayed above a monthly price in the same visual weight) — a stranger reported this to Apple, and the app was pulled from the App Store for two weeks, explicitly (per Apple's own phone call) not because the pricing setup itself violated guidelines, but because the public bragging embarrassed Apple. Resolved by aggressively appealing (writing a formal apology letter, drafted with Claude's help) until reinstated.
- General lesson drawn from that incident and a separate Meta ad-account 2FA lockout (resolved by mass-adding and DMing Meta employees on LinkedIn until someone fixed it): "never accept" a platform's default outcome at face value — always escalate and fight for resolution rather than assuming a locked account or rejected app is final.
- Payment/cash-flow tool: uses RevenueCat (a mobile app payment/paywall analytics platform) and specifically its "RevenueCat Payments" feature, which advances up to ~80% of monthly in-app revenue on a near-immediate basis (rather than waiting Apple's typical ~60-day payout cycle) in exchange for a ~2% fee — used explicitly to remove the cash-flow bottleneck on scaling paid ad spend, since ad budgets can be replenished same-day rather than after a 60-day lag.
- Paid ad unit economics example (boxing app): spends ~$400/day on Meta ads, generating ~$900-$1,200/day in revenue (2-4x day-one return on ad spend), with materially higher lifetime value once recurring renewals are factored in. Lets Meta's algorithm auto-select ad placement across Instagram/Facebook rather than manually targeting; notes Facebook Reels specifically drove surprisingly large volumes of traffic despite conventional wisdom that Instagram dominates.

## Zero-to-one app launch playbook (his generalized advice)
1. **Design one "gotcha" feature** that can visually explain the entire app's value in about 5 seconds — it doesn't need to be the most-used feature long-term, just the one that gets someone to install. Test: if you show your phone to someone unfamiliar with the space (his example: "your mom") and they don't immediately understand what the app does from the visual, the feature isn't simple enough.
2. **Fabricate initial social proof before outreach** (an approach he says draws criticism, but has repeatedly worked): buy Instagram followers cheaply (cited tool: "MSSMM" or similar cheap follower-seeding services, on the order of ~$1 per 1,000 followers) to reach roughly 15,000 followers, buy Meta/Instagram verification (blue check), and post 2-3 polished static product-shot images before doing any real outreach. Rationale: cold outreach into an empty-looking, zero-follower brand profile gets ignored or laughed off; a profile that already looks established and "legitimate" dramatically increases influencer response and willingness to accept a modest flat rate rather than assuming they're being lowballed by "some broke kid." View counts on these seed posts don't matter — only the appearance of activity/history does.
3. **Prioritize your first several influencer deals as collaborative posts** (using Instagram's native "collab" post feature) so your brand account's own feed visibly shows recognizable creators who've worked with you — this compounds the legitimacy effect for future outreach once the seeded-follower phase is behind you.
4. **Outreach method**: manually browse your own "For You"/algorithmic feed within the target niche, and DM anyone averaging 25,000+ views per video (not follower count — average views is the relevant filter) with a templated message that leads with "Paid promo for [app name]" as literally the first words (so it's visible in the truncated DM preview before the recipient even opens it) plus one sentence describing the app. Send as many DMs as possible; expect a low response rate and iterate on wording over time as more people adopt similar outreach templates.
5. **Hire a VA immediately after your first $1,000** in revenue (sourced via Fiverr/Upwork) — described as effectively infinite ROI, primarily to scale outreach volume.
6. **Only invest in Meta/paid ads once you have real committed budget** — his rule of thumb: don't bother with paid ads unless you're willing to commit at least ~$3,000; below that threshold, focus exclusively on the free/cheap organic influencer-outreach playbook.
7. **Test which feature drives the most reaction/conversion in organic content and paid creative, then concentrate spend behind that specific feature/message** rather than promoting the app generically — but have a hypothesis about the likely best feature going in, rather than testing totally blind.
8. **When an influencer's engagement/results start plateauing on repetitive content about your app**, introduce and promote a different feature of the app through them — this effectively "re-launches" interest as if it were a new product to their audience, extending the useful life of that creator relationship.
9. **Match creative style to the channel**: subtle, native-feeling "day in my life" style organic influencer content converts well organically but underperforms as a *paid* ad (the sponsorship message needs to be too deeply buried); paid ad creative needs an explicit call-to-action/product reveal within roughly the first 15 seconds (vs. more like 30 seconds of leeway for organic content) to convert efficiently as a cold paid ad. Tactic: pay creators purely for raw or edited *content* (without requiring them to post it themselves) specifically to produce this more overt, CTA-forward style for paid distribution, separate from the more subtle content the same creators post organically to their own following.
10. **Study the Meta Ads Library** for high-performing ad examples in your category before briefing creators on paid-specific video concepts.

## Business philosophy / mindset points
- His single biggest early mistake, in his own words, was operating from a scarcity mindset (fear of "running out" of a niche's total addressable creators/users), which caused him to under-invest by demanding disproportionately high margins/ROAS early on rather than reinvesting aggressively; he now deliberately scales spend up to a much longer CAC-payback horizon (cites being comfortable with a 90-day payback period) once he has cash reserves and doesn't urgently need the money personally.
- Sets aside personal living-expense runway (cited as roughly 6-12 months) specifically so he can reinvest business cash into growth rather than personal profit-taking in the near term.
- Explicit growth-vs-focus dilemma the interview surfaces: whether to pursue one large, deep product (further building out WrestleAI or a new higher-ticket idea, e.g., a personal health/bloodwork-analytics app akin to "Superpower" or "Function Health") vs. running a portfolio/studio of narrow niche-specific apps under one parent holding company (parallel drawn explicitly by the host to Flow Sports/FlowWrestling's real-world playbook: starting hyper-niche in one underserved sport, then expanding sport-by-sport into an eventual multi-hundred-million-dollar company). No final decision reached in the interview — George leans toward eventually running an app portfolio delegated to trusted junior operators per app, funneling profits up to a parent LLC he wholly owns, while individual app entities may have partner co-owners (e.g., 50/50 with an influencer co-founder like Kaden).
- Expects natural attrition across a multi-app portfolio (the 80/20 rule: a minority of apps will drive the majority of revenue over time) and frames regularly culling underperforming apps as normal, not a failure signal.
- Stated goal: reach $1,000,000 in total revenue before turning 20 (April 26 of the following year at time of filming).
- Used Rork specifically (an AI app builder focused on native mobile/Swift apps, similar in spirit to Replit but mobile-specific) — was later hired onto Rork's own growth team as a founding team member based on his organic advocacy for the platform, though was preparing to leave that role to focus full-time on his own app portfolio at the time of this interview.

## Caveats
- All revenue figures ($225,000 total, individual app numbers) are the guest's self-reported claims, not independently verified.
- The Instagram follower-buying and platform-gaming tactics (fake followers, purchased verification) are explicitly framed by the guest as controversial/criticized practices, not universally endorsed growth tactics.
- WrestleAI's revenue is highly seasonal and dependent on wrestling-season timing; the underlying playbook's transferability to other niches with different seasonality or influencer density is untested within this interview.
- The Apple App Store suspension example illustrates real platform risk from public bragging about pricing/paywall structure choices, even when the structure itself passes formal review.
