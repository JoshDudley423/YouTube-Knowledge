# You're Being Sold Without Knowing It (How To Copy This Trick)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uGzNhjFJYFI | Published: 2025-09-17

Interview with copywriter Neville Medhora (AppSumo's early copywriter, founder of copywritingcourse.com). Primarily a copywriting/email-marketing skills episode, relevant to any side hustle that needs to sell via text (email, landing pages, cold outreach).

## Core framework: ADA (Attention, Desire, Action... actually Attention, Interest, Desire, Action)
- The only copywriting formula Neville says he actually uses (despite dozens of others existing).
- Attention: the hook/headline.
- Interest: interesting facts, testimonials, and especially varied *use cases* for the product — bullet-pointed. Example: LED finger lights sold as a rave product also got bulk-bought by plumbing companies (light under sinks without needing a headlamp) and by parents of autistic kids (calming visual stimulus); light-up golf balls ("rave balls") got adopted by wedding planners for centerpieces ($3,000 orders) — far higher-value customers than the original dance-floor buyer. Lesson: multiply use cases in the "Interest" section to expand total addressable market to non-obvious buyer segments.
- Desire: paint the after-state ("if you buy this, your life will be better because...").
- Action: explicit instruction on how to buy/act now.
- Applies to almost any sales pitch (emails, landing pages, cold outreach, even convincing a kid to clean their room); doesn't apply to very short-form content (a single tweet) or non-sales writing (a novel, a purely informational local newsletter).

## Applying ADA to email/business results (concrete case studies)
- First AppSumo email Neville wrote (selling a font-pairing subscription to designers) turned a previously ~$1,000-profit-per-email list into a $10,000 profit day — a 10x result — by explaining the product and use cases instead of just showing pictures/buy buttons.
- His own e-commerce brand (House of Rave) was making no money on a "warm" list of 7,500 past customers using pretty, image-heavy emails; after rewriting emails in plain, direct "Gary Halbert style" copy, it became more of an email marketing company than an SEO-dependent e-commerce store overnight.
- A different e-commerce brand (Texas Snacks, reselling Buc-ee's products, texasnacks.com) with a 160,000-person list found that stripped-down, all-text, ugly-hyperlink emails ("New Halloween shirt just dropped" — link only, no images) outperform image-heavy emails; short subject lines that don't reveal the payoff also perform better (drives curiosity/clicks).
- Alternative format suggested: "headline + picture, repeated" style emails (short punchy H1 + image, repeated 6-10 times) — reads like a long scrollable story despite containing very little actual text; used successfully for flash sales (example: a "Best Self Journal" flash sale).
- Cold email strategy: send three separate emails in sequence — short (almost uncomfortably short, reads as human-written, e.g. AppSumo's CEO pitching "we have 700,000 customers who want to buy your software"), medium, then long — because if the short one doesn't get a response, sending progressively more detail increases odds of eventually landing a reply.
- Newsletter cadence rule: 70% content / 30% sales lets you email a list as often as you want without fatigue/complaints (this was AppSumo's internal rule for sending a sale every single day without unsubscribes).

## Business model example: running a copywriting course/community as a side hustle
- Neville runs "copywritingcourse.com" — beyond course videos, the core value is a community forum where members post their actual emails/social posts/YouTube scripts and Neville + his writers critique them directly (positioned as more valuable than the course content itself), plus live office hours.
- Implies a repeatable service-business model: sell an educational product bundled with ongoing feedback/consulting access as the real value driver (subscription/community model), rather than a static course.

## Practical AI-copywriting tip
- Use ChatGPT/Grok "Projects" (persistent context feature) rather than re-explaining your brand each time: upload your website copy, past case studies, images, PDF decks once; then every generated email/ad can pull real numbers and real case studies instead of generic filler.

## Other observations / caveats
- Old-school long, "salesy" language and long-form sales pages are described as declining in effectiveness; plain, personal, conversational writing style ("just talking") now outperforms polished/formal copy, partly because audiences now see a brand/person across many touchpoints (social, YouTube, podcast) rather than needing one page to close the sale — meaning fewer, shorter pages are increasingly viable versus the ultra-long sales pages common ~10-15 years ago.
- Long-winded is bad; simply "long" content is fine if every part earns its place.
- A hand-drawn/"imperfect," clearly-not-AI-generated visual style (rough sketches vs. polished Canva/AI-generated graphics) is called out as a current differentiation advantage precisely because it signals human effort in an AI-saturated content environment.
- Warning about feedback blindness: customers/subscribers rarely tell you when your content/copy is bad — they just quietly stop reading/unsubscribe — so an email list or content channel can decay for years without obvious warning signs; treat any negative feedback you do get as a valuable, above-average-informative signal even from a tiny sample.
- Historical color/caution: aggressive direct-mail tactics (e.g., Gary Halbert's 1970s "coat of arms" mass-mailing scheme, ~600 million pieces mailed) generated huge profit but also drew enough complaints to result in the sender going to prison for mail fraud — a reminder that scaling an edgy or exploitative angle to enough volume creates real legal/regulatory risk, even at a low per-recipient complaint rate.
