# Buc-ee's Viral Marketing Stunt
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l1RVNhFu058 | Published: 2024-08-11

Chris Koerner tells the origin story of Texas Snax (texassnax.com), a business he and his cousin Kirk built by reselling Buc-ee's (Texas gas-station/convenience chain) products online — a case study in creating an "unofficial partnership" via a publicity stunt, later formalized into a legitimate, growing e-commerce business.

## The business idea
- Noticed that Buc-ee's, a hugely popular Texas gas station/convenience chain, had no online store despite obvious brand demand — an opportunity "hiding in plain sight."
- After cold-emailing the founders repeatedly with no response, launched an unauthorized but legal resale website ("Beaver Snacks," later renamed "Texas Snax") selling Buc-ee's products bought at retail and shipped to customers nationwide.
- Legal basis: the First Sale Doctrine — anyone can resell a legitimately purchased product (like reselling a used iPhone) as long as they don't create confusion about being the original brand/manufacturer.
- Explicit strategy: engineer media/PR attention (a "viral publicity stunt") to force the brand's attention and hopefully convert into a formal partnership, rather than simply asking permission first.

## Numbers
- Initial investment: ~$3,000 worth of Buc-ee's snacks purchased at retail, plus $300-400 for a professional product photographer (hired via Thumbtack).
- First 30-45 days after media coverage broke (timed against Thanksgiving/Christmas): did almost $200,000 in sales.
- Current state (per host, without exact figures): net profit margins of 10-20% (roughly ~15% depending on discounts/paid ads), doing "millions of dollars a year," growing 30-70% year over year.
- Sales reportedly doubled every time Buc-ee's opened a new physical store location.

## Process / steps
- Cold-emailed the two Buc-ee's founders directly first — got no response.
- Researched the legal footing (First Sale Doctrine) before proceeding, specifically to confirm they wouldn't be sued for trademark/brand confusion.
- Bought ~$3,000 of product, hired a professional photographer, and built a cheap/ugly initial website under a domain that referenced the "Beaver" mascot (BeaverSnacks/x.com) rather than the Buc-ee's name directly.
- "Soft launched" via a Reddit post — got a modest first sale (a jar of pickled quail eggs) and some engagement, but no real virality from that alone.
- Proactively cold-emailed journalists/reporters (Nov 2020) pitching the "guy secretly selling Buc-ee's snacks online" story; a Texas Monthly reporter picked it up and reached out to Buc-ee's for comment before publishing.
- This triggered contact from Buc-ee's own lawyer, who — rather than shutting it down — required three specific changes: (1) remove "Beaver" from the business name (renamed to Texas Snax), (2) add a disclaimer inside the logo, displayed on every page, and (3) add the same disclaimer to every individual product description.
- After making those changes, the story ran in Texas Monthly and several other Texas outlets, driving a large, immediate sales spike (~$200k in the first 30-45 days), amplified by lucky timing against the holiday shopping season.
- Leveraged pre-existing operational infrastructure: Koerner and his cousin already ran an e-commerce food fulfillment company (Send Eats) with warehouse space and shipping systems, though managing Buc-ee's thousands of SKUs and hundreds of different expiration dates (to avoid shipping expired product and risking legal liability) required building new processes on the fly.
- Eventually made a hard pivot: realized their larger e-commerce fulfillment clients (paying $200-300k/month) were actually less profitable overall than the smaller-margin-per-order-but-higher-value Buc-ee's resale business; fired all other fulfillment clients, laid off some staff, broke their warehouse lease early (leading to a landlord dispute), downsized to a smaller warehouse, and went all-in on the Buc-ee's resale business — described as "the best decision we ever could have made."

## Tools / platforms named
- Thumbtack (used to hire the product photographer).
- Reddit (initial soft-launch/awareness channel).
- Cold email outreach to journalists as a deliberate PR/growth tactic.
- texassnax.com (the resulting legitimate business).

## Caveats / risks
- Legally risky by design at the outset — deliberately operated in a gray area relying on First Sale Doctrine protection, and explicitly prepared for the worst-case scenario (a cease-and-desist or lawsuit) before launching; got lucky that Buc-ee's lawyer was cooperative rather than adversarial.
- Buc-ee's itself had considered building e-commerce but rejected it because they felt they'd need to charge more online than in-store to make the economics work, and didn't want to upset their loyal in-store customer base or give people a reason not to visit physical locations — a structural reason the opportunity existed for someone else to fill, not a guaranteed opportunity for other big consumer brands.
- Managing a huge, fast-changing SKU catalog with variable expiration dates was operationally difficult and risky (shipping expired food could create legal exposure) and took real infrastructure to solve.
- Required an existing e-commerce fulfillment operation (warehouse, logistics know-how) to execute at the scale the PR spike created — not a zero-infrastructure side hustle.
- The pivot required painful decisions: cutting profitable-looking-on-paper large clients, layoffs, and breaking a lease — illustrating that recognizing a better opportunity sometimes requires abandoning a larger-revenue but lower-margin existing business.
- Framed explicitly as an "asymmetric bet" / "white whale" opportunity: risking a small, defined amount ($3,000) for a shot at an outsized outcome — the approach is presented as a repeatable pattern (look for a specific company/market with an obvious unmet need) rather than a one-off lucky break.
