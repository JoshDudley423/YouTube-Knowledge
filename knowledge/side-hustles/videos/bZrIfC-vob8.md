# Everyone's Chasing AI. Start This Simple Business Instead
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bZrIfC-vob8 | Published: 2025-07-10

Overall thesis: the hot tub industry is a >$5B/year market, and the real opportunity isn't selling hot tubs but the "businesses around the business" (inspection, covers, moving, rental, maintenance). As more entrepreneurs chase AI businesses, non-AI "sweaty startup" services like these face less competition and rising prices — AI "is never going to lift that hot tub off the deck."

## 5. Hot tub inspection services
- Problem: general home inspectors routinely exclude hot tubs from inspection (not their specialty), leaving buyers exposed to costly surprises (example: a Reddit case of a hot tub marked "good working condition" that actually needed $4,000 in repairs — dead pump, cracked shell).
- Case study: Jersey Hot Tub Services charges $200 for a standalone inspection, $40-75 as an add-on to a home inspection, $500 for combined hot-tub-plus-pool inspection.
- Go-to-market: partner with general home inspectors as a referral/subcontract arrangement — inspector charges homeowner an extra ~$400 for a hot tub add-on, keeps $100 as referral fee, passes $300 to the specialist.
- Startup costs: ~$500 total (test kit ~$250, GFCI checker ~$50, basic tools ~$200), plus unexpected extras.
- Suggested lead source: real estate agents (touch many transacting properties regularly).

## 4. Hot tub covers
- Origin case study: Ocean Spa Covers founder ("Babul") arrived in the US with $10, worked as a pool tech, saved $1,200 in 1991 to buy a sewing machine, made custom covers from a garage; got a $2009 industrial cutting machine that cut production time from 1 hour to 10 minutes/cover; now a multi-million-dollar business with a 2-3 week turnaround.
- Modern approach recommended (no sewing machine needed): use AI design tools (Canva, VO3, Sora, Grok, ChatGPT) to generate 10-20 polarizing/attention-grabbing cover designs; build a Shopify store (~$39/month theme); run Facebook ads to all designs and reallocate budget toward the lowest cost-per-click winners; source manufacturing via Alibaba/AliExpress with direct-to-consumer shipping (no warehouse/logistics needed) — collect payment, forward the order to the Chinese manufacturer, they ship directly to the customer.
- Target unit economics: LTV:CAC ratio around 5:1 (e.g., if customer acquisition costs $100 via ads, the product should sell for around $500).
- Custom covers retail $300-$1,000+, need replacement every 4-5 years (built-in repeat purchase cycle if you capture customer email/phone for remarketing).
- Case study on differentiation: spacovers.com founder, started at 19, competed purely on 2-week shipping vs. competitors' 4-week shipping (same quality) — literally strapped covers to the roof of his Honda Accord to start.
- Caveat: "the days of easy drop shipping are long gone" — a pure dropship approach will hit defects, wrong sizes, misprints; consider a hybrid model where you handle more of the process but still outsource logistics/manufacturing.

## 3. Hot tub moving and relocation
- Two distinct revenue lines observed in one real business: (1) hot tub cleaning for Airbnb property managers, (2) hot tub moving for spa/hot tub retailers needing old units removed and new ones delivered.
- Case study: Charles (Quebec) started as a general household mover, noticed niche hot-tub-only movers charged as much ($750-900) for just the tub as he charged for an entire house move; gradually pivoted (not a hard pivot — just started asking movers "do you have a hot tub?" and quoting that separately) into hot tub moving, then began buying/hauling away unwanted hot tubs advertised free on Facebook Marketplace, eventually building a full retail operation (CK Spas).
- Reported average hot tub move: $600, taking 2-3 hours; 5 moves/day = $2,500/day gross; net profit margin to the owner cited at 35-45% (most costs are fixed overhead).
- Other case studies: Big Beard Movers (Houston) charges $500/move plus per-mile rate over 75 miles; Utah Tub and Spa Movers runs 3 crews, 6-8 moves/day at $350-650 each, reported 2024 revenue over $4 million.
- Startup costs: spa dolly $3,000-5,000 (financeable), insurance ~$3,000/year, straps/equipment ~$1,000 — roughly $10,000 all-in to fully equip. Validate first with Facebook Marketplace ads, Google ads, and Facebook groups before buying equipment.

## 2. Hot tub rental business
- Case study: Steve Nadraia, a Long Island high school teacher, previously made $10K/summer lifeguarding. Bought 3 used hot tubs for $6,500 total; rents weekend ($200-400 depending on model), weekly ($269), monthly ($499). Initial cost recouped within a couple months.
- Scaled to 25+ used hot tubs; even at 50% capacity, generates $12-15K/month gross revenue, nets $7-9K/month after delivery and chemical costs — while still working his teaching job full time. Wife handles scheduling, he handles delivery.
- Framed ROI comparison: even one tub at 50% capacity nets over $10,000/year — over 50% annual return on the ~$2,000-2,500 unit cost, compared to typical rental real estate returns of 9-11%/year.
- Being first mover in a local market (dominating local search) was cited as a major advantage.

## 1. Airbnb hot tub cleaning/maintenance service (the video's top pick)
- Case study: Austin (Colorado, originally a land-clearing business owner) started this to fill seasonal downtime, marketing only to Airbnb owners. Spent $750 on a website (3 years ago; near-$0 today with AI website builders) plus a couple hundred dollars for an LLC and ~$1,000 in chemicals. First month's recurring revenue: $2,000+ (recouped startup costs almost immediately); some one-time service calls exceed $900, generated with zero paid ads.
- Second case study (Reddit): spent $30 on a logo, $250 on a generic service contract (today doable free via ChatGPT), $220 on hoses/chemicals; landed 3 clients from a single local Facebook group post, clearing $3,000+ net profit in month one. By month 7, getting multiple quotes daily; identified a local TAM of 1,700+ hot tubs within a 25-mile radius. Pricing used: $45-65 for weekly chemical balancing, $199 for drain-and-fill, $100/month maintenance plans; at 40 hot tubs/week this models to $7-8K/month gross.
- Third case study: Davis (Chicago) built an Airbnb-only hot tub maintenance company, 3 years in, 100+ units under management, $1M+ annualized revenue, 17 employees. Uses Zapier + Slack (~$30/month) for automation; buys chemicals in bulk; guarantees Airbnb-level cleanliness.
- Fourth case study: Spa Service Company (Vermont) services 30-40 hot tubs/day, 4 days/week, charging $45-65/week (summer) or $65-75/week (winter) — roughly $9-10K/week in revenue.
- Why this is #1: strong recurring revenue and aligned incentives — a hot tub can add $40-100/night to an Airbnb's rate, or up to $1-2K/month extra booking income at 20 nights/month; normal Airbnb cleaners lack the skill for hot tub maintenance, so owners will pay for a specialist even though it's a separate line item.
- Suggested validation pitch to Airbnb owners without a hot tub: propose a short trial period (e.g., one month) advertising the property as having a hot tub to see if bookings/pricing improve, letting data (not a sales pitch) justify the investment.

## General launch playbook (applies across all 5 ideas)
- Start via low-cost/no-cost local Facebook groups (every town has several; ~10-20% are active/useful, the rest are spam-filled — filter accordingly). Post polls (including Instagram polls) to gauge actual local demand for cleaning vs. covers vs. moving vs. repair before committing.
- Ask prospective customers directly what's breaking/missing in their current setup — let real answers direct which of the 5 sub-niches to pursue locally.
- Be transparent with early customers that you're still building out the offering; trade discounted/free first jobs for testimonials and reviews.
- Set up a Google Business Profile immediately (photos, videos, 5-star reviews, hours, website link).
- Build a simple one-page website (contact info, services, pricing) using AI website builders (Bolt, Replit/"Replicable" mentioned, Windsurf) — don't overbuild it.
- Don't incorporate until you have real customers; once validated, use a service like Bizee (referred to as "Bizzy" in transcript) rather than dealing with state incorporation sites directly.
- Track expenses in a simple Google Sheet using a personal bank account initially; don't bother with a dedicated business account or QuickBooks/Xero until revenue justifies it.
- Budget for insurance: roughly $2,000-3,000/year across these niches (described as readily available).
- Use Facebook ads to target people actively planning trips/vacations to the area for the rental business specifically.
- General caution: don't overbuy inventory/chemicals/equipment early — start lean, avoid getting "shop happy."
