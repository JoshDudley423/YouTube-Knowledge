# How to Import Products from China with Almost No Money
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y6leozSnT0E | Published: 20240423

Q&A/AMA-format episode (Chris Koerner answering listener-submitted questions). Despite the title, it's a broad small-business advice episode; only one segment is specifically about China importing. Notes below organized by question.

## Getting first customers for a new local service business (gutter cleaning example)
- The main blocker to getting first jobs is pride/ego, not lack of demand — friends/family already pay someone to do these jobs (gutter cleaning, pressure washing, detailing, lawn mowing).
- Concrete tactic: call friends/family individually (not text), frame it as "I'm testing this new business, want feedback/testimonials," offer 20–50% off for the first jobs.
- After the job, explicitly ask for referrals. Cited example: an Australian founder built an **$8M window-washing company** starting only from friends/family referrals and word of mouth — the logic: each person knows 300-400 people; if even 1% refer, 20 friends → 60 referrals → 180 referrals, compounding.

## Bookkeeping tools for gig/freelance work
- Recommended: **Xero** or **QuickBooks** (both scale from $1 to $100M+ revenue, integrate with bank accounts). Alternative for very early stage: Google Sheets/Excel.

## Avoiding sales pipeline drying up
- If your business depends on only a handful of open quotes, you're too reliant on inbound/word-of-mouth.
- Need constant outbound "hooks in the water": Google Business Profile optimization, review requests, cold email campaigns (always-on autopilot), cold text campaigns, ringless voicemail campaigns, mailers, door knocking, door hangers, Facebook/Google/Etsy ads. Don't rely on SEO alone (it's changing/unreliable).

## Finding a technical (React) co-founder
- Look on Twitter, Reddit (specific subreddits), Indie Hackers, Hacker News — build a public reputation/comment history there first.
- Alternative unconventional approach: hire a developer via Upwork (e.g., in Bangladesh) for paid work first; if he's responsive/hardworking, offer him a co-founder role later.
- Structuring idea: co-founders can share a Stripe account and split revenue directly ("trustless" split) rather than a formal cap table.

## Retaining good employees in a low-margin business
- Example: his e-commerce fulfillment (3PL/"pick-pack-ship") business — competed with Amazon's 23 DFW fulfillment centers paying $17-18/hr while he could only pay $12-13/hr.
- Explains the "messy middle" concept: a business too small to be a cheap high-volume niche player and too big/unfocused to charge premium boutique prices — avoid landing here.
- Margin context: this 3PL business was a $4-6M topline company; supplements (80-90% gross margins, shelf-stable, shippable) are a much better category to be a 3PL for than food (thin margins, low % of overall sales shipped online).
- Compensation workaround for low-margin businesses: offer perks with high perceived value but low actual cost — e.g., letting employees listen to podcasts/music at work, free weekly lunch, paying for Netflix/Hulu/YouTube Premium subscriptions (~$50/month cost but perceived as much more valuable than a $50 raise).
- If you can't afford to hire proven "rockstar" talent, hire promising but affordable people and mold them over time (pay market rate, expect more managerial effort/frustration up front).

## Raising money in a tough economy
- "Dry powder" narratives don't apply evenly — funding is easy if you already have a track record (e.g., in real estate with a fund and history); hard/impossible without one.
- If you can't raise capital: ask friends/family, or pick a lower-capital business idea. Many service businesses require near-zero capital — learn a skill from YouTube and start a service business chargeable at $500–$5,000/month.

## Increasing predictable cash flow / when to hire a VA
- Highest-leverage action for predictable revenue: build a personal brand and an **email list** in your niche — email can't be taken away from you (unlike social platforms).
- Concrete example: his Texas Snacks business (resells Buc-ee's products) has a 130,000-person email list; a single email blast reliably generates ~$12,000 in sales (range: ~$6,000 for niche items up to ~$25,000 for seasonal/hard-to-find items). Took 4 years to build that list.
- Hire a VA (virtual assistant, overseas, reduced rate) once you're swamped with tasks/revenue — don't hire too early (a more common mistake than hiring too late). Use Loom (7-15 min recordings) to offload training/knowledge transfer instead of repeating yourself.

## Importing products from China cheaply (the titled topic)
- Ask vendors for broker referrals; look specifically for brokers who charge a **percentage-basis fee** (not flat fee) on your total order.
- Prefer "one-stop shop" brokers in China who can negotiate shipping, manage all customs/duties/forms, and also help source additional vendors — a 2-6% fee is worth it for this bundled service.
- Process: DM ~20 vendors on AliExpress/Alibaba, order samples, narrow to 3-4 finalists, ask them for broker referrals, then ask that broker if they'll also help find more vendors (more vendor options = better pricing leverage).
- Use **multiple brokers (2-3) and multiple vendors** so you can compare pricing and avoid a broker quietly eating margin if market prices drop (a single broker has no incentive to pass savings to you once you don't know current market rates).
- Once you know market prices, brokers can't meaningfully overcharge you.

## Doubling down on an exhausting current business vs. a more enjoyable side business
- If you're asking whether to chase a new idea, that's itself the signal you should test the new idea — "shiny object syndrome is a feature not a bug."
- His own example: staying with his failing 3PL business (stuck at 4-5% net margin) would have been a mistake; chasing other ideas is what let him find better opportunities.
- Framing: compounding rewards endurance, not focus — keep moving/testing, don't worry about "loyalty" to one idea.

## How much risk to take on while still employed
- "Test everything except drugs" / "don't let go of one railing before grabbing the next" (wing-walker's code, referenced from a previous episode).
- While still employed, test multiple startup ideas in parallel (home service, SaaS, acquiring a small business) until one clearly proves itself a "win."

## Self-employment taxes vs W-2 friends
- Getting a refund doesn't mean a W-2 friend paid less tax overall — it means they overpaid throughout the year (an interest-free loan to the government).
- Small business owners should pay quarterly estimated taxes. Get a dedicated tax professional; don't compare your tax burden to W-2 earners — self-employment carries unlimited upside that W-2 income doesn't.

## Career change advice for a college coach (40, wants to leave collegiate sports)
- Recommends a **home-service business** — leverages people/team-management/systems skills. Explicitly advises against real estate (lonely, capital-intensive, not people-oriented) and against software/content businesses (same reason).
- Suggests broad, high-referral-frequency verticals (fencing, house painting, cement work) over niche/low-frequency ones (roofing, pools) since broad categories generate more referrals from an engaged local community.
- Exercise: list what you loved/hated about past ventures with friends/family, compare side by side, then brainstorm verticals with ChatGPT based on the pattern.

## Carpet and floor care business
- Not a naturally recurring business — don't force a fake recurring/subscription model onto it (if a natural recurring angle existed, someone would already be doing it).
- Better to **buy** an existing carpet/floor care business (a typical "boomer-owned," undercharging, under-marketed business with word-of-mouth-only growth) rather than start one from scratch, unless you can run it as a lean sub-contracted operation (compares to his tree-trimming business model — you're a "lien factory" subbing all labor to a crew).
- Generally prefers other home-service niches over opening a storefront/inventory-heavy business.

## Validating lead-gen leads sold to businesses (e.g., legal industry car-crash leads)
- Don't worry upfront about verifying close rates — you don't yet know if your leads are even good.
- Strategy: sell leads very cheap initially to get businesses "addicted" (aim for a 20-50x ROI for them) — once they're hooked and asking for more leads, you have pricing leverage (can raise prices, or resell the same lead to multiple buyers if disclosed).
- Be relentless about follow-up: maintain a shared Google Sheet and pester clients to keep it updated with lead outcomes — without this, you'll never know true close rates, and clients are prone to undercounting/underreporting when asked to self-report for billing purposes.
