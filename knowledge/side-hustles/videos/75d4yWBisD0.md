# Bookkeeping Basics for Beginners
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=75d4yWBisD0 | Published: 2025-02-28

Short clip answering a viewer question (Austin) about bookkeeping/accounting when starting a first company. Not a business idea video — operational/back-office advice applicable to any side hustle in this knowledge base.

## Chris's three-threshold bookkeeping framework
1. **Threshold 1 — Google Sheets, using your personal bank account**: When first validating a business idea, don't set up an LLC or business bank account yet. Track revenue/expenses in a simple Google Sheet, exported from your personal checking/bank account. Recommended banks: Mercury (preferred) or Bank of America (Bank of America integrates with Zelle, which matters if you pay subcontractors via Zelle; Mercury does not support Zelle).
2. **Trigger to move to a real business setup**: once revenue confirms "this is going to be a thing," (a) file an LLC and get an EIN (tax ID number), then (b) open a business bank account (preferably Mercury) using those documents. True up the Google Sheet totals from the personal account into the new business account, then run everything through the business account from that point forward — strict separation from then on.
3. **Avoid credit card rewards optimization when running multiple businesses**: Chris explicitly avoids chasing 2% cash back / Amex points cards for business spending (even significant spend, e.g., $30K/month on Facebook ads) once he has multiple businesses, because managing many businesses' worth of separate credit cards, debit cards, bank accounts, Venmo, and PayPal accounts becomes an operational nightmare. He says if he only had one business, he probably would use a rewards card (mentions Capital One Venture as an example).
4. **Threshold 2 — hire a bookkeeper once the business is real**: Don't personally manage QuickBooks long-term even though it's not hard to use — it's not a good use of a founder's time (better spent on sales/marketing). A good bookkeeper (in the "low hundreds of dollars a month," up to ~$500/month max for a more complex business — e.g., his own crypto business with many accounts/transactions) will set up and maintain QuickBooks and categorize everything. A skilled virtual assistant can also be trained to do this. The only deliverables you personally need monthly: a P&L (profit and loss statement) and a balance sheet (plus annually, and quarterly if desired).
5. **Threshold 3 — bigger than QuickBooks**: Chris says he's personally never had a business outgrow QuickBooks, since it can handle businesses over $100 million in revenue.

## Tax structure note (important caveat)
- An LLC is a pass-through entity: you're taxed on the business's net profit, not on what you personally withdraw from it. Example given: if the business nets $1M in profit but most of that is reinvested into growth/ads/inventory and only $200K is actually taken home, the tax bill will still be based on close to the full $1M profit — roughly double what was actually taken out in cash. This surprises a lot of new business owners.
- Quarterly estimated tax payments are required and are "a pain" — plan for them.
- Chris mentions being personally audited once and calls it "the worst period of my life," reinforcing the message not to overcomplicate or neglect bookkeeping.

## Recommended order of operations (summary)
Google Sheet (personal account) → validate the business is real → file LLC + get EIN → open dedicated business bank account (Mercury) → true up historical numbers → hire a bookkeeper (a few hundred dollars/month) to run QuickBooks and produce a monthly P&L/balance sheet → prepare for quarterly estimated taxes.

## Caveats / risks
- Always form an LLC eventually ("LLC always") once the business is validated — but don't over-invest in legal/bank structure before you have real revenue.
- Don't let convenience (a nice hairball of shared credit cards/cash-back optimization) create bookkeeping complexity across multiple businesses.
- Pass-through taxation catches many new owners off guard; budget for taxes on net profit, not on cash actually withdrawn.
