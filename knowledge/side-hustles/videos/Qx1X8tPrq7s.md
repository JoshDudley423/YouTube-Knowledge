# How to Find Customers for Your Home Service Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qx1X8tPrq7s | Published: 2025-03-05

Part 2 of an interview with Daniel Cronauer (Apex Softwash / power washing, Kringle Lights Christmas light installation, and a 24-hour gym business, Mountain Top area, LinkedIn: danny-cronauer). Focus: low-tech, low-cost residential customer acquisition tactics and side businesses layered on top of a core home-service business.

## The ideas covered
1. Residential power/soft washing (Apex Softwash) — commercial power washing add-on averaging ~$1,800/stop; roof/HVAC "coil cleaning" service (400-600 jobs/year, ~$600K revenue line, described as the business's best margin service).
2. Christmas light installation and removal (Kringle Lights) — seasonal add-on business run with his brother as a partner; ~$450,000/year projected revenue.
3. A ground-up-built 24-hour gym business (two locations) — started from noticing his mountaintop hometown (12,000-15,000 residents) had no local gym.
4. A "permanent" (installed year-round, app-controlled) Christmas/holiday lighting product, discussed as a cautionary case study, not a recommended core business.

## Numbers
- Commercial power washing: ~$1,800 average per stop.
- Coil cleaning (HVAC coil/roof cleaning): ~400-600 jobs/year, ~$600,000 in revenue — called out as the highest-margin, favorite service line.
- Christmas lights (Kringle Lights): projected ~$450,000 next year; minimum job price $800; crew can install lights on 3-4 houses/day (skilled crew) and take down ~15 houses/day; each partner (Daniel and his brother) nets roughly $90,000/season from this line; competitors reportedly charge as low as $500/job, but Daniel intentionally prices higher ($800 min, sometimes $5,000-6,000 for large jobs) and turns away price-shopping customers, citing a hard 8-week installation window and a "we don't need you" pricing philosophy.
- Rock flyers: ordered up to ~20,000-40,000 flyers in a year; estimated conversion rate on rock flyers alone is "5 to 10%" resulting in "100" business (dialogue is a bit garbled, but the claimed conversion range is roughly 5-10%, described as "crazy" high because targeting is precise).
- Permanent lights side business: seasonal price ~$1,000 vs. permanent installation quoted at $4,500 (a customer chose permanent specifically because it pays off over 3-4 years vs. repeat seasonal costs — cited example: one customer paid ~$20,000 cumulative over 7 years for seasonal service). Daniel has sold "almost $100,000" of permanent lights total but calls the recurring maintenance/liability burden (e.g., ~20-25 outstanding permanent installations he must support indefinitely, including a 70-year-old customer who can't use the control app) not worth it as a business line, despite ~$3,000 profit per job achievable in days of work — explicitly says seasonal work is "an asset" to the business while permanent work is "a liability."
- Gym: opened 24-hour gym from scratch after seeing zero gyms serving 12,000-15,000 mountaintop residents; land purchase alone cost $200,000 (framed as "my marketing" — buying into obvious pent-up demand/no-supply market). Construction (self-managed, built by "able-bodied" family/friends rather than a contractor) cost ~$600,000 vs. a professional quote of ~$1.2 million, but took 3 years to complete. Grew from 0 to 1,000 members in about 1.5 months at opening. Monthly membership price started at $25, raised to $29 during COVID (partly to intentionally filter out price-sensitive members, partly due to rising costs). Peaked at ~1,500 members pre-COVID; sits around ~1,000-1,200 members post-COVID (some members never returned). A second gym location (~half hour away) has ~1,200 members. Rule-of-thumb cited: roughly 10% of the US population holds a gym membership — used as a market-sizing heuristic for how many members a town's population could support ("10% of the town" was treated as the practical ceiling in his market).

## Process / how to do it (customer acquisition playbook for home-service businesses)
1. **Yard signs**: described as "the single best thing you could have" for a residential service business — Daniel attributes "hundreds of thousands, actually millions of dollars" to yard-sign-driven word-of-mouth and organic neighbor curiosity. Rule: put the sign out immediately — his crews are trained to place the yard sign in the customer's yard the moment they start the job (e.g., "hook up water, next: put a yard sign out"), not after job completion, and never leave the customer's property without a sign placed even if something else is forgotten. Never ask permission to place a yard sign. Complaints are rare and considered a non-issue — treat any complaint as negligible and not worth changing behavior over (he cites paying a township fine rather than removing a sign, and multiple examples of customers explicitly requesting a sign be placed because they want neighbors to know who did the work).
2. **"Rock flyers"**: a flyer stapled/bagged with a rock (for weight, thrown into driveways so it doesn't blow away) distributed door-to-door/driveway-to-driveway in targeted neighborhoods, typically by the lowest-seniority crew member while the truck is being packed up after a job. Bag type: simple Ziploc or a custom clear plastic bag sized for the flyer (sourced from an Orlando bag maker in Daniel's case); rocks sourced from a bag of landscaping rock at Home Depot or from the customer's own driveway — weight should be tested by throwing a few to find the right balance (heavy enough not to blow away, light enough not to hit/damage anything). Distributed to the ~4-5 adjacent houses around a completed job, or "carpet bombed" into an entire neighborhood at the start of a season.
3. **"Operation Carpet Bomb"**: an aggressive early-spring tactic — blanket an entire targeted neighborhood with flyers in one push, then deliberately don't answer the phone for the next few hours to let the initial wave of complaint calls die down; the people who called to complain typically don't call back the next day, and instead the tactic generates business from neighbors of complainers who see the crew working across the street shortly after.
4. **Targeting/selection over broad-reach ads**: flyers and yard signs are deliberately placed only in higher-propensity neighborhoods (e.g., outskirts, golf-course communities, homogeneous-build-age neighborhoods where a shared plumbing/roofing issue is likely to recur across neighbors) rather than blanket digital ads (Google/Facebook Ads) which Daniel says pull in too many low-quality/mismatched leads ("blanket coverage... picking up so much garbage"). Vehicle wraps ("wrap truck") on service vehicles serve as an additional low-cost, always-on visibility/brand-touch layer alongside signs and flyers.
5. **Copywriting rules for yard signs**: put the company NAME first and prominently (a sign with no business name reads as an unbranded, less trustworthy "chuck in a truck" operator); include the specific service performed if it adds clarity (e.g., "roof cleaning / house wash"); always include a phone number. If the business name alone doesn't make the service obvious (e.g., a plumber), a phone number and company name are still sufficient since referrals/neighbor word-of-mouth fill in the rest.
6. **Copywriting rules for flyers**: always drive the reader to a website (a flyer has limited space for information; the website carries the rest — product breakdown, portfolio photos, service process). Break the service into simple bullet-point steps (his Christmas-lights flyer example: "design it, install it, maintain it, take it down") plus a photo of a finished, sign-marked house.
7. **Networking/relationship-based lead generation**: casual, non-transactional relationships (e.g., meeting someone at a kids' wrestling event, a bachelor party, or a networking meetup) repeatedly surfaced high-value B2B contracts — cited example: a ~$100,000-plus water tower cleaning contract sourced from a wrestling-coach acquaintance met purely socially over a year without either party knowing what the other did for work. General principle voiced by Koerner: "the harder way is usually the better way" — physically driving neighborhoods and attending in-person events outperforms passive paid-ad strategies for this kind of local B2B/B2C sourcing.
8. **"No one knows who you are yet" mindset**: treat every new business launch as starting from zero brand awareness — the founder must aggressively self-promote/distribute (flyers, signs, conversations) because, initially, literally no one else knows the business exists.
9. **Gym-specific launch/competitive tactics**: Daniel identified an underserved, geographically isolated market (a mountaintop town separated from the nearest gym by a literal driving barrier) and used land purchase as his core "marketing"/thesis validation rather than traditional advertising. When a competitor also moved to open a gym in the same small town (learned about via a chance networking conversation), Daniel disclosed his own plans/location advantage transparently rather than hiding it; despite warning the competitor about the strength of Daniel's location, the competitor proceeded and eventually failed. A specific tactic used to poach the rival gym's members: since the competing gym was closed on Sundays, Daniel offered free Sunday access to anyone who could show ID from any other gym membership — driving trial and eventually contributing to the rival's closure (~4 months after the tactic began). Gym floor layout was designed using informal "modern psychology" input from an equipment vendor: place the easiest/most familiar equipment (treadmills) at the entrance to avoid intimidating new members, and place the most skill-intensive equipment (squat racks) in a back corner, separating high-skill and low-skill users.
10. Willingness to publicly and confidently disclose serious intent to competitors/at networking events (rather than staying secretive) is presented as a deliberate personal strategy, paired with heavy execution investment (e.g., $100,000 spent on gym engineering) to signal genuine commitment.

## Tools/platforms/resources named
- No specific software/tools were emphasized in this video — the tactics described are entirely manual/physical (flyers, yard signs, vehicle wraps, in-person networking) as a deliberate alternative to paid digital ads (Google AdWords, Facebook Ads), which Daniel explicitly says he avoids for being too broad/low-quality.

## Caveats / risks
- Permanent/"forever" Christmas lighting installations are explicitly flagged as a bad business line despite attractive per-job profit (~$3,000 in days of work): the recurring support burden and open-ended liability (~20-25 outstanding permanent installations requiring ongoing app/technical support, including for less tech-capable elderly customers) make it a worse business model than seasonal, repeat-revenue work — "seasonal is an asset, permanent is a liability."
- Gym business has hit a "ceiling" at roughly 10% market penetration of the local population (per Daniel's own market-sizing heuristic) — growth options at that point are limited mainly to raising prices, which conflicts with the original "high value, low cost" positioning.
- COVID caused lasting membership attrition at the gym (from ~1,500 down to current ~1,000-1,200), with some members never returning even years later.
- Aggressive flyer/yard-sign tactics (placing signs without permission, "carpet bombing" flyers) generate real complaints and occasional threats (e.g., a customer implying legal/press action over rock flyers) — Daniel's stance is to accept this risk as negligible rather than avoid the tactic, which may not suit every operator's risk tolerance or every municipality's sign/solicitation regulations (mentions actually being fined by a township for signage and simply paying the fine).
- Results are anecdotal/non-tracked: Daniel explicitly says he does not rigorously track conversion metrics (UTMs, attribution, etc.) for tactics like the "show your ID on Sunday" gym promotion, relying instead on qualitative signal (recognizing new faces) — so the specific effectiveness numbers in this video should be treated as rough estimates, not measured data.
