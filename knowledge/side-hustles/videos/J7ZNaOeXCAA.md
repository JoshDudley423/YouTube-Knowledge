# An Average Guy Built This in One Weekend. Here's How
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7ZNaOeXCAA | Published: 2026-06-04

Second interview with Jon Cheney (founder of Gen AI PI / genaipi.org — an AI consulting business, "vibe coded" over a weekend). Focus: how he actually found customers (media/PR, social media, cold outreach) after building the product, not the build itself.

## The business idea

- Jon "vibe coded" (built via natural-language prompting of AI tools, no traditional coding skill) a software product/AI consulting business over a single weekend, aiming to land a customer by the next day. This grew into an AI implementation/consulting company.
- Revenue trajectory: **$180,000 within his first six weeks**; grew to **$1 million within six months**; his prior venture (referenced from an earlier interview) reportedly made $2.5M in a year doing similar AI/website "vibe coding" work for business owners.
- Also runs a paid community, **Playmakers (playmakersai.com)**, teaching people to start an AI consulting/agency business charging local businesses **$500-$5,000/month** to set up and manage AI tools — includes live weekly training calls, templates, a "5-day first client challenge," cold email/Facebook ads/SMS/cold call frameworks, and a lead-list spreadsheet; cited at 200+ members.

## Core customer-acquisition tactics (the meat of the video)

### 1. Local TV/traditional news pitching
- Process: fill out the "contact us" / story-tip form on local news station websites (e.g., a local Fox/ABC/NBC affiliate); pitch a specific human-interest angle, not a sales pitch — reporters/producers explicitly do not want to give a "free commercial."
- His actual pitch angle: "I built a business in a weekend with AI" — a relatable, surprising hook (most people don't know what "vibe coding" even is), not a claim about his specific product's features.
- Result of first local news segment (KUTV, Utah): landed within the same day of pitching, aired that night. Follow-on inbound over the next few days: a $20,000/month contract, a $10,000/month contract, and several one-time trainings worth $10-15k each — **total ~$200,000-$300,000 in deal value from one ~3-minute local TV segment.**
- Key tactical insight: **local news outlets often have "no backlog"** — a story pitched same-day can air same-day; this is a general signal that most small/local news outlets are hungry for content, not besieged with pitches. Lack of backlog is common, not unique to a slow news day.
- **Craft of the pitch**: hand the reporter a "packaged deal" — clearly state your angle and, explicitly, why their audience/listeners will care — don't make them do the work of finding the hook themselves. This makes the ask fast for them to say yes to (interview itself takes ~25-30 minutes on-site, most of that just setting up the microphone).
- **Repeat/follow-up story pitching**: six months later, pitched the same reporter a follow-up ("I did a million dollars in the last six months") combined with a second newsworthy item (a new partnership with BYU athletics to teach AI to student athletes) — landed a second segment the same day it was pitched. Building an ongoing relationship with a specific reporter (having their personal cell number) creates a repeatable "PR network" — described as a symbiotic relationship similar to inviting past podcast guests back.
- Applies the same logic to **digital print/local newspapers** (example: Provo Daily Herald) — same pitch-a-real-story-angle approach.
- Track record from a previous company (Seek): built ~50-60 press mentions over about a decade of ongoing, unpaid media outreach; one article in a UK publication (Rakenour) led directly to landing Nestle as a client, worth roughly $1M over a 3-year, ~$300k/year contract — entirely from one press placement.
- Not every pitch lands: acknowledges many outlets don't respond or say "not for us" — framed as a normal, expected hit rate; the response is to keep trying with genuinely newsworthy angles rather than spamming or getting discouraged.

### 2. Press releases
- After signing the BYU partnership, also arranged a co-branded press release involving BYU and Replit (he personally texted Replit's CEO Amjad Masad to ask for a donation of 6 free months of Replit per student-athlete, and got a yes) — combining a small operator with two credible, recognizable names (a top university + a multi-billion-dollar startup) to boost pickup credibility. Getting approved quotes from each partner organization is required and can take a few weeks to coordinate.
- Result: press release picked up by Yahoo News, MSNBC, TechCrunch, and others — generates backlinks and third-party credibility mentions.
- Service used: **PR Newswire**, cost **~$1,000 per release** — described as worth it for a high-value/high-ticket strategic announcement, not necessarily for every small update.

### 3. Consistent daily social media posting ("day trading attention")
- References Gary Vaynerchuk's book "Day Trading Attention" as his framework/inspiration.
- Posts about AI daily in a low-pressure, non-salesy "reporter" style (e.g., "I checked out this new platform, here's what I like/don't like, have you tried it?") — gives free value/opinion rather than pitching.
- Posting cadence comparison discussed: Gary Vee reportedly posts ~60x/day across platforms; the podcast host (Chris) estimated at 25-40 posts/day across all platforms; Jon estimated himself at 12-15/day.
- **30-day public gratitude-post challenge**: post something you're grateful for every day for 30 days (Jon personally did this for a full year — 366 days) as a discipline-building, engagement-generating exercise; suggested amendment discussed in the episode — include at least one post about a real failure/embarrassment and what you learned, to add authenticity.
- **"Jab, jab, jab, hook"** content strategy (also from Gary Vee): give free value repeatedly before ever making an ask.
- Deliberately does **not use AI to write his own social posts** — writes them himself so the voice is authentically his, though he says this is a personal preference, not a claim that AI-assisted posts are inherently bad.
- **Platforms shift from a "social graph" to an "interest graph"**: content gets algorithmically distributed to people interested in the topic, not just existing connections — meaning posting consistently about a specific topic (e.g., AI) attracts strangers who care about that topic, while posting about personal life (family, hobbies) surfaces to people who know you personally. Recommends being a "full person" across platforms — mixing professional content (AI) with personal content (family, kayaking, faith, piano) — because people found him as a client lead specifically *through* personal-life posts (e.g., a kayaking video prompting a stranger to reach out about AI consulting needs).
- **Social proof / low-follower-count workaround**: cites a team member ("Kevin") with a small following who posts authentic "day in the life" content (dad, former cop, early-morning workouts) — despite low view counts, his cold-DM response rate is unusually high because recipients check his profile, see 5 seconds of authentic personal content, and trust him enough to respond. Being genuinely human/relatable in your content can substitute for a large follower count when doing outreach.

### 4. Cold outreach / "asymmetric bets" (direct examples)
- Jon's own earlier company (Bucky's, apparently a bulk-buy/reselling concept — "bought one of everything at Buc-ee's convenience store, sold on a website"): cold-emailed ~5 reporters found via bylines; one, Dan Solomon at Texas Monthly (~5 million monthly readers, Texas's largest news outlet), responded and published an article (Nov 12, 2020) that directly generated **$180,000 in first-year sales**, and led to a cascading chain of pickups (Southern Living → Eater → CNET). The underlying business was still operating 6 years later doing **over $1 million/year in net profit**, attributed originally to that single email/article.
- Chris shares his own cold-email example to Mark Cuban when starting a company (LCD Cycle, grew from $30k/month to $600k/month in 17 months): got a 17-minute-turnaround one-word reply ("Congrats") and no further engagement — cited as a normal, low-cost, "nothing lost" attempt.
- Also references John McAfee (started a business together after a cold email) and a secondhand story about "Bucket List Family" founder Garrett Gee, who built a QR-code scanning app, got initially rejected by a TechCrunch writer, flew to her office uninvited and took her to lunch until she agreed the personal effort itself was a story worth writing — the resulting coverage helped the app reach 50 million users and led to a $54 million acquisition by Snapchat (the app's tech became the basis for Snapcodes).

## Mindset/framework points

- "No one is afraid of failure — we're afraid of what other people will think if we fail." Framed via a stranded-island/raft-building analogy: you'd always build the raft alone, but might hesitate if being watched (Truman Show scenario) — reframing failure-avoidance as social-perception-avoidance is presented as the actionable unlock.
- Ideas are "a dime a dozen" — execution and consistent, public visibility (building in public) are what create value; not worth hiding your idea out of fear of it being copied.
- Success from these tactics is not overnight — emphasizes multi-year consistency (posting for 2 years, not 2 months) as the real threshold before judging whether social/outreach efforts "work."

## Caveats / risks

- These case studies are self-reported, unaudited figures from the guest and host, not independently verified.
- Press releases (~$1,000/release via PR Newswire) are explicitly framed as worthwhile mainly for high-ticket/strategically significant announcements, not as a blanket recommendation for every update.
- Local news pickup and cold-email success (Cuban, McAfee, TechCrunch examples) have a low hit rate — most cold outreach in the examples given did NOT lead anywhere (e.g., Mark Cuban's one-word reply, several TV stations that never respond) — the tactic's value comes from occasional high-payoff hits, not a reliable conversion rate per attempt.
- Advises tailoring personal-life content to the platform's professional context (e.g., relating a kayaking or faith post back to how it helps his professional life on LinkedIn specifically) rather than posting purely personal content without any throughline.
