# How To Turn Street Sweeping into a Lucrative Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VErm-eG_wlY | Published: 2024-02-16

Live call-in Q&A episode ("Koerner's Corner") — Chris Koerner takes questions from six different callers on six different business situations. Notes below are organized by segment/topic.

## Segment 1: Street sweeping business (Brandon, Ventura CA)
- Business idea: buy/grow a street-sweeping service; underlying insight is that street sweeping reduces wildfire risk and many communities don't realize they lack it — "invent the disease and sell the cure" (create awareness of a need, then supply the fix).
- Bought a struggling street-sweeping company as an asset sale with 5–6 existing customers (some HOAs, a county contract) six months prior; roughly doubled revenue since.
- Pricing: $185/hour for one-off/call-in jobs; county/HOA contracts priced per month with multiple sweeps (frequency varies by client — some weekly, some twice-monthly, tied to their budget).
- Capacity/unit economics: each truck running full-time can generate ~$25,000/month in revenue; had 2 trucks active (one full-time, one ~30 hrs/week) plus 2 more available to deploy — so up to 4 trucks × $25K = ~$100K/month topline potential without more capex. Night sweeps are less profitable than day sweeps.
- Customer mix: HOAs, commercial/construction contractors needing pre-inspection sweeps (recurring 5-6 projects/month from this), county/government contracts (only rebid every ~2-3 years, larger but higher-hassle/more complaints, often "prevailing wage").
- Most profitable segment: commercial construction jobs (shorter projects, less recurring, but least headache and most profitable). Government contracts pay well but come with more complaints/friction and infrequent bidding opportunities.
- Local competitors are increasingly being bought by venture-capital-backed roll-ups — the caller's clients often come to him because the larger consolidated competitors provide worse service.
- Marketing: started Google Ads ~2 months before this recording, spending ~$2,500/month, driving ~9-10 new customers (mix of one-day and recurring), and roughly doubled revenue month-over-month since starting. Also listed on Google Business Profile (had only 1 five-star review at time of call).
- Chris's advice: (1) build up Google Business Profile reviews aggressively (target 20-50, even from residents/non-municipal contacts — Google doesn't distinguish) since it drives organic visibility; remove "LLC" from the public business name; (2) since ad spend is already converting, the priority is to scale ad spend further, and get a paid audit (e.g., hire an expert on Upwork for $100-200) rather than hiring a full ad agency for ~$3K/month, since most of an agency's value is front-loaded in the initial audit; (3) because the market (contractors/developers in a defined county) is finite, build a master target list using tools like Apollo and Outscraper (scraping Google Maps listings) to see total addressable market vs. current customer count, then run direct outreach (e.g., $5 Venmo requests to grab attention, personalized Loom videos) to those specific named prospects — treat it like account-based sales given it's a high-ticket, limited-prospect-pool business (unlike something low-ticket/high-volume like landscaping).

## Segment 2: Aspiring entrepreneur in advertising sales, Houston (Emil/caller)
- Caller: $10K-$50K capital available, strong sales skills (self-rated upper-tier vs. peers), works 15-20 hrs/week in ad sales, wants an in-person/local business leveraging sales skill; had previously tried a house-cleaning business that failed due to a saturated, race-to-the-bottom local market.
- Chris's advice: avoid house cleaning entirely — low ticket, "not a binary outcome" (many small ways to fail a client, e.g., showing up late, leaving a hair), oversaturated with course-sellers teaching it.
- Recommended path: pick a higher-ticket home service (mentioned fencing, foundation repair, roofing as examples) that can be *subcontracted out* rather than performed personally — buy a cheap templated "card"-style website (~$30-50/year), duplicate it across 5 different home-service niches (swap city/service name), then validate demand by posting in local Facebook/Nextdoor groups asking what services people can't find, before committing to one niche.
- Advised against buying an existing business at this stage — better to first validate a service model with near-zero capital risk (find the customer/job, then subcontract fulfillment to a trusted vendor), and only consider buying an existing business (e.g., via SBA loan) later once more experienced.

## Segment 3: Custom websites for real estate agents (Joe)
- Original plan: cold-email real estate agents scraped from realtor.com who *don't* have a website, offering to build one for ~$2,000.
- Chris's critique: targeting agents without a website is a poor signal — they're likely either inactive agents (stale data) or already-successful agents who've never needed one and won't be convinced.
- Better approach recommended: target agents who already have a website but on a specific dated platform/plugin. Use BuiltWith to find all sites using a WordPress IDX (MLS integration) plugin — this surfaces real, active real-estate websites that are likely outdated/low-converting. No need to build custom sites — use existing high-quality SaaS templates (e.g., "Carrot"-style real estate site builders) and still charge $2,000 even though setup takes ~30 minutes with a template.
- Pitch approach: don't lead with "your site looks bad" — lead with a value/lead-generation stat (e.g., "a website with X/Y/Z features drives 36% more leads") pulled from the platform's own marketing stats, and point to other live sites built on that same platform as implied "examples," without needing your own client portfolio yet.
- Realtors are relatively easy to reach (public contact info) but are heavily solicited already — upside is they're highly likely to answer phone calls/texts since a call could be a live listing lead.

## Segment 4: RV park occupancy issues (Matthew Stewart, north Alabama/Tennessee, 6 mobile-home/RV parks)
- Problem: 50-pad RV park revenue swings from ~$25,000/month in summer to ~$15,000/month in winter (contractor-tenant contracts largely end in December, don't pick back up until March/April) — MH (mobile home) park revenue stays stable; the volatility is RV-specific.
- Idea validated as working elsewhere: had already added fully-furnished, utility-included tiny homes renting for $1,100/month (400 sq ft, 1BR/1BA) — reported strong demand, and tiny homes are seen as easier to live in through winter than an RV.
- Chris's core fix for RV/park occupancy: buy a used travel trailer (8-15K, 25-35ft, 5-10 years old, pull-behind not motorhome) and offer it as a lease-to-own unit targeted at tenants likely to stay 6 months to 6 years — validate demand cheaply by posting a Facebook Marketplace ad using stock/Google images before actually buying the trailer, to see inquiry volume first. Chris says he's "never seen this not work" from his own park-ownership experience (cited a park in Kiowa, Oklahoma, pop. ~800).
- Suggested exploring annual leases (charging a lump sum, e.g., $8,000/year vs. $600/month) to smooth revenue given predictable seasonal vacancy, and doing structured exit interviews/surveys with departing tenants (caller hadn't done this) to understand true churn reasons — most departures were job-driven relocations, not dissatisfaction or competitor switching.
- Storage-only option (letting tenants store their RV over winter without occupying a pad) discussed as a possible revenue source but caller lacked spare land not on gas lines or in flood zones.

## Segment 5: Converting park-owned mobile homes to tenant-owned (same caller, MH parks)
- Caller owns 77 mobile homes outright and wants to reduce landlord maintenance burden (HVAC, flooring, staffing) by converting to a tenant-owned model, currently running ~10-11% cap rate even giving homes away, given their basis in the land.
- Chris's playbook (used previously in his own MH park deals, e.g., Texas/Oklahoma): deed the home over to the tenant for free or near-free, without raising their total payment (e.g., if paying $700/month total, split it to $350 lot rent + $350 (now $0, since owned) — tenant's payment can even stay the same during transition). Be explicit that ownership now means the tenant is responsible for repairs.
- Reported historical result: ~80% of tenants accept the conversion readily; ~20% decline/move out ("too hard").
- Regulatory caveat: in some states you must be a licensed mortgage broker to do owner-financed transfers like this — check state law (Texas and Oklahoma were described as "very forgiving"; caller needed to check Alabama's rules).
- If homes are in decent condition (could otherwise sell for $20-25K on Facebook Marketplace), extract some value ($3-5K) from the tenant rather than giving fully away — only give away homes that are essentially worthless/trashed.
- Good idea surfaced during the call (validated by Chris): fund a tenant-paid optional "maintenance fund" (e.g., extra ~$50/month) that acts like insurance for shared repair costs (e.g., HVAC failure) post-conversion, rather than the landlord bearing full risk or tenant being totally on their own.

## Segment 6: Family business idea with kids (David, Norfolk VA area, mortgage sales + rental property owner)
- Wants a semi-passive business he can run with minimal direct involvement (hired help) and potentially get his 13-year-old son involved in, in a smaller town; has access to land/capital.
- Chris's idea: buy 1-5 acres of appropriately zoned raw land, put up a prefab metal building (1,500-5,000 sq ft, cost roughly $60K circa 2016-17 for a ~2,100 sq ft example, likely 2-3x that by ~2024) with HVAC, insulation, plumbing, and a bathroom — creates a flexible-use foundation/asset that can later be subdivided and rented out piecemeal, used for storage, or repurposed for almost any use (indoor sports facility, contractor storage, classic-car storage, bounce-house/inflatable storage, etc.) while a specific business model is validated over the following year.
- For a kid-friendly rental business specifically: recommends starting broad rather than picking one niche immediately — e.g., a combined bounce-house + laser-tag + kayak-rental "rental" brand, observing which offering has the most demand before specializing. Cited a mobile petting-zoo business and mobile festival/event-rental business (own past experience) as examples of niche rental businesses that can be lucrative, plus lower-cost rental niches like wedding chairs/wreaths.
- Caveat from Chris's own experience: a mobile festival business he started left him stuck with unsellable leftover restaurant-supply inventory — prefers rental businesses built around cheaper, easier-to-liquidate assets (like bounce houses) over big illiquid equipment.
- General framing: any small business started this way is sellable or can be shut down later without major loss — framed as a good way to "taste entrepreneurship" and develop transferable skills with the kids, not necessarily a permanent commitment.

## Segment 7: Scaling/exiting a bootstrapped moving business (Tommy, Alexandria VA)
- Origin story: accidentally started a moving/hauling side business after buying a small utility trailer for personal use, then renting it out informally (started at $60/job, grew via word of mouth/Nextdoor/Facebook Marketplace to $100+/job); now ~$275,000 cumulative revenue, 2 trucks, still run as a side hustle alongside a full-time corporate sales job.
- Owner is the sales/estimating/customer-facing bottleneck; his business partner handles all physical field work but not sales/customer service.
- Marketing that's worked: ~90% referral-based (used personal high-school/local network), Nextdoor ads (the original acquisition channel and still strong), some Google ads, and physically parking a branded truck in high-traffic areas (a mall/Target parking lot, a busy downtown shopping street) on off days — caller confirmed this directly generated inbound calls.
- Cross-sell opportunity flagged: family also owns a 60-year-old high-end German-import auto repair garage with a large, automated CRM of wealthy long-term customers — Chris's recommendation was to actively mine that existing customer list/relationship (flyers at the garage counter, staff mentioning the moving business, calling past auto customers) as free/low-cost lead generation given implicit brand trust.
- Chris's broader strategic advice: given strong local growth (DC-area, high housing/office-move demand) and an already-working truck+brand system, this could become an eight-figure ($10M+) local moving business — recommended the caller quit his day job, add a third truck, invest more in wrapped trucks and Google ads spend, and specifically noted that a billboard salesman (Clear Channel) told him movers are one of the few business types that can reliably attribute leads to billboard geography (e.g., a billboard in a specific suburb drives calls from that suburb) — worth testing local billboards for brand awareness with trackable ROI.
- Caveat acknowledged: quitting a stable six-figure job with three kids is a real risk; Chris said he doesn't usually recommend it but made an exception here given the caller already had a validated, proven revenue base and unused capacity (customer demand outstripping ability to serve it).

## Cross-cutting themes from Chris Koerner's advice across all segments
- For low-capital/early validation: test demand with a cheap ad (Facebook Marketplace post using stock photos, a $30-50 templated website) *before* buying assets or building anything custom.
- For businesses with a small, defined universe of potential customers (e.g., B2B/local commercial), building a complete target list (via Apollo, Outscraper/Google Maps scraping) and doing direct personalized outreach beats broad advertising.
- Recurring/subscription-style local service businesses (or any business with an existing operational base and demand overflow) are worth aggressively reinvesting in and scaling rather than diversifying, once genuinely validated.
- Selling/leveraging an existing brand's trust and customer list (e.g., a family auto-repair shop's CRM) is a high-value, near-zero-cost growth lever for an adjacent new business.
