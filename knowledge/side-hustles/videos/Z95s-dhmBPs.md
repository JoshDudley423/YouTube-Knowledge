# The Secret Hack to Landing MASSIVE Commercial Service Jobs
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z95s-dhmBPs | Published: 20240605

Follow-up call with Brandon, who bought a ~30-year-old Southern California **street sweeping** company (residential, HOA, and construction clients), on expanding into high-ticket **commercial/parking-garage pressure washing** as an adjacent, subbed-out service line.

## The business idea(s)
- Core business: commercial street sweeping (HOAs, construction sites, grocery stores, parking lots).
- Adjacent high-ticket idea discussed: **commercial pressure washing of multi-story parking garages** — sold and managed by the street-sweeping company but fully subcontracted out to a specialist crew, at least until volume justifies buying equipment.
- Rationale for the pairing: any pressure-washing job on a large paved structure requires a sweep-through first anyway, so the two services are natural bundle-mates; pressure washing has a vastly higher ticket size than sweeping for similar site visits.
- Suggested add-on: build a **separate, narrowly-focused landing page/domain** (e.g., "LAParkingGaragePressureWashing.com") leveraging the parent company's 30-year track record, and run low-competition Google Ads against it, rather than folding it into the generic company site.

## Numbers
- Street sweeping company: bought ~1 year prior; revenue trajectory reported to host over time: ~$15-20K/month at acquisition → ~$28-30K/month three months before this call → ~$60K/month at time of this call. Net margins ~8-12% (capital-intensive: buying/updating aging sweeper equipment).
- A single seven-story airport-area parking garage pressure-wash job: quoted ~$55,000 (validated against 3-4 other pressure-washing operators' quotes of $50-60K, based on square footage) for the whole structure; a prior partial (half-building) job by another contractor two years earlier cost $30,000, which the customer disclosed and which anchored Brandon's estimate.
- Cost to subcontract that job out entirely: ~$30,000-35,000 — roughly a 35-45% gross margin on a single job with zero equipment risk.
- One job like this ≈ a full month of the existing sweeping company's total revenue.
- By contrast, sweeping alone (no pressure washing) on that same garage would only be worth ~$500-600 — roughly a 100x smaller ticket for a comparable site visit.
- Equipment cost to do pressure washing in-house at this scale: ~$35-40K per truck (truck + trailer + rig), ~$75K for two crews/trucks — the operator quoted said it would pay for itself in 2-3 jobs.
- Lead volume: 4-5 commercial pressure-washing leads in ~2 months just from initial cold outreach, ranging $2,500-5,000 (smaller commercial jobs) up to $55K (the garage). Only 10 pressure-washing subcontractors contacted before finding one both capable and trustworthy enough to take a $30-35K sub job — most turned it down as "too big."
- A specialist competitor cited: after 10 years, doing $7-8M/year revenue, 80% commercial (parking garages, sandblasting/stencil removal, fleet washing) plus some residential — evidence of how large this single niche can scale.
- Only ~3-4 companies in all of Southern/Northern California believed capable of handling a job of that size, per the subcontractor consulted — indicating low competition at the high end despite pressure washing being a broadly saturated, TikTok-viral home-service category at the low end.
- Typical sweep frequency for recurring revenue context: grocery stores can be nightly (low margin, "race to the bottom"); nicer HOA communities once a week or every other week; a parking garage only gets swept when it's being pressure-washed (has to be emptied floor by floor for the service), i.e., much less recurring but far bigger ticket.

## Process / steps for finding these jobs
1. Cold outreach: scraped lists (mentioned tool: **Outscraper**) of HOAs and commercial property managers; joined a local contractor association to access the construction-company side of the market.
2. Mix of cold calling, cold emailing, and following up on old/dead leads.
3. On a promising cold call, always ask a prospect if they have *other* properties/contacts — one parking-lot-owner call led directly to the referral for the $55K garage job.
4. When quoting an unfamiliar job type/size, ask the customer directly if they've done this before and what they paid last time — used as a benchmark, then cross-checked against 3-4 other operators (contacted via Twitter) before finalizing the quote.
5. Sub out the specialized work entirely at first: find an established, trustworthy subcontractor (not someone who might "flood the job" and create liability), quote the customer a markup over the sub's price, and only invest in owning equipment once volume justifies it — de-risking the vertical before capital investment.
6. Suggested next step (from host): scrape a full target list of parking garages/lots in the county (recommended tool: **Rami**, used with a virtual assistant during a free trial to build the list cheaply) and hit it systematically with a local (non-VA) caller.
7. Suggested cross-sell: mine your *existing* customer list (from the sweeping business) directly for pressure-washing demand before doing any other outreach — potentially uncovering "a whole other $60K/month" hiding in an existing book of business.

## Caveats / risks / host's broader business commentary
- High capex risk in street sweeping itself: a single equipment breakdown can run ~$15,000.
- Pressure washing overall is extremely crowded at the low end (TikTok-driven wave of teenagers/college kids buying a Home Depot pressure washer) — but this crowding is concentrated at the low-ticket/residential end; the $50K+ garage-tier segment has a natural barrier to entry (truck/equipment cost, project complexity) that filters out casual competitors, making it comparatively uncrowded even though the parent industry is oversaturated.
- Host's framing: look for underserved sub-segments within an overall crowded/viral industry rather than assuming the whole industry is bad — the "top of the funnel" (new entrants) may be huge, but a specific expensive niche within it can still have a small top of funnel.
- Host generally discourages diversifying into unrelated new verticals (a business usually only has ~1% market share of its current vertical to still capture) but makes an exception here because this vertical is operationally adjacent (same sweep-first workflow) and dramatically higher-ticket.
- The buy-versus-build framing extends to future scaling: once organic growth stalls, consider *acquiring* an existing pressure-washing company (as the host does in his own tree-service business — buying a profitable operator and layering in better marketing/an operating partner) rather than starting one from scratch.
- Uncertain whether pressure washing garages is required by regulation or purely aesthetic/cosmetic — the customer in this case cited it as partly cosmetic (a "VIP" section needing a deep clean) and partly a long-term cost-saving maintenance measure (preventing pavement resurfacing costs), similar to the logic for regular sweeping maintenance on asphalt.
- Approval friction: large commercial customers may need internal budget sign-off (this customer had to get his boss/VP's approval before the $55K job could proceed), which can delay timelines by months (job was pushed to "mid-to-late summer").
