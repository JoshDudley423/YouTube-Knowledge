# Watch Me Judge 9 Of Your Business Ideas, Live!
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GGc03e2oDvc | Published: 2024-06-24

Chris Koerner reacts live to ~11 viewer-submitted business ideas, grading each "stud" or "dud" with reasoning. Format is rapid-fire; capturing each idea plus his reasoning/numbers below.

## Ideas judged, with verdicts and reasoning

1. **Nonprofit building robots to clean the planet** (dud as pitched) — too ambitious to start with; advises starting with something simple and provable (e.g., a campus cleanup nonprofit) to build traction/credibility before attempting anything requiring heavy funding or partnerships (e.g., with Tesla/Figure for robots).

2. **Marketing agency exclusively for cash-pay psychiatrists** (stud) — liked specifically because it's narrow/niched, the submitter has a direct network "in" with multiple psychiatrists (not just one), and multiple psychiatrists sharing the same complaint (can't fill appointments for a certain audience) signals real, validated demand.

3. **Fresh-made-to-order potato chip shop** (live pitch, mixed: "test it, don't invest big yet") — customer picks a flavor, chips fried fresh in ~5-10 minutes, bagged to go. Chris's take: high margin, no direct competitor doing exactly this, but risky as a brand-new food concept (compares to a popcorn-focused candy shop model, "Mom & Pop corn," and a French-fry-focused concept called "Fizz N Fries" out of Utah that stayed single-location for years). Recommended validation: get a used fryer, test at a farmers market for ~$100 all-in, and specifically check for repeat-purchase intent (a memorable one-off product isn't enough — needs people to come back and get "addicted" to a specific flavor, the way Crumbl did with cookies). Concern: chips may lose to french fries in consumer passion/craving intensity.

4. **Real-time HVAC bidding marketplace (two-sided, AI-assisted)** (dud as a marketplace, stud as a different pivot) — as a Thumbtack-style real-time bidding marketplace, judged too hard to bootstrap (Thumbtack itself needed nine figures of funding) and would require competing directly with an entrenched incumbent. Chris's suggested pivot: instead sell a ~$200/month SaaS widget to individual home-service companies (HVAC, plumbing, etc.) that lets their own website give an instant, automated price quote from 1-2 questions (vs. the typical "call us for a quote" — most companies' sites don't do this), differentiating them from competitors without needing a marketplace at all.

5. **Web development for small/medium businesses** (stud) — judged evergreen regardless of no-code competitors (Squarespace, Wix, Webflow, AI website builders): businesses will always want someone else to just build it for them.

6. **Hydrosol/essential oil business (rosemary, eucalyptus) sourcing distilling equipment cheaply from China** (dud as described) — disliked specifically because it requires owning/leasing land to grow the source plants. General principle stated: when importing from China, prioritize small, high-ticket items shippable via DHL air (3-day delivery from China) over low-ticket/bulky goods — higher ticket size supports profitable paid-ad acquisition even at lower sales volume (Chris's own preference: sell a $1,000 item to 10 people rather than a $100 item to 100). Suggested path: import a finished essential oil product from China first to validate demand for a specific product before considering vertically integrating into growing/processing it yourself.

7. **Selling $20-50 PDF market research reports to local service businesses** (dud) — price point too low to be viable per sale, and local service businesses (often solo operators without staff who screen incoming calls for real customers vs. salespeople) are notoriously hard to sell to. Recommended fix: raise price roughly 10x (to $300-$350+) and target mid-size businesses with an offer framed around directly making them more money, not a low-cost commodity report.

8. **"Spin the wheel" street game (pay $10, win either a shot combo or a branded sticker) in a leased NYC storefront** (dud as leased storefront, stud as street cart) — leasing a commercial space in a dense city like NYC creates too much fixed cost to cover with $10 transactions. Recommended instead testing as an unlicensed/lightly-licensed street vendor setup (cites a real example: immigrant street vendors in Times Square charging ~$20 for a phone-holder platform that spins and records a video set to a "Welcome to New York" song, using a ~$2,000 Amazon-bought rotating platform, likely paying only a one-time vending license fee rather than ongoing rent — a tweet about this reportedly got 1M+ views). General principle: test novel/gimmicky concepts in high-foot-traffic tourist areas for cheap, immediate, face-to-face feedback from people who by definition have disposable income (tourists or people who can afford to live in an expensive city).

9. **General Q&A: can you build a $1M revenue business in a year?** — Yes, cited his own example: sold $220,000 Bitcoin miners, reaching $1M in revenue in about 2 weeks (50 units x $20K), leveraging supplier relationships and market timing built from 7 prior businesses; scaled to $4.3M revenue in the second month (November 2021) at ~12% profit margin, fully bootstrapped with no outside funding. Framing: $1M/year only requires 50 sales at $20K average ticket — the key lever is picking a high-ticket-size business model (examples given: a pool company selling $100K pools needs only 10 sales/year; a driveway-replacement company at $10K average ticket needs 100 sales, achievable via door-knocking older neighborhoods with cracked driveways, paying canvassers a $1,000 commission per closed job). Caution: revenue ("topline") is not profit — many well-funded companies raise huge amounts and stay unprofitable for years or indefinitely; optimize for profit, not gross revenue headlines.

10. **Door-to-door roof pine-straw/leaf cleanup service** ($25/property, using leaf blowers + ladder, hiring 2 people from day one) — liked the core idea (most homeowners don't want to get on their own roof) but explicitly advised against hiring help immediately: do the sales and service yourself first (sell to 100 doors solo) to personally learn objection-handling, pricing psychology, and the real workflow before delegating, since an employee without ownership stake won't optimize the same way.

11. **AI repair-coach chatbot for a specific niche (e.g., auto repair, later "medical repair")** (stud) — doesn't require major funding to start (unlike the submitter assumed); recommended go-to-market: find a large, highly engaged Facebook group around one extremely specific niche (example given: a 1983 Ford Bronco owners' group, sized 10,000-50,000 members), build a free chatbot trained specifically on that model's service manual (using an LLM like Claude or OpenAI), become an active/recognized contributor in the group first to build visibility and credibility, launch the free tool to that one audience to gather feedback (what's missing, what breaks), iterate, expand to additional vehicle models, and only then start charging. Argues that building a passionate niche fanbase this way makes later fundraising realistic.

12. **Craigslist automation/tools question** — recommends a paid tool called "Cubby" (Craigslist automation software, around a decade old, ~$4, not Mac-compatible) for auto-emailing new listings and scraping — states there remain many ways to profit on Craigslist today despite its age.

13. **Travel website connecting travelers with locals + educational content + in-person tours** (stud with a pivot) — advises starting hyper-narrow on one specific travel corridor (e.g., "Americans traveling to Bolivia") rather than a broad multi-country platform, using SEO keyword-gap research (e.g., via Moz) to find destinations with high search interest but low existing content/advertiser competition. Key monetization pivot recommended: do NOT operate the tours yourself — instead monetize purely via referral/affiliate commissions to existing local tour operators (a high-ticket item), funded by content-driven SEO traffic and an email list, rather than banner ads or subscriptions. Keep the brand name broad so more destinations can be added later without rebranding.

14. **Short-form content/coaching agency pitched with vague, jargon-heavy language** ("synchronistic flow of life through creative awareness coaching") (stud on substance, dud on messaging) — liked that the founder is already all-in and the underlying business (short-form video content/editing agency) is proven to work; strongly critiqued the marketing language as needlessly complex, arguing that copy above a roughly 4th-grade reading level creates friction that costs sales, and that how you pitch to yourself likely mirrors how you're pitching to customers.

15. **Pay a small fee to spend an hour with someone else's pet while traveling** (dud, personal opinion, but flagged as an exception-prone category) — Chris personally dislikes this specific idea (states he doesn't value time with pets while traveling) but explicitly flags that he has been "surprised by pet ideas over and over" historically, meaning gut dislike of a pet-related idea isn't a reliable signal it will fail; recommends testing cheaply in a narrow setting regardless of his personal skepticism.

## General frameworks repeated across the episode
- Favor a specific, provable "in" (existing network, existing complaint pattern) over an idea validated only by personal enthusiasm.
- For import-from-China products: prioritize small + high-ticket + DHL-air-shippable over bulky/low-ticket goods.
- When judging a marketplace idea, ask whether it would require competing head-on against an incumbent that already required massive funding (e.g., Thumbtack) — if so, look for an adjacent SaaS/tooling angle instead of trying to out-marketplace the marketplace.
- Test novel/unproven concepts (new food concepts, street gimmicks, chatbots) as cheaply and quickly as possible in a narrow segment before investing in it, and always do the first reps of any service business yourself before hiring, so you personally learn pricing, objections, and workflow.
- Distinguish gross revenue from net profit when evaluating any "I made $X" claim — the harder and more important number is profitability, not topline.
