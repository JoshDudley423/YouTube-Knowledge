# Webinar: RV Park Investment Breakdown + 2026 Bonus Depreciation Drop
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1DF3AU7aWMg | Published: 2024-12-05

Investor webinar (Chris Koerner + John Cascarano of Blue Metric/Streamside) pitching two live real-estate syndication offerings: an individual RV park deal in Springdale, AR, and a diversified RV park fund. This is a passive real-estate-investment/syndication pitch rather than an operator side-hustle, but it lays out the RV park business model, deal economics, and tax mechanics in detail.

## Background / operator model

- Chris Koerner's real estate path: single-family rentals starting in college → small RV parks + mobile home parks in Oklahoma starting 2014 → sold that portfolio in 2021 → wrote online about mobile home park investing → met John Cascarano and other partners → co-founded "Blue Metric" (investor-facing brand, ~2 years old) which recently launched "Streamside" as the consumer-facing park brand (aiming to be like KOA, with cross-park loyalty/membership).
- Portfolio scale at time of recording: 26 RV parks, 10 states, 2,430+ pad sites, ~$60 million invested.
- Business thesis: RV parks are seen as an underconsolidated, overlooked asset class. Parks >$10M are being bought by private equity; parks <$2M by individual/small investors; the $2-10M "messy middle" is hard for solo investors to manage at scale but too small for PE — that's the gap Blue Metric targets. Long-term exit thesis: consolidate, professionalize management/branding, compress the cap rate, and eventually sell to private equity at a premium multiple.
- Preference for parks near a "destination" (state/national park, water feature, lake) and states with strict zoning (avoids Texas specifically, reasoning: loose zoning lets competitors build a rival park nearby); this scarcity/zoning-difficulty is described as a moat, similar to what happened in mobile home parks (increasing regulation makes new supply harder, protecting existing owners).
- Hybrid pad model preferred: a mix of short-term (2-4 day) and long-term/monthly stays, explicitly as a hedge — short-term stays are more profitable in a strong economy (~$60-90/night per pad) but the same park pivots toward monthly/annual tenants ($600-1,100/month) if the economy weakens, since RV parks then become a de facto affordable-housing option. Preference for being amenity-rich ("Barbell" strategy: fully-staffed + amenity rich, avoiding a "messy middle" of just a pool + part-time manager) to maximize occupancy/rate.

## Example deal economics (Springdale, AR park)

- $10M purchase price; 2024 NOI ~$950,000 (99.7% "cap rate" as presented, though this appears to be a rounding/labeling quirk given a $950K NOI on $10M is actually a 9.5% cap rate — presenter states it as "99.7%," likely a transcription/speech artifact worth noting as unclear in source).
- 145 pad sites on 50 acres, with room for expansion; currently 83% long-term/monthly tenants vs. transient, seen as upside because it means there's room to shift the mix toward more-profitable short-term stays.
- Raise: $5.6M for a $10M park; seller financing $6.5M at 4.5%, interest-only, 36 months; raise sized to cover both acquisition and a planned 50-pad-site capex expansion (explicit policy: raise more than needed up front rather than doing a second capital call later).
- Return targets: 8-10% preferred return to LPs (paid first), targeted ~25% IRR overall (lower for more established/lower-risk parks, higher for riskier/rural ones); this specific deal targets 26.8% IRR and 2.32x return of capital over 3-5 years.
- Typical structure: debt paid first, then LP preferred return, then return of capital, then GP/LP profit split; GP's share of profit increases once a ~15% IRR hurdle is cleared (a "promote"/carry structure).
- Cash-on-cash return modeled at 11.1% in year one; expense ratio ~40% initially (expected to improve as the park is "operationalized," often after replacing an owner-operator seller).
- Standard plan: refinance around year 3 (returning an estimated 80-120% of capital to investors at that event) after pulling rent/expansion levers, then exit around year 5.
- Financing environment noted: recent refis being locked below 7%, described as back to rates from ~18 months prior.

## The RV park fund (alternative offering)

- A diversified fund holding partial/LP positions across ~12 of the firm's 26 parks (fund closing later that month), spanning Tennessee, Kentucky, Alabama, North Carolina, Montana, Louisiana.
- Structural difference from single-park deals: full return of capital to LPs is prioritized before the GP earns anything (a more investor-friendly waterfall than the promote structure on individual deals).
- Fund benefit specific to this webinar's timing: because the fund had already closed on qualifying parks in-year, LPs investing in the fund could still capture 60% bonus depreciation for the current tax year, whereas the Springdale deal (not closing until Q1 next year) would only qualify at a lower ~40% rate next year.
- A "Northern Kentucky" park (already closed, still raising for capex) is presented as the other in-year option to capture the 60% depreciation rate before year-end.

## Bonus depreciation tax strategy (why RV parks specifically)

- RV parks are mostly 5- and 15-year property (electrical, pavement, infrastructure — minimal vertical construction), which is exactly the property class that qualifies for accelerated/bonus depreciation, described as "almost like the tax code was written for mobile home parks and RV parks."
- A cost segregation study lets the fund/park take the maximum depreciation deduction in year one; investors receive a K-1 (by March 15) showing a significant paper loss.
- Framed conceptually as "almost like a tax-free loan" — not a permanent free lunch, since the loss carries forward and offsets future distributions from that property until the investor's basis hits zero, but it materially increases effective after-tax return versus the stated cash-on-cash yield.
- Only offsets passive/real-estate-related income, not active W-2 income.
- Rough rule of thumb given: on a $100,000 investment in a park with 60% bonus depreciation eligibility, expect roughly $60,000 of depreciation to flow through.
- Context/timing risk flagged: bonus depreciation was scheduled to step down to 40% the following year at the time of recording; a bill to restore it to 100% had stalled in Congress amid election-cycle politics — outcome uncertain, so timing of an investment mattered for which depreciation rate applied.

## Tiny homes / ancillary revenue add-ons

- Some parks (especially premium/destination locations like a Kentucky lake park or the Montana park near Glacier National Park) add tiny homes/cabins as premium nightly rentals — cited case: "Everleigh"-brand tiny homes at higher-end lake sites charging $350-500+/night in season.
- Economics vary sharply by market: a ~$40,000 tiny home renting for $1,100-1,200/month on a long-term lease "pencils well," while a ~$100,000+ premium furnished unit at a high-demand lake/mountain site can command $500/night.
- One-off anecdote: acquired a foreclosed/repossessed tiny home, fixed it up, and got it on-site for under $10,000, then rented it for ~$1,000/month.
- Compared favorably to single-family "1% rule" investing (monthly rent = 1% of asset value) — a $100,000 luxury tiny home fetching $350-500/night even at only 20% occupancy blows past that benchmark.
- Case example of reinvestment over distribution: at a Louisiana park near a new multi-billion-dollar Meta data center project (bringing ~5,000 contractors with nowhere to stay), investors were asked to forgo near-term distributions so the park could add more pads/tiny homes to capture that demand — presented as an opportunistic, situational judgment call made transparently with LPs' buy-in.

## Track record / risk disclosure

- Only one capital call across 26 parks to date (for a land purchase); every park has hit its distributions to date, though roughly 6-7 of the 26 parks underperformed 2024 projections, mostly the more transient-heavy parks (more exposed to fuel-price-sensitive short-term travelers), prompting a strategic shift toward more monthly/long-term tenants at some properties (a shift also cited as happening industry-wide, e.g., at publicly traded Sun Communities) as a recession hedge.
- Manager pay: roughly $40,000-55,000/year for a single-park on-site manager, more for district managers overseeing multiple parks; rough revenue threshold to justify a manager cited as needing to be doing "north of a million dollars" in park revenue (a 10-minute-from-home ~$200K/year park generally can't support a dedicated manager).
- Investment is illiquid: capital locked up 3-5 years, redeemable early only via an unforeseeable early sale to private equity.
- Guest demographic: skews older (50s-early 60s, largely retiring/traveling Baby Boomers), though some families and remote/digital-nomad younger travelers are appearing in transient segments.

## Caveats

- This is explicitly an investment/syndication pitch with specific live deals (Springdale, AR park and "RV Fund One") that were time-limited to a specific December closing window — the specific deal terms are dated and not a generic "how to start an RV park business" tutorial.
- Presenters explicitly decline to give personalized tax advice and recommend consulting an accountant for individual bonus-depreciation situations.
- Some cap-rate figures stated in the transcript appear internally inconsistent (a 99.7% cap rate on $950K NOI / $10M price does not mathematically reconcile with the standard cap rate formula) — flagged here as a possible mis-transcription rather than treated as a verified fact.
