# The Most Overlooked $81K/Month Business Anyone Can Start
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9U4MCbfL8MQ | Published: 2026-09-11

Interview with Parker J. Smith (25, no degree, formerly DoorDash driver) who launched a residential water filtration/softener installation business ("Raleigh Water Pros") and hit $81K revenue in month 3 (85 days in). Model: he does sales/marketing/ads, subcontracts licensed plumbers for installs — never touches the physical work himself.

## The idea and why it works
- Core insight (stated explicitly): "the model has nothing to do with water filtration." It's about finding a high-ticket home-service niche where a handful of legacy players (e.g., Culligan, Kinetico, Leaf Home Water) have set artificially high prices (~$6,700–$8,000 for a whole-home system) via an old-school in-person sales process (in-home water test, sales rep visit).
- Strategy: undercut with a much simpler funnel — post the price directly in a Facebook ad, skip the in-home sales visit, and "pick up the scraps" of price-sensitive customers priced out by the big players (analogy used: "opened a Chevy dealership next to a Ferrari dealership" — the incumbents do the price anchoring for you).
- Generalizable principle he argues: any skilled-trade/home-service niche (electrical, HVAC, plumbing) likely has similar overpriced sub-segments (e.g., he guesses a main panel upgrade "MPU" in electrical work is priced ~$10K with no real cost justification) because of a shrinking skilled-labor pool and PE roll-ups distorting market rates. The opportunity is in picking one narrow high-ticket service line, not becoming a general contractor.

## Startup process and costs
- Filed LLC ~May 15; went fully live June 8 (ads on) — ~3 weeks of prep between filing and launch.
- Total setup cost: about $500.
- Tech stack (~$300/month total): website built using Claude (end-to-end, describing himself as not a developer — "vibe coded" it), Google Workspace for domain/email, GoHighLevel (GHL) white-label CRM snapshot, GHL/LeadConnector for phone, Jobber for scheduling.
- Insurance: general liability + plumbing insurance policy, ~$180/month.
- He is not a licensed plumber and doesn't do installs himself — installs are subcontracted entirely to independent licensed plumbers.

## Finding subcontractors (plumbers)
- Sourced plumbers via a sponsored Indeed job post: budget $90/day for 4 days, targeting pay of $75–$125/hour, requiring the plumber to be independently licensed, have their own supplies, and commit to at least 2 jobs/week doing only whole-home softener/carbon filtration/point-of-use drinking water installs.
- Got 4 qualified leads from that post, hired 3.
- All hires were small independent "chuck-in-a-truck" plumbing business owners (not employees of a larger firm).
- Deliberately avoids hiring plumbers who are desperate for work ("about to get evicted") or already fully booked 7 days/week with a big crew — wants someone busy ~30 hrs/week who'd like more work.
- Pays plumbers ~$400–$650 per install (avg ~$450–$500 on the flagship system), based on ~4 hours of labor; can average ~$100/hour for the plumber. Plumbers reportedly earn an extra ~$8,000/month through this arrangement without having to market, buy equipment, or run their own sales.
- Vetting plumbers requires the founder to know enough about the trade (pipe type, fittings, bypass size, crawl-space constraints) to hold a credible technical conversation — described as harder than talking to customers, since customers are largely uninformed (or repeating ChatGPT-generated questions).

## Product and pricing
- Flagship product: single 10"x54" mixed-bed tank (resin + carbon) for whole-home softening/filtration, bundled with a reverse-osmosis (RO) point-of-use drinking water system under the sink — sold as one bundle, which is the core differentiator vs. competitors who upsell RO separately.
- Price: $2,699 installed, all-inclusive (taxes, install included). Typically installed in the garage, tied into the main water line (looks like a tall water heater plus a smaller "brine tank" that holds softener salt).
- Cost breakdown per $2,700 install: ~$950 hardware (tank, control valve, brine tank, RO unit) + ~$100 misc materials (fittings, salt, insulation) = ~$1,050 in materials, plus ~$450–$500 to the plumber → total cost ~$1,500, leaving ~$1,000 gross profit (~40% gross margin) per job.
- Pricing philosophy: targeted "$1,000 net margin per job" as the design goal, priced at the lowest point that still hits that target, with room to raise prices later once reputation/reviews are established, rather than starting high.
- Larger/harder jobs (e.g., well systems, burying components underground) can run higher — one $4,000 well-system job mentioned.

## Marketing / customer acquisition
- 95% of customers come from Facebook/Meta ads; remaining 5% from referrals and Google Business Profile.
- First-ever sale came from a single static image ad with $41 total ad spend: photo of the unit + headline "Whole home plus drinking water $26.99 [sic, $2,699] installed." No video ads used — three static images total in the original ad set.
- Ad targeting: initially just broad geo/radius targeting around Raleigh, ages 30–60, no gender/homeowner filters. Later, after the Meta pixel had enough data, added retargeting and lookalike audiences based on people who clicked/called/purchased.
- Cost per lead: $30–$40 to Meta; blended customer acquisition cost (CAC) around $175 (he says this is unusually cheap and expects it to rise — considers $300–$350 CAC still very justifiable at this price point, roughly a 3x return).
- Lead-to-close economics: ~25% "true closing rate" (lead to close) overall; but only ~35% close rate *among leads who actually get on the phone* — the bottleneck is making contact, not persuading once contacted.
- Follow-up discipline is treated as the single biggest driver of success: leads are contacted repeatedly (text first, "Hey [Name], this is [Rep] with Raleigh Water Pros, when's a good time to chat for 5 min?") via automated GHL trigger, then phone calls if no text response within about an hour. They never stop following up until the lead explicitly opts out or says "leave me alone" — some closes came after 8+ touch points and up to 49 days from first contact. Concrete data cited: of 18 closed jobs from one lead batch, only 1 closed on the very first contact; 17 needed at least one more touch point; 10 of 18 closed more than 3 days after first contact.
- Math justifying persistence: even a lead requiring ~20+ touch points (at ~60 seconds each) plus a few 20-minute calls is only ~1.5 hours of labor to close a $1,000-profit job — still highly worthwhile.
- Reviews: aggressively solicits Google reviews using reciprocity (e.g., offering to drop off free water-softener salt, then asking the customer, their spouse, or a family member to leave a review). Result: 52 five-star reviews in 80 days, ranked #1 for "water filtration" in his part of Raleigh (East Raleigh, matched to Google Business Profile pin location).
- Sales tactic for objection handling: many leads had already been quoted by a competitor (Parker estimates over 35% of customers had a competitor's rep already in their home) — he asks if they got a water test done; if yes, he requests it so he has the same data as the competitor without having to do his own in-home visit, letting him compete on price with full information. If no water test and it's a private well, he'll arrange one; for city water customers he instead pulls public EWG water quality reports to show exactly what's in their tap, positioning an in-home sales visit as an unnecessary "sales gimmick."

## Growth and staffing timeline / numbers
- June (month 1): 5 installs, ~$15K revenue, ~$3,000 operating profit (no real profit taken — reinvested into inventory). Ad spend ~$100/day (~$2K for the month).
- July (month 2): ~16 jobs, ~$49,800 revenue, operating profit lower than June's rate due to sales/follow-up bottlenecks — founder was a one-man show and got overwhelmed by lead volume; had to pause ad spend in the back half of the month. Ad spend ramped from $300/day to $400/day then to ~$0 once overwhelmed.
- Mid-August: hired an "operations manager" (Jack) — first tried him purely as a sales/follow-up caller, which didn't work well because of disconnect with scheduling/plumber coordination; switched him to own the full lead-to-completed-job process end to end, which worked much better. Jack is paid a base salary plus a flat $200 per closed job (not commission-based, specifically to avoid incentivizing him to oversell people who don't need the product).
- August 15–31 (17 days) at full $200/day ad spend: 89 fresh leads, 70% contact rate (62 leads reached by text/call), 18 closed (~30% of contacted leads, ~20% of all leads) at an average ticket of $3,300 (many buy the RO upsell). 
- August total: $81,000 revenue from ~25 jobs; gross profit per job settled around $750–$800 after paying Jack's $200/job fee; total operating profit for the month roughly $18K-ish (no owner profit taken yet — still reinvesting in inventory).
- September (in progress at filming): $54,000 in installs already on the calendar from August leads; targeting $150K+ / ~47 installs for the month, which would need about 2.5–3 plumbers working roughly full-time (each doing ~16 jobs/month at ~$500/job).
- Total timeline to $81K/month: 85 days (about 3 months) from launch.

## Operations detail
- Inventory: kept on hand deliberately (not made-to-order) to avoid the biggest recurring bottleneck — running out of stock and having zero installs some weeks (happened in week 3 of July and week 2 of August, costing him installs during otherwise his best month). Stores inventory in a 10x20 storage unit (~$125–150/month) with a smart lock and individual access codes for each plumber and the delivery driver; visits only ~twice/month to break down freight (LTL, palletized) deliveries.
- If you don't want to carry inventory (to keep upfront costs low), Parker advises taking a customer deposit at time of sale to prevent them from walking away or taking a competing offer while inventory ships in — a technique he does not use himself since he stocks ahead.

## Caveats / qualifiers
- Founder explicitly says his $175 CAC is unusually cheap and not to expect it to persist; plan around $300–350 CAC as more realistic and still profitable.
- Emphasizes not to judge sales/marketing performance in the first month — most closes take multiple touch points over days/weeks, and giving up early on non-responsive leads throws away profitable business.
- This is a physical-goods, high-ticket ($2,700+ average ticket) home-service model — not a low-effort digital business; requires subcontractor relationships, insurance, and inventory financing/storage.
- The specific "water filtration" framing is almost incidental; the transferable lesson is finding any high-ticket trade niche with an entrenched, overpriced incumbent sales process and undercutting it with direct-price advertising plus outsourced skilled labor.
