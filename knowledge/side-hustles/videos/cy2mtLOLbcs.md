# Perfume vending!? 6 Budget Friendly Businesses to Start in 2026
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cy2mtLOLbcs | Published: 2024-07-17

Brainstorm conversation between Chris Koerner and Nik ("HoldCo Bros") -- rapid-fire business/content ideas, most conceptual, but with a real inbound call-center business example and concrete perfume vending machine numbers.

## Idea 1: "Known For" app/content series
- Concept: a Google Maps/Yelp alternative (or content series) that surfaces what a specific place is *known for* locally (e.g., a random rural Arkansas gas station famous for giant soft-serve cones) -- something local review aggregation doesn't easily surface.
- Proposed build method: scrape reviews (Google/Yelp) and use AI to find the most common recurring words/themes per location to auto-detect what a place is "known for."
- Referenced comparable: Sam Parr's early hyperlocal roommate-matching app that went viral using shareable infographics (e.g., "the Jerry Seinfeld of [market]") -- suggested as a growth/marketing template.
- Ultimately reframed as possibly better suited as a content series/YouTube channel (like "Diners, Drive-Ins and Dives" but talking to owners about unit economics) rather than an app.

## Idea 2: "Off the Record" podcast (anonymous insider/whistleblower interviews)
- Concept: long-form podcast/YouTube interviews with people (possibly anonymous) revealing insider secrets about a company, organization, or industry. Compared to the app "Whisper."
- Adjacent idea: anonymous, unfiltered customer/employee feedback aggregation as a product (people want unfiltered "what's this company really like" info vs. biased feedback from friends/family).

## Idea 3: Business trauma/failure-story podcast
- Concept: interview format modeled on the podcast "This Is Actually Happening" (long-form personal story episodes, ~400 episodes, ~1 hour each, edited so only the subject's voice is heard -- no audible interviewer). Applied to business specifically: people's business failure/trauma stories.
- Validation point: Koerner's own viral tweet (~2M views) about being "screwed by two business partners" (a 1,700-word long-form story) suggested real audience appetite for this content type.
- Caveat raised: audience-stated interest ("we want more content about failure") doesn't always convert to actual attention/dollars -- people "speak with their wallets/ears, not their words."

## Idea 4: Niche inbound call center business (concrete example -- the most actionable idea in this video)
- Case study: Donald Span launched a cleaning business in 2012, built an internal inbound call center to handle sales leads (qualifying, scheduling, sometimes closing), then spun that into a standalone niche call-center company selling to *other* cleaning businesses. Sold that company in 2019 for a strong exit. Has since launched a second niche inbound call center targeting the legal cannabis industry.
- Business model: NOT outbound telemarketing -- inbound call handling only (answering, qualifying, scheduling, sometimes closing) for other businesses' sales leads, sold as a service to a specific vertical rather than as a generic "call center."
- Economics cited: ~$10,000/month revenue per agent, billed at $1.50-$3/minute of talk time. Agent cost: $3,000-4,000/month if US-based, ~$1,500/month if international -- yields 80-85% gross margin.
- Time investment: founder reports spending ~10 hours/week running the first (cleaning) call center at scale, even less on the current cannabis one, partly due to having an operating partner.
- Average customer retention: 22 months; customer acquisition cost ~$300 -- implies a churn rate under ~5%/year (roughly <1%/month), an unusually sticky B2B service.
- Framed as effectively "a Shepherd/VA marker-upper": source a niche-specialized virtual assistant (or team), train them to answer/qualify/convert calls for a specific vertical, and mark up the rate -- lower operational complexity post-onboarding than the "call center" label implies.
- Reference comp: Ruby Receptionist (now ruby.com) -- cited as proof of a large addressable market: "$300 billion industry growing at 6.7%/year" per figures the guest shared. Ruby's listed served industries (per their site): legal, home services, business services, healthcare, financial services, small businesses -- used as a signal of which verticals are provably sellable.
- Specific vertical opportunity discussed in depth: home health & hospice after-hours on-call answering services. Every home health/hospice agency needs 24/7 on-call coverage (hospice must respond any time a patient is actively dying); most outsource this rather than rotating in-house clinicians. Chris has direct industry experience/network here (mentioned a prior home health/hospice business) and floated launching this specifically, using an existing operator contact to run quality control while VAs/an outsourced "Shepherd" service handles staffing.
- Caveat on niche selection: Chris had already tried something similar once before (a call center for real estate agents, "Assistant Realtor") and it didn't stick -- attributed partly to picking a lower-opportunity vertical and partly to weak execution/follow-through. Selling into small blue-collar owner-operators (HVAC, plumbing) is described as very hard because they're "set in their ways"; better-fit customers tend to be newer/younger operators (hypothesized: someone in their 30s starting a business fresh, not a 56-year-old who's run one for 20 years).
- Suggested cheap validation method before building: call 5 people you know in the target industry, ask directly if they'd want/pay for the service, and get a real commitment (not just a "would you theoretically use this") before investing further.

## Idea 5: Novelty vending machines
- Anecdote (not something they plan to build): a bar in Lansing, MI had a urinal-embedded device measuring pee stream strength/volume, displaying a leaderboard vs. other bar patrons, requiring payment to use and running ads during use -- cited purely as a wild example of vending/monetization creativity, not a recommended venture.
- **Perfume/cologne vending machines** (the idea Chris actually likes and pursued -- see also the year-in-review video for later results):
  - Machines currently made/sold out of Europe (mentioned Latvia/Estonia) and used mainly in Europe, Dubai, and Saudi Arabia/Middle East -- not yet common in the US at time of recording.
  - Machine cost: ~$2,000-2,500; can be pedestal-mounted or wall-mounted; roughly the size of a large mailbox; holds 5 bottles of cologne/perfume behind glass, each with a credit-card-activated dispensing tube.
  - Pricing: $1 per spray via card swipe.
  - Margins: 90%+ -- each bottle holds ~300 sprays, so per-bottle revenue vastly exceeds bottle cost.
  - Added monetization: can run ads on the machine's screen; QR codes under each bottle link to an affiliate purchase link for the full-size bottle (no e-commerce/inventory needed on the operator's part -- pure affiliate commission, cited around $10 per bottle sold).
  - Cultural-fit risk debated: perfume/cologne vending is more common in regions with less deodorant use; unclear if American demand supports it the same way. They partially fact-checked with ChatGPT: top fragrance-exporting countries were France and Spain, but top fragrance-*consuming* countries by dollar sales were US ($8.7B in 2023), Brazil (~$4B), France ($2.6B), UK ($2.3B), Germany ($2B) -- suggesting real US consumer demand exists despite the cultural-fit concern. Turkey noted as having the highest % of population regularly wearing perfume (85%) globally.
  - Suggested mitigation for the cultural-fit risk: place machines in 10-20 different location types and learn empirically rather than assuming; candidate high-traffic placement ideas raised: gym locker rooms/exits (post-workout use case), busy mall common areas, movie theater lines.

## Caveats raised generally
- Speak with intended customers directly and get a real commitment/price before building anything (applies to the call-center idea specifically).
- Anecdotal enthusiasm ("there's not enough content about X") doesn't guarantee people will actually pay attention or pay money -- validate demand with real signals, not just stated interest.
