# Make $1K an Hour With This Simple Side Hustle
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WydE1wzv2_Y | Published: 2025-10-22

On-location interview with Wesley (owner of a Grounds Guys franchise in Keller, TX) and Jeremy (his production manager) about the Christmas/holiday lights installation business, plus lawn care comparison.

## Business overview and market size
- Holiday lighting is pitched as a seasonal add-on to a landscaping business, valued for high margins and low competition relative to mowing.
- Lawn mowing margins cited as only 10-15%, especially thin on commercial accounts due to high competition.
- One franchisee in the Grounds Guys network reportedly does $1.25M/year just in Christmas lights and landscape lighting combined (breakdown not given, but described as largely commercial accounts — estimated 200-400 commercial customers to hit that revenue level).
- Wesley's own 3-year-old holiday lighting operation: goal of $500K-$1M/year within 2-3 years across holiday + landscape lighting combined.
- Commercial customers can be worth "100x" a typical residential customer (one commercial account cited around $40,000-$50,000 in revenue).

## Pricing
- Average home installation (roof-line lighting on a 3,000-4,000 sq ft, two-story, three-car-garage house): $1,500-$2,000 for the first year, priced by linear foot of roofline (measured with a measuring wheel).
- Minimum job price: ~$800, typically for single-story homes with 150-200 linear feet of wire.
- Returning customers get a 10% discount each of years 2 and 3, then price holds flat thereafter as long as they stay with the same provider — customer retention is high because switching providers annually is inconvenient and the price doesn't keep dropping past year 3.
- Permanent (year-round) lighting installs cost 2-3x a standard seasonal install, but the interviewee says he personally dislikes offering permanent lighting because it's a one-time sale rather than recurring seasonal revenue (only does it when customers insist).
- No charge for a customer changing bulb color between seasons (e.g., warm white to red/green) since the lights are already leased/owned by the company and get re-bulbed anyway; could be offered as a paid upsell but currently isn't.

## Two business models for holiday lights
1. **Lease model (Wesley's preference):** the company owns, cuts-to-fit, stores, installs, and takes down the lights each year; customer never touches them. Lights are reusable on a different house if a customer cancels — keeps material costs down.
2. **Sell/own model (used by some competitors):** company cuts lights to fit the customer's home and sells them outright; if the customer doesn't renew, they keep the (already custom-fit) lights, meaning the installer must buy new material for the next customer.

## Margins (with employees installing, not owner-operator)
- Year 1: ~20-25% margin (installer pays for all new material — cited as ~$200 in material for a $1,500 job, with most of the cost being labor for cutting/fitting).
- Year 3+ (same customer, no re-cutting needed): 50-60% margin.
- Margins would be higher still if the owner does installs personally rather than paying employee labor.
- Bulbs last 5-6 years before significant degradation (faster degradation from UV/sun exposure, notably in Texas); defective bulbs from a bulk order are typically replaced free by the manufacturer.

## Costs and operations
- Insurance: ~$12,000/year total (auto insurance, general liability, workers' comp) for the described operation — roughly $1,000/month. Holiday lighting and ice-melt/snow services are rated by insurers as higher-risk activities, raising premiums versus a lawn-only operation.
- Startup material costs: as little as $400-$500 to get started with one reel of wire, some lights, and fittings; Wesley's own business spent $4,500-$5,000 in year one buying material in bulk to service ~15 homes. Buying material in bulk lowers per-foot cost.
- Home Depot-bought lights (fixed 25/50/100 ft lengths) are not recommended for professional installs since they don't cut-to-fit; recommends sourcing bulk socket wire/bulbs from a holiday-lighting supply distributor instead (mentioned coming from Utah, with local supply stores generally available in most markets).
- Labor time per job (once material is pre-prepped/pre-bulbed in the shop): ~30-60 minutes shop prep, ~1-1.5 hours on-site install for an average home, and only ~10 minutes to take down (using a painter's pole from the ground, no ladder needed for teardown if installed properly). Route density (minimizing drive time between jobs) is called out as a major margin driver since drive time can exceed on-site labor time.
- Crew size: minimum of 2 people per job (one on ladder, one on ground feeding material/holding ladder) for safety and liability reasons, even on single-story homes; 3 people on larger jobs (2 installing on roof, 1 doing ground-level bed/driveway stake lights).
- Trees are far more labor-intensive/expensive to light than roofline and are less commonly requested — roofline is described as "the most bang for your buck."
- Equipment: wire clippers, zip ties, a 30-foot ladder, "Cougar Paws" roof-grip boots, and a "pitch wedge" (a felt-padded platform for standing on sloped roofs) are the core safety/tool kit.
- Product detail: uses cut-to-length two-wire "socket wire" bought in bulk rolls (500-5,000 ft), with punch-through male/female fittings that don't require stripping wire, plus "zip cord" (non-lit connector wire) to bridge gaps without hanging bulbs where they shouldn't show. Pre-bulbing and pre-clipping wire in the shop before going to the job site minimizes on-roof time.
- Lights are installed on timers (included in price) so customers don't need to manage on/off manually.
- Wire runs/strands are labeled (e.g., color-coded zip ties marking which peak each strand belongs to) when taken down, to make next year's reinstall faster and cheaper.

## Sales/marketing
- Marketing timeline: initial "Christmas in July" outreach campaign starting in July to get customers on the schedule early (early sign-ups get discounts since the fall installation calendar fills up); a second marketing push begins late September/early October.
- Take-down begins the second week of January (after New Year's, allowing for holiday travel).
- Lead generation channels: door hangers (crews hang them on neighboring homes while installing — described as "Box 9": the 2 homes on each side plus 5 homes across the street, creating two touchpoints — visual + physical hanger), Facebook posts (before/after photos) and Facebook paid marketing, and previously yard signs (now noted as illegal in Texas as of the time of filming, so pivoted away from that channel). One competitor cited putting out 80 yard signs/month despite complaints, because it still generated leads.
- Suggested untried tactic: when a lease customer moves, proactively knock on the new homeowner's door and offer a discount since the lights are already custom-fit to that specific house.
- Cross-selling tip for hiring landscapers: ask upfront whether they're willing to get on roofs, since many landscaping hires balk at the actual climbing/rooftop work when it comes time — screen for this before hiring, not after.

## Safety and risk
- Getting on roofs (20-30+ ft up) is explicitly called out as the main liability/risk driver behind pricing and insurance costs — recommended to use ladders as much as possible rather than walking on roofs, and to start with easier single-story homes to build experience.
- Recommends new operators start by practicing on their own home first; framed as low-downside ("worst case you get to keep a ladder for your house").
- Squirrels chewing through wires is cited as a real but infrequent service issue (roughly 1 in 20-30 jobs); repaired via simple snip-and-patch.

## Caveats
- This is a seasonal business (roughly July prep through January teardown) best run as an add-on to an existing landscaping/grounds-service business rather than a standalone year-round company, per the interviewees' own model (they run it under a "Grounds Guys" franchise alongside mowing/landscape lighting/irrigation).
- The $1M+/year example cited is from a large, mature operation with significant commercial account penetration — not representative of a first-year solo operator.
- Communication with customers (proactively flagging problems, e.g., "we hit your mailbox today, we'll fix it") was repeatedly emphasized by both interviewees as the single biggest driver of renewal/retention and reducing complaint escalation.
