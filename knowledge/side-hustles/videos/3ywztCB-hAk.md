# You Can Start This $1M/Yr Business With No Tools and No Crew
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ywztCB-hAk | Published: 2025-08-27

Interview with Wrex Lindsay (Cozy Pros, livecozy.co) on the "home performance" business (insulation, duct sealing, duct cleaning, air scrubbers) run almost entirely via subcontractors, marketed with Facebook ads.

## The business / services offered
- Core services: insulation removal + re-insulation, duct sealing (mainly via "Aerosal" — see below), duct cleaning, air scrubbers, and add-on AC unit sales/roofing referrals.
- Sales process: offer a **free attic inspection** as the lead magnet/ad offer. Every sales rep carries an **infrared camera** to visually show homeowners heat/cold leaking through the attic/ducts/can lights — used as the core sales visual ("show them the picture of the glowing red and yellow... they see their money burning up").
- Typical prescription: remove all existing insulation (most homes have old cellulose insulation, ~6 inches in Phoenix, which degrades and gets dusty over time), seal attic leaks, add R49 insulation (~17 inches, more like R60 in colder climates e.g. Minnesota), then duct-seal (mostly via Aerosal) and often duct clean at the same time.
- **Aerosal**: a duct-sealing technology/company — blocks all vents/registers, pressurizes the duct system, and injects an aerosolized sealant that finds and seals even invisible cracks/holes from the inside (analogized to Fix-a-Flat tire sealant). More expensive than manual taping but far more thorough. Cozy Pros is described as one of the few companies that sells Aerosal as a standalone/main-course service rather than an add-on — Aerosal itself consults with them on how they do this.
- **Air scrubbers**: an ionizing/atomizing air-purification device installed in the AC plenum (supply or return side) that charges dust/pollen/allergens/mold/viruses so they're captured; ~30-minute install.

## Numbers / unit economics
- Average job ticket: ~$10,000 cash price (~$11,000 with financing fees) for a ~2,000-3,000 sq ft home (insulation removal + R49 + duct seal + often duct clean).
- Big jobs: up to $30,000-$40,000 when a home needs "everything" (insulation, duct seal, duct clean, air scrubber, new AC unit).
- Air scrubber unit economics: sells for ~$1,200; costs ~$400 (product) + ~$50 (labor) = ~$450 COGS. Recurring revenue angle: the internal cell needs replacing every 2-3 years, creating a repeat-sale touchpoint.
- Air scrubbers alone did ~$56,000 year-to-date for this company as a side offering; Wrex estimates a business focused solely on air scrubbers could do "a million a year." US air purification industry cited as several billion dollars/year (~$10B/year in the US).
- Standard duct cleaning: ~$800-$1,000 average ticket for a 2,000 sq ft/12-vent home; can be sold entirely over the phone without an in-person inspection. Upsell duct sealing (manual, non-Aerosal) at $400-$500 on top.
- Blended company gross margin: ~52%; net margin target 20% (currently landing ~15%). Cost breakdown as % of revenue: ad spend ~10%, sales commissions ~10-12% (with overrides), overhead ~10%.
- Ad economics: lead cost ~$75/lead via Facebook video ads (rep's face, filmed in an attic, discussing pain points, CTA to book a free inspection). ROAS ("x" on ad spend) ranges 8-9x in a bad month up to 15x in a good month.
- Utility rebates available in Phoenix (SRP utility): $400 rebate for duct sealing, $600 for insulation — plus federal tax credits (noted as expiring "after this year" at time of filming).
- Homeowner savings: DOE estimate of 25-30% reduction in energy bills from a full package; on a $400-500/month Phoenix summer power bill, that's roughly $100-150/month saved, with payback period of 4-6 years.
- Volume: ~60 jobs/month in the Phoenix market handled by a single project manager.

## The subcontractor model (the video's main thesis)
- Cozy Pros is "pretty much" fully subbed out today — subcontractors own their own equipment (insulation blowing/removal machines, duct cleaning machines), so the operator doesn't need capital for equipment.
- Why sub instead of owning equipment in-house: a good insulation-blowing machine costs $15-20K, insulation removal machine ~$10K, duct cleaning machine $5-10K (brush-and-vacuum) or $8-12K (negative-air machine, used for bigger/industrial jobs). Wrex previously ran ~100 employees and all equipment in-house and switched away from that model.
- Subcontractor split: subs get ~48-50% of the $10K ticket (the company procures/marks up materials ~2x, similar to the tree-service business model); the company still handles all Facebook ad marketing, sales, and having a project manager on-site to manage the homeowner relationship and quality.
- Where to find good subs: **best channel is through equipment/material suppliers** (insulation suppliers, Aerosal itself) — they know firsthand which subs do good, high-volume work. Secondary channels: Google search, Facebook Marketplace, and even poaching subs discovered via a competing customer quote (calling the sub named on a rival bid and offering to pay them directly for the job).
- Concerns addressed: quality doesn't necessarily drop with subs — small local sub-companies whose owner is on-site tend to care more about repeat business (often 90% of that sub's business comes from this one company, so they won't risk the relationship). Risk of subs "stealing" the customer relationship is described as a non-issue in practice because the company still manages payment, communication, and keeps a project manager on-site.
- Also cuts overhead: eliminates ~$15,000/month the company was previously spending on rental/replacement equipment when machines broke down in-house; reduces general liability exposure since subs carry their own GL insurance; subs are responsible for damage (e.g., stepping through a roof/ceiling) rather than the company.
- The framework the host draws out explicitly: "you don't need to learn how to install insulation — you need to learn Facebook ads," i.e. subs become "partners" you pay rather than competitors.

## Growth path / market fit
- Recommended entry point: start narrow (e.g., just duct cleaning, or just air scrubbers) as a lower-barrier "self-liquidating" offer, then upsell once you're in the home (e.g., duct cleaning at $800 pays for the ad spend and profit already; ~1 in 4 customers then buy a bigger add-on like an air scrubber or the full $10K package).
- Duct cleaning specifically: two machine types — brush-and-vacuum (cheaper, ~$4-6K, one-person operation, limited to residential/light commercial) vs. negative-air machine (~$8-12K, two-person operation, needed for bigger/industrial jobs, more visually dramatic — the type seen in viral TikTok duct-cleaning videos).
- Best geographic markets: places with **extreme seasonal swings** (very hot summers AND very cold winters) — cited Dallas and Salt Lake City as ideal because pain is felt twice a year, versus mild year-round climates (e.g. San Diego) where demand is weaker. Phoenix works because the business leans on selling "get ready for summer" urgency even in winter.
- Long-term expansion paths mentioned: branching into roofing (comes up often since crews are already in the attic), or building a full standalone duct-work business (new builds, adding returns, full replacement) with tickets reaching $10,000-$15,000+.
- Tech stack: HighLevel (GoHighLevel) used for marketing/lead intake; company has also built a custom CRM in-house over 5-6 years, integrated with HighLevel.

## Caveats
- Insurance costs are called out as "not cheap" and a real, non-trivial line item.
- Finding good insulation subs specifically is harder/more specialized than finding, e.g., landscaping subs — higher upfront vetting effort, though described as worth it.
- Vent/duct cleaning alone has thin margins (~$800-1,000 ticket) — Wrex suggests this specific piece may be worth keeping in-house rather than subbing, unlike insulation/duct-sealing.
