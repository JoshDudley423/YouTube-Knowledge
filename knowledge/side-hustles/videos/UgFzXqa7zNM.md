# The Easiest Way To Start A Business That Almost Never Fails
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UgFzXqa7zNM | Published: 2025-12-09

Interview with Spencer Scott, who built a subscription residential trash-pickup business (Lone Star Trash, Texas) from zero, still driving trucks himself for the first stretch.

## The business idea
- Residential trash pickup sold as a subscription, positioned as a "software business in disguise" — recurring billing, near-zero churn, snowballing via branded bins and referrals.
- Best opportunity window: when an incumbent trash company gets sold to private equity, cuts costs, and starts missing pickups / mishandling billing — angry residents in a Facebook-group neighborhood are primed to switch.

## How he validated and pre-sold (before owning a truck)
- Spotted a neighborhood (400 houses) whose incumbent was failing service after a PE acquisition.
- Posted a no-risk offer to the neighborhood Facebook group: sign up 200 of 400 houses in advance, billed 3 months upfront ($105/customer); if the goal isn't hit, 100% refund.
- Used Stripe's escrow/payout-delay feature to hold funds as long as possible, so a full refund wouldn't cost double transaction fees if the goal failed.
- Got ~150 signups in 10–15 days, ~200 in the first ~45 days = **$15,000 in pre-sales** (~$5K/month recurring) before buying a truck.
- Being totally transparent about having zero trash industry experience did not hurt signups — the risk-free refund guarantee offset it.

## Buying the first truck financed with $0 down
- Bought a used 2006 side-loader ("under CDL" — no commercial driver's license required, one-person robotic-arm operation) sight-unseen via a niche Facebook group called "mom and pop trash group" (not findable by normal search).
- Price: $35,000 truck + $5,000 shipping ("empty-leg" logistics broker arranged the last-mile delivery). Paid half upfront, half later, purely on trust with the seller.
- Financed the truck 100% via a $40,000 personal loan offer that arrived unsolicited by email from American Express (a "just be a good customer" preapproved offer) — 7% interest, 10-year term, ~$700/month payment, not tied to the equipment (personal loan, so the lender can't repossess the truck, but it's tied to him personally).
- Never took a dollar out of pocket: the truck payment was covered by revenue from one day/week of pickups (roughly $5K/month at the time).
- No one taught him to drive/operate the truck except the delivery trucker who dropped it off in a parking lot.
- Advantage of buying an old/high-mileage truck (2006, 200K miles at purchase, 215K a year and a half later): industry veterans said it was "too risky," but ignorance let him get a deal others wouldn't touch; regular oil changes and maintenance keep the diesel engine going — the hydraulic/compactor system is expected to fail before the engine.

## Bins
- Trash bins ("polycarts") are hard to source retail; found via a manufacturer (Cascade, Michigan) that couldn't deliver in his <30-day window, then via a bin liquidator (BNG Sales, Grand Rapids) that resells used bins stripped of branding from companies that no longer want them.
- Bought 200 used bins for ~$10,000 all-in (with shipping); delivered dirty, unbranded — power-washed ~20/day in the driveway and re-stickered with his branding as stickers arrived (some bins went out with the previous company's branding since customers already knew the story).
- Mistake acknowledged: paid cash for bins instead of financing them or putting them on a credit card — this ate most of the $15K pre-sale cash. Lesson: finance/leverage any bulk purchase if the customer base is already paying recurring revenue that can cover it.
- Now manufactures own bins (hot-stamped with logo) rather than buying used.
- Bins left at the curb after a customer moves function as passive billboards/street marketing that drive new-resident signups without any ad spend.

## Sourcing trucks cheap via municipal auctions
- Best-value truck buy: municipal auctions where cities sell off garbage trucks (routinely replaced every ~5 years) — found via Facebook garbage-truck groups (multiple exist: "under CDL trash truck," "four-wheel trash," etc.).
- Bought a 2020 Mack diesel with a Heil "Dura-Pak" body, 50,000 miles, for $60,000 at a live in-person municipal auction in Wichita Falls, TX — same model was worth ~$190,000 on the open market (~$130K instant paper profit / ~70% discount).
- In-person-only auctions are underbid because most buyers won't travel — recommends flying in for these.
- 100% financed via equipment financing preapproved for $150K on the spot at the auction (before even owning a business bank record) — described this equipment financing as "a little sharky": no early-payoff discount (interest is charged in full even if paid off early), and lenders were eager to see him default since repossessing an underpriced truck would be profitable for them. Approval didn't require 2 years in business or filed tax returns — lenders just want "a pulse."
- Recommends buying only from a truck brand/manufacturer with local service support (he uses Heil, which has a Dallas office that can dispatch a repair in 24 hours).
- Floated (as an idea, not something he's doing) that a side-business could exist just calling every municipality to find out their truck-auction schedules and inventory and selling that list, or buying/refurbing/reselling municipal surplus trucks at scale — "could be an eight-figure business."

## Route economics / unit economics
- Route density is everything: a truck holds ~400 houses per trip to the dump; a good side-loader driver can do 15 seconds/house vs. up to a minute/house for a rear-loader. One trip to the dump vs. two saves ~1 hour round trip.
- One experienced driver did 400 houses in ~4 hours, then dump run, finishing a 5-hour day.
- At $9/house/week pricing, a fully dense truck (400 houses, 4-hour route) generates ~$3,600/4 hours (~$900/hour).
- Theoretical max (hypothetical, not real): a single truck running at max density (800 houses/day, 2 dump trips, 30 days/month) could gross ~$210,000/month — illustrative ceiling, not achieved.
- Actual reported numbers: first-year revenue ~$300,000 (all recurring/ARR), roughly $50–60K/month in revenue with ~$30K/month in costs → **~40% gross margin**.
- Monthly cost breakdown (2 trucks): truck loan payments $700 + $1,700 = $2,400/month; insurance ~$1,600/month per truck for physical/liability coverage on the vehicles (separate $1,200/year general liability policy); fuel ~$200/day/truck ×20 driving days = $10,000/month; truck storage (RV storage lot) $200/month for 2 trucks; bin storage unit $400/month; driver salary ~$50–60K/year (main driver, salaried) plus a part-time driver at $17–18/hour.
- Other recurring costs: tire replacements every ~2 weeks, oil changes/repairs, GPS trackers (SAMSARA) with OBD2 engine sensors, forward-facing dash cams (month of storage), bin replacement (~$50/bin).
- Customer acquisition cost (CAC) via Facebook ads: started around $100/customer, optimized down to as low as $35/customer in some months. Facebook ad setup that mattered: install the Meta Pixel and route customers through a Stripe checkout success page so Facebook's algorithm can optimize for actual paid conversions, not just clicks — without pixel/conversion tracking, "Facebook ads don't work" is the common (and wrong) complaint. Recommended ramp: spend ~$300/day for 5–7 days to let the algorithm learn the ideal customer profile.
- Referral program: give a free month of trash service for every neighbor referred; one customer referred 16 houses (won a full year free). Referrals/word-of-mouth are the highest-ROI channel since there's no CAC — but reviews and word-of-mouth alone rarely convert someone; people mostly switch when their *current* provider messes up (missed pickup, billing problems). Marketing's job is to be visible/top-of-mind for when that happens, not to "win business just by being good."
- Churn is extremely low — no customer had ever cancelled due to service quality in ~2 years (2 cancellations total, described as "we fired them"). Because bins stay at the address after a resident moves, a new resident often just calls the same company — effectively a near-zero true address churn. Estimated LTV given ~1%/month churn: ~$3,500 per customer against $30–100 CAC.
- Mistake acknowledged: didn't invest in Google (Business Profile) reviews for the first 1.5 years — hurt local-search visibility once AI/search started prioritizing reviewed local businesses; also didn't try LSA (Local Services Ads, pay-per-lead vs. pay-per-click) ads early, which he'd do differently in hindsight (set at ~$50/day).

## Scaling path / bigger opportunity
- City/municipal contracts are the scale-up target: a mid-size city (~20,000 homes) at ~$40/home/month implies ~$800,000/month; a larger city (~150,000 houses) can be a ~$20M/year contract.
- Winning municipal contracts requires proven reliability (no missed-pickup days) and track record — hard to win before 2 years in business; typically only 2-3 incumbent regional players bid on a given metro's contracts.
- Cited a private, decades-old regional trash operator (initials CWD) as reference point for how big this can get: reportedly ~$200M in trucks alone, owns its own recycling facility, wins essentially every city contract in the Dallas area — framed as evidence a single-operator trash business can become enormous over decades.

## Recommended low-capital path for someone starting from scratch (his advice)
1. Start with bulk/junk pickup using just a truck (e.g., F-150) and Facebook Marketplace leads — no trailer or dedicated truck needed initially, roughly $600/job type pricing.
2. Add a trailer once volume justifies it — can also make dump runs.
3. Build a story/brand publicly (just explain honestly why you're starting and what you didn't like about the incumbent) — this creates a "snowball" of word-of-mouth signups.
4. Layer in a referral program (free month per referral) once you have ~10 customers.
5. Proactively seed 10–20 local neighborhood Facebook groups, watch for complaints about "trash/pickup/garbage," and plant awareness before an incumbent's contract lapses or the company is sold.
6. Subscription/recurring-revenue businesses like this tend to be more valuable/sellable long-term than one-off-job trades businesses (plumbing, HVAC) because they don't "restart" every month.

## Caveats / risks raised
- The pre-sale success was explicitly called "lucky" / a "perfect storm" (an incumbent had just failed publicly) — not reliably repeatable by everyone.
- HOAs and municipalities often won't allow parking a garbage truck at a residential house — his truck got a violation notice after 4 days; requires renting outside storage (~$200/month for 2 trucks in his case, though bin storage adds another $400/month).
- Winning a city contract carries real reliability risk — a service outage (failing to pick up even one day) can be disqualifying, so scale (more trucks/drivers) must be in place first.
- Equipment financing for a brand-new operator can be predatory: no discount for early payoff, full interest charged regardless, and approval doesn't require a business track record — treat these loans cautiously.
- Costs are easy to undercount: tire wear, repairs/maintenance, GPS/dash-cam subscriptions, bin losses, and marketing spend all "nickel and dime" the stated ~40% margin.
