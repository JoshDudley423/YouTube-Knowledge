# How to Make Millions with Sledding, Massage Chairs and Candy
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fLqK7AYjz-s | Published: 2024-06-13

Interview with Matthew, owner of Pigeon Forge Snow (indoor snow tubing) and a former Alpine Coaster co-owner, in Pigeon Forge/Sevier County, TN (tourist area near Dollywood).

## Business ideas discussed
1. **Alpine (mountain) coaster** — turnkey amusement ride business. German manufacturer (Wiegand/"Wiggens") engineers and ships a modular steel track; buyer just needs land with elevation change and a construction crew to assemble it ("giant Lego set"). Very low headcount to operate (3-4 minimum, 6-7 comfortable) relative to revenue.
2. **Indoor snow tubing facility** (Pigeon Forge Snow) — 35,000 sq ft "temperature independent" building (kept ~65-72°F, not subzero), open 365 days/year, staffed in three shifts including an overnight shift that machine-blows fresh snow.
3. **Candy store** (added into the snow facility's gift shop) — bulk/by-the-pound candy, bright colors/lights, novelty items (3-ft gummy snakes as display/gimmick items). High margin, simple to run.
4. **Gem/gold mining attraction for kids** — idea to truck in real dirt from Arkansas (gems) and Colorado/Alaska (gold) to build a mining attraction in a big non-tourist city (e.g., Dallas), since existing gem-mining attractions are only in remote/tourist locations. Cites an actual competitor "Dig World"/"Diggin Zone" in Katy, TX doing ~50,000 customers/year on 3 acres, charging ~$25/person for 2 hours (includes non-participating "grandma" spectators).
5. **Massage chairs as an ancillary revenue add-on** — coin/dollar-operated chairs placed in the facility for non-participating family members (e.g., grandparents); described as the single most profitable line item by margin. Started on a revenue-share deal with a vendor, later bought the chairs outright when the vendor cut the rev-share to 80/20 in the operator's favor... actually vendor kept more — Matthew still continued because volume justified it.
6. **"Illusion of saturation" strategy**: once a concept proves demand (e.g., a hit alpine coaster location), open several near-identical attractions under *different* brand names in the same market rather than one visibly-chained brand — deters competitors from entering (looks saturated) while you secretly share the same backend, email list, and lookalike ad audiences.
7. Mall/high-traffic retail plays: leasing mall parking lots for seasonal pop-up attractions (e.g., a pop-up snow event, easier to run in Nov-Jan than summer); putting novelty attractions (mini gem-mining kits, inflatable bull riding) at multiple entrances of a single large mall to capture different foot-traffic flows.
8. Candy-by-the-pound as a **turnkey add-on service for gas stations**: install a small (~80 sq ft) self-serve bulk candy scale/bin setup at gas stations (compared to the Hunt Brothers Pizza franchise-within-a-store model) — sell at ~80% margin vs. the ~20% margin on packaged candy the gas station already carries.

## Concrete numbers
- Alpine coaster (built ~2014-2015): coaster hardware from German manufacturer cost **$1.6-1.8M**; shipping ~**$500K**; construction ~**$1M** more. Total group investment ~**$1M equity + ~$2.2-2.75M borrowed** (~$3.5-3.75M all-in for a ~1-mile track).
- A more recent (larger, ~4-uptrack) coaster in the same area reportedly cost **$8-10M** to build.
- Matthew's estimate: today, he'd want a budget of **at least $10M**; below that he'd be skeptical of the plan.
- Land: leased ~10-12 acres at **$6,000/month with no price escalators** (handshake deal); an adjacent 5 acres purchased by the ownership group for **~$200,000**. The original/competing coaster's owner reportedly paid **$700-800K for 40-50 acres**.
- Ticket pricing (coaster): **$15-16 for one ride**, discounted second-ride tickets; despite that, people frequently bought multiple re-rides in a single visit lasting 1-2 hours.
- First year revenue for the coaster: **~$2M**, from three revenue lines only — adult/child ride tickets ($12-15), re-ride tickets ($5), and a photo-booth add-on.
- Margins: coaster business ran **50%+ net margins** once past the debt-service/fixed-cost hurdle rate; marginal cost of an additional rider is close to zero (staff and utilities are already running).
- Insurance: roughly **$50-60K/year** in the coaster's first year; rose after a bad stretch of accidents across multiple coasters in the region (including one where a rider broke her arm) but did not double — insurance remained a low single-digit percentage line item.
- Larger competing coasters run carts roughly every **18-20 seconds** at peak efficiency (an automatic magnetic braking system prevents cart collisions).
- Snow facility: 30-50 employees depending on season, operating 3 shifts/day, 365 days/year.
- Candy margins: ~**80% gross margin** even accounting for shrinkage/theft ("if they steal 20%, I'm still fine at 80% margins").
- Gem-mining comparable (Dig World/Diggin Zone, Katy TX): **~$25/person for 2 hours**, ~**$70/adult all-day pass** at the Pigeon Forge competitor, ~50,000 customers/year off just 3 leased acres in a mall parking lot.

## Steps / process notes
- To break into an already-proven-demand market: identify a competitor with a visible line/wait-time problem (a sign of unmet demand) and open a second, similarly-positioned business nearby rather than trying to invent a brand-new category.
- Early guerrilla marketing tactic (pre-privacy-lockdown Facebook): scrape/download a competitor's Facebook page follower list and run retargeted ads directly against that audience with a lower price point.
- Structural note on family-style attractions: always design for a "spectator" experience (something for non-participants to do/watch), which both increases per-visit revenue (via add-ons like massage chairs or snacks) and improves the group's overall satisfaction/likelihood of returning.
- For any high-margin, easily replicated concept, principle is to look for ways to **10x** the experience (bigger, cheaper, more premium) rather than making marginal tweaks.

## Caveats / risks
- Alpine coasters require rare terrain (significant elevation change) and are extremely land/zoning constrained — although the German builder now offers modular "chopped" uptrack/downtrack designs that need less elevation.
- Coasters need constant maintenance: track sections would shift 4-6 inches and need realignment; the business would close 1-4 weeks per year for repairs, denting what would otherwise be a stronger growth year.
- Coaster business is highly weather- and temperature-dependent (rain shields help but slow the ride; extreme cold makes it miserable though people still ride).
- The alpine-coaster market in this region has since become saturated (grew from 1 to ~10 competing coasters), yet Matthew reports none of them appear to have closed — demand has apparently kept pace, which he still finds "baffling" and doesn't fully trust will continue.
- Real accidents happened: a cart-to-cart low-battery collision issue causing whiplash, and one rider (at fault per Matthew) broke her arm from a seatbelt/body-positioning issue; a regional cluster of serious accidents across three coasters occurred in the same week and drew negative press/PR ("Black Thursday").
- The "illusion of saturation" multi-brand strategy requires large capital (an entire portfolio of $8-10M+ builds) and heavy execution/logistics risk (site scouting, securing land options/leases, long build lead times) — Matthew calls it a "grand slam or bust" that could easily be over-leveraged.
- A newer gem-mining attraction he researched (Diggin Zone) reportedly nearly ran out of money mid-construction and is in what Matthew considers a poor (off-the-beaten-path) location, requiring heavy ongoing marketing spend to compensate for the lack of foot traffic — a caution against replicating a concept without also nailing site selection.
