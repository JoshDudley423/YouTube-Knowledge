# How to Build a Thriving Business in the Middle of Nowhere
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kg66hxP9tzc | Published: 2025-05-12

Interview with Al Doan, owner of the Missouri Star Quilt Company (Hamilton, Missouri, population ~1,500) -- turned a single quilt shop into 16 quilt shops, a hotel/retreat center, restaurants, Airbnbs, and an e-commerce empire around quilting. Rich with specific tactics for niche e-commerce, content-driven retail, and rural/small-town business models.

**How the business actually started and grew (origin story)**
- 2008-2009: started with one quilt shop (mom's business) + a "daily deal" e-commerce site (quiltersdailydeal.com, modeled after Woot.com/Steep&Cheap but aimed at an older female demographic). Initial traction was near zero -- first sale came from a relative three weeks in.
- Accidental pricing breakthrough: mispriced a "Crazy Eights" charm pack at $3.88 instead of a higher price (a fat-finger error); sold 11 units even at a loss on the item itself, but most buyers added another item -- this taught him the loss-leader model, which became core to the business.
- Content (not the deal site) is what actually drove growth: started uploading quilting tutorial videos to YouTube in 2008 (when YouTube itself was only ~2 years old), initially low-production (a Canon PowerShot, Windows Movie Maker). Switched from posting many videos/day to one per week (Fridays), which built a reliable traffic/newsletter surge each week. Newsletter reportedly hit 60-70% open rates because it was weekly, high-quality, and led with free value.
- Early low-cost marketing attempts that failed/got him banned: posting links in blog comments, Craigslist/Kijiji -- these got flagged as spam. YouTube ended up being "the only marketable thing" they had since the product catalog (fabric) was too small (<30 SKUs) to sustain a daily-deals model.

**Physical retail / "retail warehouse" model**
- As the business needed more space, he opened additional themed quilt shops instead of a plain warehouse, because in a small town retail and warehouse space cost about the same per square foot -- and fulfilling online orders out of staffed, themed retail locations kept the town's Main Street feeling active/energetic (avoids "customer walks into an empty store" problem). Each shop is themed (boutique fabrics, kids' fabric, novelty, solids, florals, reproductions, wide fabrics, modern, etc.) so it functions as inventory-driven merchandising and a "Disneyland for quilters" experience simultaneously.
- Example of retail-as-experience: converted an old butcher shop into a fabric store where customers buy fabric by the pound from behind a counter, wrapped in butcher paper -- explicitly designed as a memorable, non-replicable in-person experience layered on top of standard e-commerce.
- Now owns ~18 of ~20 buildings in Hamilton's downtown district; each building typically bought for ~$20,000 with another ~$100,000+ invested in structural/interior renovation (~$200-300K all-in per building).
- Built physical infrastructure signaling "this town matters": added sidewalks, a town plaza/square (~50 ft setback from the road), streetlights -- deliberately manufacturing the feel of an established town center from what had been a single-strip Main Street.

**"Own the niche capital of the world" business model (the core transferable idea)**
- Central thesis: pick any hobby/niche with passionate practitioners who are otherwise "ignored" or isolated in their interest (quilting, sourdough baking, woodworking, cheese-making, saltwater aquariums, beekeeping), then build a physical destination town/district that gives that community an identity and gathering place -- "a Comic-Con for that space." States there are "10,000 small towns" that could each replicate this for a different niche.
- **Recommended execution sequence** (explicit step-by-step, given for a hypothetical "sourdough bread town"):
  1. Be (or partner with) an existing company/brand in the niche first -- don't build the town speculatively. Cites a counter-example (a "creator town" built outside Austin with no underlying audience/company) that failed because nobody came.
  2. Buy small/cheap, undervalued buildings in a struggling small town -- budget roughly $20,000/building plus renovation financed over ~30 years (example given: ~$3,000/month all-in for a building).
  3. Buy at least 3 buildings/storefronts (2 is "fine" but 3 is the recommended minimum) so you can credibly claim a "capital of the world" tagline for marketing/billboard purposes -- differentiate each building by sub-theme (e.g., raw ingredients/starters store, tools/equipment store, and a finished-goods bakery/cafe).
  4. Staff minimally -- as few as one employee who moves between buildings depending on foot traffic ("run it like a fairway at Six Flags").
  5. Build an audience by documenting the build-out process itself as content (renovation progress, sketches, behind-the-scenes) -- the audience becomes visitors once the physical thing exists.
  6. Add revenue-generating experiential offerings on top: classes/workshops (e.g., a paid interactive cooking-class model he experienced personally, or paid sourdough classes/stencil kits), since experiences justify travel and drive additional per-visitor spend.
  7. Media/PR follow for free: unusual, novel destination concepts (a "quilt town," a hypothetical "fish town," etc.) reliably attract press ("Wall Street Journal, CBS Sunday Morning, NBC Nightly News, NPR") because it's a story outlets want to tell.
- Visitor/traffic data point: Hamilton now gets ~150,000 visitors/year (tracked via Shopify order zip codes), versus essentially zero visitors before the quilt-shop cluster existed (previously just a gas station/Subway stop).

**Magazine spin-off ("unexpected cash cow")**
- Repurposed existing YouTube tutorial content into a print magazine (bundling the last ~10 tutorials per issue), mailed bimonthly. Priced at $6-8/issue against an $8 printing cost per unit -- explicitly a loss-leader/subscription-retention play rather than a profit-per-unit product, monetized through the fabric sales it drives, not ad sales (deliberately avoided building an ad-sales team).
- Needed 10,000 subscribers to break even -- reportedly hit that within about 5 hours of launch, later scaling to "tens of thousands" of subscribers; described as generating "several million dollars a year" in downstream revenue just from repurposed YouTube content, functioning as a way to resurface catalog products (30,000+ SKUs) that tutorials/newsletters alone couldn't showcase fast enough.

**Two adjacent home-services subscription ideas discussed (not run by Al/Chris, brainstormed live)**
1. **Neighborhood deals/maintenance membership** ("Costco + Groupon + home services"): residents pay a small annual fee (e.g. $20/year) for access to a curated list of local service deals; the platform aggregates demand within a neighborhood to negotiate bulk/route-based pricing with providers (example: a pressure washer charges $350 for one driveway but $250/house if doing 10 homes in one neighborhood), then passes savings to members while the aggregator captures a margin/fee.
2. **Recurring home-maintenance subscription** ("dude in a truck" model): homeowners pay a flat monthly fee (examples cited: $200-500/month) for quarterly scheduled visits covering routine home maintenance (water heater draining, filter replacement, fridge cleaning, etc. -- tasks homeowners know they should do but never do) plus on-demand dispatch for urgent issues (e.g., a broken light bulb) via a low-cost remote dispatcher (example: a Filipino virtual assistant triaging incoming texts). Revenue math sketched: one provider doing ~1 job/day, ~90 days/quarter → ~60-70 homes serviced per quarter at $200-1,000/month each. Optional upsell: bulk-negotiated home goods (filters, toilet paper, etc.) delivered as part of the membership.
3. Both ideas assessed favorably by both hosts as viable, untested business concepts they'd like to see someone build.

**Other rural/small-scale real estate ideas mentioned**
- Roll-up small multifamily: a 500-person town can't support a 100-unit apartment complex, but building several ~10-unit complexes across multiple small towns within an hour's drive of each other (shared property management) can achieve similar economies of scale to one large complex, while each small deal (~$1M) is easier to finance than one large ($10M) deal -- eventually attractive for institutional roll-up.
- Small-town RV parks as a similar model (cited example: a friend who bought a 6-unit RV park in a small Utah town, fully occupied, all rent paid online with minimal on-site labor).
- "Rural Airbnb network" idea: place small multi-bedroom Airbnb units outside major cities (better suited to families/kids than hotel rooms), targeted at ~weekly rather than nightly turnover to keep cleaning labor efficient, with the option to convert underbooked units to $700/month long-term rentals (e.g., to young people wanting to move out of their parents' home).
- Small-footprint rural retail/vending concept: a members-only (key-fob access), open-shelf "grab and go" store (not a vending machine, not locked behind glass) as a way to bring a convenience-store-like shopping experience to towns too small (under ~3,000 population) for chains like Dollar General to serve profitably with staffed retail.

No specific take-rate/margin figures were given for the quilt shops' overall revenue; the magazine's economics ($6-8 cover price vs. $8 print cost, "several million dollars a year") are the most concrete numbers in the episode.
