# How to Find Customers So Big That They Kinda Freak You Out
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S4JeVMhaXME | Published: 2024-07-26

Co-hosted riff episode ("Old Co-Bros") with Nik (Nikonomics). Topics: masterminds as a business/education model, "white whale" strategic partnerships, tedious-task moats, and AI-assisted cold outreach.

## Masterminds (peer group programs) as a business model
- A mastermind is a paid peer group for professionals seeking self-improvement, business growth, network access, and knowledge; costs range from ~$1,000/year to $100,000+/year.
- Case example (Justin Donald, real estate investor/"lifestyle investor"): belongs to 14 masterminds simultaneously, spends an estimated $350,000-$500,000/year total on them (not one-time fees -- recurring annual cost). Treats it like a second full-time job.
- Two value drivers he cites: (1) tailored, highly relevant knowledge from people who've actually done the specific thing (e.g., learning about bonus depreciation, accelerated depreciation, cost segregation studies directly from practitioners rather than a course/degree); (2) network effects -- e.g., access to a family-office contact managing money for ultra-wealthy families, letting him "tag along" on their investment deals.
- He also runs his own mastermind: $55,000/year, targeted at professionals who want to co-invest in private deals.
- Selection-bias dynamic discussed: if a mastermind attracts people looking for a "silver bullet" (e.g., Tai Lopez-style buyers), it degrades in quality because it selects for less serious members; a $50,000/person mastermind (cited example: "Bigger Pockets" founder's personal mastermind, 50 members x $50k = $2.5M/year, selective application process, extra-cost retreats) filters for real operators.
- Psychological dynamics noted: (1) sunk-cost/justification bias -- high-ticket buyers rationalize the purchase regardless of actual value; (2) paying a lot creates real incentive to actually network and engage (vs. a free mixer where showing up/talking to people is optional).
- The hosts' own paid-education experiments: a "Tree Biz Bootcamp" (in-person, $2,500 virtual / $5,000 in-person) and an "RV Park Investing Masterclass" ($3,000, virtual) -- both described as successful/well-received once done with heavy production value, despite the hosts' initial discomfort ("feels slimy") charging that much.
- Caveat/critique: many masterminds are described as "garbage," effectively selling hope/access to people who won't execute (compared to Amazon FBA course sellers who found teaching more profitable and easier than actually selling on Amazon).

## "White whale" strategic partnerships (chasing one big customer/partner)
- Definition: landing one large company as a customer/partner that becomes the foundation of an entire business.
- Case example: Alchemy (Irish company) raised $2.5M in seed funding and built its entire business around managing Apple's iPhone trade-in/buyback program -- reportedly now does $440M/year in revenue, without Apple owning any equity in it. Framed as possibly linked to Apple's overseas profit-sheltering strategy in Ireland (repatriation tax context).
- Host's personal white-whale attempt: while running "Phone Restore" (4 iPhone repair stores), cold-emailed and got a call with Best Buy's CMO in 2010 pitching a repair partnership (citing precedent: Best Buy's in-housing of Geek Squad and Magnolia Audio Visual). Got ghosted after an initial positive-seeming call -- didn't work out, but Best Buy later publicly acknowledged services/repair as an underexploited profit center.
- Proposed "White Whale Fund" concept: invest small amounts (~$25,000 checks, "single digit percentage equity") into founders chasing a specific big-partner dream -- most fail, a rare hit could return 1,000x.
- Leveled-up version: instead of funding outside hustlers, recruit mid-level executives/managers inside large companies (making ~$250k/year, "golden handcuffs") who already see an internal opportunity/pain point but won't act on it -- offer them a ~$500,000 check to cover 2-3 years of salary plus 20% equity if they quit and build the spinout, after having a lawyer review their non-compete.
- AI-assisted outreach idea: build a target list (e.g., 100-200 executives/companies), feed it to an LLM (ChatGPT) to pull specific, personalized details about each contact from their public web presence/social media/press releases, generate a templated-but-personalized outreach message referencing that detail, then send via a mail-merge/multichannel service like ClickSend.com (physical "handwritten-style" letters, SMS, etc.) to scale what used to be manual, time-intensive personalized prospecting (cited real example: a founder targeting 100 skilled nursing facilities for acquisition via ClickSend got 3-4 leads worth potentially millions).
- Related tool named: ringless voicemail software (drops a voicemail without the phone ringing; can now voice-clone and insert the recipient's first name) -- caution that this cannot be used on Do Not Call List numbers.

## "Tedious task as a moat" businesses
- Thesis: a defensible moat often doesn't require unique IP or a novel idea -- just being willing to do something extremely tedious/unscalable that competitors won't bother with.
- Example: Plaid built its moat by manually building/learning APIs for thousands of small credit unions and banks (not just major banks) so fintech apps like Venmo could connect to any bank -- expensive and tedious (~$15k in developer hours per integration, per the hosts) but became a valuable, hard-to-replicate data layer.
- Example: Odeko (founded by a Squarespace co-founder) is a middleman/software+marketplace connecting coffee growers/suppliers with independent local coffee shops (excluding big chains like Starbucks) -- handles supply chain and software so shops don't need to negotiate with big distributors (Cisco) or cold-call growers directly. Grew from $0 to $150M in 2 years. Started manually/unscalably: founder visited 3 coffee shops within a 5-minute walk of his house daily to learn their needs before building the product.
- Generalized strategy suggested: identify a niche/artisan product category consumers already want (e.g., specialty coffee, craft beer, artisan chocolate/food), find one authentic small producer abroad (example cited: a company that sourced traditional 3-ingredient corn tortillas from a single small corn grower in Oaxaca, Mexico and built a viral consumer brand around that one relationship), and become the wholesale/distribution middleman capturing margin -- education of the end consumer about wanting the niche product is the hardest part, so it's easier to serve already-educated demand than create new demand.
- Caveat: marketplace businesses (two-sided, e.g., Odeko-style) are explicitly called "really hard to build" because you must simultaneously recruit buyers and sellers -- a slow, capital- and education-intensive process even when the long-term outcome can be large.
