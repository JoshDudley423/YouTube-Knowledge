# How to Start a Lead Generation Business That Prints You Cash
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j9ovHBUEC_I | Published: 2024-07-24

Brainstorming session with Connor Gross (e-commerce operator, self-storage wholesaler, writes the Biz Brainstorms newsletter). Wide-ranging idea generation covering research marketplaces, food/supplement brands, fitness-niche businesses, lead-gen sites, and subscription-model conversions.

## "Tegus"-style expert research marketplace model
- Business described (Tegus, a real company): connects capital/institutions (e.g., a hedge fund wanting expertise on an industry like self-storage) with industry experts/practitioners for paid consulting calls.
- Key differentiator vs. competitors (e.g., Catalant, AlphaSights): Tegus does NOT mark up the expert's hourly rate to the client — instead it charges the paying customer (the fund/researcher) a flat annual platform fee (~$5,000-$10,000/year, per Connor's estimate) for unlimited access to call any expert on the platform at that expert's own rate, "Costco-style."
- The real value/moat: every call is transcribed, and (with sensitive/incriminating details scrubbed) the resulting transcripts and key insights become a searchable internal database available to other paying subscribers after a 2-3 week embargo — meaning the platform accumulates crowdsourced proprietary research data over time without having to generate the content itself.
- Experts pay nothing to join and only earn hourly fees for calls taken — a no-brainer, zero-downside pitch for recruiting the supply side.
- Scale cited: ~3,000 paying customers, but the company is valued at $4 billion — illustrating a business model where a relatively small customer count can still support outsized valuation due to high-value/high-margin subscription revenue plus a compounding data asset.
- Generalized takeaway: "building a research company that can turn into a data product" is seen as a broadly valuable, low-cost-of-goods pattern — you extract a sellable data product ("the sawdust") from work you're already doing for another purpose.

## Applying the same model to a niche: food-brand retail placement broker + data product (real friend's business, food industry)
- Origin story: a friend started an artisan bison jerky brand, discovered that getting into small independent grocers or major chains (e.g., Kroger) is hard because small stores don't publish buyer contact info (they don't need to) and large chains have opaque, hard-to-navigate org charts for reaching the right category buyer.
- Pivoted from making the product to selling a service built on that exact pain point: acts as an outsourced "business development arm" for small independent food/beverage brands, cold-calling and pitching retail buyers on the brand's behalf.
- Pricing/economics: charges a small flat/recurring fee plus a percentage of the resulting purchase order value ("eats what he kills") — recurring revenue, but only captures partial upside per deal, functioning essentially as a broker.
- Second, higher-margin revenue stream layered on top: packages the buyer contact database he's built (small-store owner contacts plus large-chain category-buyer org charts with names/emails/phones, updated daily) and sells it as a standalone monthly-subscription data product to brands that don't want to pay for the full outreach service — essentially monetizing his own CRM as a byproduct of the core service, at near-zero incremental fulfillment cost since the data already exists from doing the primary business.
- Explicit comparison: the hosts note this data-selling sideline will likely end up more profitable than the underlying service business itself, since niche food/beverage brands themselves are typically low-margin, cash-constrained, and often unprofitable (a related past venture, "Send Eats," a 3PL logistics company for niche food brands, is cited as evidence that servicing food brands directly is a "brutal, tough business" even when the service itself is viable) — better to be positioned on the data/services side of a hard, capital-intensive product category than in the product category itself.

## Cautionary framing on venture-scale food/CPG brands as a business choice
- Anecdote: "Eat Your Coffee" (a real caffeinated protein-bar-style product, Northeastern University-founded) scaled, raised funding, got into retail, but had only an "okay" exit — not a retirement-changing outcome despite real effort and traction.
- General critique of food/CPG brand economics: most such businesses generate little to no real profit while growing, because growth requires continuously raising and spending cash on new inventory — profitability (if it comes) is realized only via an eventual large acquisition exit (RxBar, sold to Kellogg, cited as the aspirational reference case) rather than ongoing cash flow.
- Direct advice given (paraphrased) to a hypothetical founder pitching a novel snack concept (e.g., a "seaweed chocolate bar"): being a hard-working, idea-driven entrepreneur will likely lead to success in something, but CPG/food specifically is a harder path to real financial outcomes than other categories — worth considering opportunity cost before committing years to it.

## Electrolyte/supplement market observations
- Cited financial performance of LMNT (electrolyte supplement brand): roughly $130-200 million topline revenue with an estimated ~22% margin, implying roughly $30-40+ million in bottom-line profit — used as evidence of a large, provenly monetizable niche (electrolyte/hydration supplements) built on a simple, low-COGS powder product.
- Underlying market thesis discussed: mainstream nutritional guidance has historically pushed "reduce sodium," but for people who are physically active, sweat regularly, or need sustained cognitive focus, typical salt intake may actually be insufficient — creating demand for electrolyte replenishment products.
- Personal DIY example from the host: created his own homemade electrolyte drink ("Busy Juice" — lemon, stevia, caffeine, plus "lite salt" for potassium/sodium and added magnesium) after becoming uncomfortable with artificial dyes in commercial flavor packets (e.g., MiO); drinks it daily but has not commercialized it, citing the general difficulty/undesirability of competing in a CPG/food category (all the "hard things" of e-commerce plus food-specific complexity) despite believing in the product.
- Proposed under-served sub-niches within the electrolyte category (unbuilt ideas):
  - A version specifically formulated/marketed for intermittent fasting practitioners (branding idea: "Fast" or similar) — positioned as "won't break your fast," taken first thing in the morning before coffee, with the same high-margin (~80-90% gross margin) supplement-style economics as existing brands.
  - Sport-specific variants (e.g., "for soccer," "for football," "for endurance/ultra running") distributed via free sample packets included in race-day packets at endurance events (noting competitor "Nuun" already does something similar) — general critique that most entrants in this space try to be broad/universal ("the next Nike") rather than deliberately niching down.
  - A running-specific variant for people who train fasted (branding idea: "Run Fast") targeting the specific electrolyte-before-a-fasted-run use case.
  - Electrolyte supplement specifically for dogs — inspired by an anecdote (a dog licking sweat off its owner after a run, inferred as seeking salt) — proposed as a co-packed (white-labeled) salt/potassium/magnesium powder marketed to be sprinkled in a dog's water bowl or food, explicitly positioned as an underexploited niche since most dog-supplement/DTC competition (cited: "Farmer's Dog," a $100M+ business) targets dog food, not the water bowl. Flagged risk: potential liability if owners oversalt/harm a pet by over-dosing; a possible mitigation raised (unvalidated) is a solid salt-lick-style format (like deer-hunting salt blocks, broken into smaller pieces) so the animal self-regulates intake rather than a owner-dosed powder.
  - Cautionary real-world anecdote included on electrolyte dosing risk: an experienced ultramarathoner (100+ mile race veteran) had his only career DNF (did not finish) after accidentally using salt packets 3x the strength of what he'd trained with, combined with a drier climate causing him to sweat less than expected — the resulting overdose caused severe swelling/possible bone injury by mile 95, illustrating that even trained athletes can dangerously miscalibrate salt/electrolyte intake.

## Endurance race industry observations (tangential, not a business pitch)
- Anecdotal claim (from a source who races ultramarathons, e.g., Moab 240): race organizers appear to run on thin/questionable economics despite charging real entry fees (cited example: $200/entrant with 3,000 entrants) while also relying heavily on unpaid volunteers — raised as a genuine unanswered question about where race revenue actually goes, not a proposed business idea.

## "Hyrox" fitness niche as an emerging market opportunity
- Hyrox described: a global hybrid fitness competition combining an 8km run with 8 functional workout stations, marketed as a "world series of fitness racing" — reported 40+ global races and ~990,000 athletes/50,000 spectators in 2023; positioned as more accessible than CrossFit Games while still offering the competitive/PR-chasing appeal that draws people to running.
- Observed growth signal: rising Google Trends interest and a growing number of gyms specifically branding around Hyrox training over the past 3-5 years.
- Proposed adjacent business opportunities (analogous to how TRX and Rogue built equipment/brand businesses around CrossFit):
  - Equipment/training-focused brands for the specific machines Hyrox emphasizes (assault bikes, ski-erg machines).
  - A Hyrox-specific supplement brand.
  - A content/news site specifically covering Hyrox.
  - Premium Hyrox-focused gyms bundling extras like blood-work testing and recovery services — a concrete real example cited: a gym being built in Denver marketing itself as the premier Hyrox gym, with average annual member spend reported at $20,000.
- General framing: "build everything that already exists for CrossFit, but for Hyrox" as a repeatable playbook for riding a newer, still-growing niche sport before it's saturated with supporting businesses.

## Core lead-generation business model: programmatic SEO + AI content + paid ads, for high-ticket local services
- Concrete flagship idea discussed at length: "BurpAgain" — a content/lead-gen site targeting people who cannot burp (a real, documented condition affecting a meaningful population; cited: a ~30,000-member Reddit community r/CantBurp, plus a viral Andrew Wilkinson Twitter thread about being cured via Botox injections, believed to target the neck/laryngeal area though explicitly caveated as not medical advice).
- Business model: rank organically (SEO) plus run paid Google ads for terms like "can't burp," "Botox burp," "bloating after carbonated drinks," etc.; build extensive content around the condition and its Botox-based treatment; then sell the resulting leads to local Botox-providing doctors/plastic surgeons, who pay well per lead given the high transaction value of the underlying medical procedure.
- Live validation done during the recording: checked Google Keyword Planner and found ~200,000 average monthly US searches for "Botox," low competition, ~$5 top-of-page bid, 22% year-over-year search growth — plus confirmed the domain "burpagain.com" was available at time of recording.
- Revenue potential estimate (speculative, from Connor): unlikely to become a $10M/year business, but plausibly a "couple hundred thousand dollars per year, mostly profit" business if built out and put on autopilot.
- Legal caveat raised explicitly: uncertain whether selling medical/healthcare leads is legally similar to well-established lead-selling frameworks in legal services (attorney lead-gen is a known, regulated space) — flagged as an open question, not resolved in the episode.

## Generalized programmatic SEO lead-gen playbook (Connor's own in-progress, unnamed business)
- Method: identify a high-ticket, high-search-volume, low-competition local service category; generate AI-written content targeting long-tail keywords; programmatically create landing pages combining each of several service variants with each of ~1,000 U.S. cities (in Connor's case: 3 services x 1,000 cities = 3,000 pages at time of recording, scaling toward 5,000), each with its own lead-capture form.
- Traction reported: only ~236 of ~5,000 generated pages were indexed by Google at time of recording, but the indexed pages were already ranking top-10 for target terms and generating real organic leads (not just clicks) — validated first with paid Google ads before committing to the SEO/content buildout, to confirm demand existed before investing in scaled content production.
- Business-model extension being considered: productizing this into a programmatic-SEO-as-a-service agency — client specifies an industry, the team builds and delivers the full page/content/lead-capture infrastructure for a one-time fee in the $3,000-$7,000 range, and the client then owns/runs the resulting lead-gen asset.
- Two critical success criteria emphasized for choosing a niche:
  1. The underlying product/service must be high-ticket and/or highly recurring (high lifetime value) — low-ticket affiliate products (e.g., a prior failed attempt in the dental space selling water picks/retainers via an affiliate site called "Superior Smile") are explicitly cited as not worth the time/effort even with real traffic.
  2. You need buyers for the leads who operate at national/multi-location scale (a company with hundreds of locations who will buy every lead you send) rather than having to manage relationships with many small, "flaky" local blue-collar business owners individually — described as the harder-to-satisfy but ultimately more important criterion, since finding the overlap of high-ticket + high-search-volume + low-competition + a nationwide buyer is "the gold mine" but genuinely hard to locate.
- Missed-opportunity lesson shared: Connor previously ran a tiny-house blog that ranked well for informational terms ("best appliances for tiny house," "best dishwasher for tiny house") and generated original content, but only made $1,000-2,000/month in profit because he treated it as a low-priority content/affiliate site rather than building out lead generation for tiny-house builders (a $20,000-30,000+ purchase) — retrospectively judged as a much bigger missed monetization opportunity than the low-ticket affiliate approach he actually used.
- Google AI/search-disruption risk acknowledged but downplayed for the near term (as of the July 2024 recording): AI-generated search summaries were seen as low quality enough that users were already trained to skip past them to familiar organic results; localized, long-tail search terms (example: "fence repair Clarksville, Tennessee" returning zero relevant results) are seen as likely to remain valuable to Google's AI specifically because there's no better answer for it to surface, unlike broad/competitive nationwide terms.
- Broader strategic framing: compared this opportunity to private-equity roll-ups — thousands of niches exist that nobody has thought to apply this exact SEO/lead-gen model to yet (analogy given: someone rolled up music schools in Denver alone and sold that portfolio for $40 million, an obscure niche nobody would think to target first), meaning even though "growth hackers" have used this tactic for years, there remains a long tail of un-mined micro-niches, including brand-new/emerging categories (e.g., lead gen specifically for first-time Hyrox race sign-ups) that create fresh opportunities as new niches emerge over time.

## Subscription-model conversion of traditionally one-off/infrequent services
- Core idea: take a business model where customers traditionally pay per-visit for an infrequent service, and convert it to an unlimited-use monthly subscription — profitable specifically when the service's marginal cost per use is low and actual usage frequency remains modest even under an "unlimited" plan (classic "gym membership" psychology/economics).
- Concrete real example cited: an entrepreneur in South Florida is executing a roll-up strategy buying chiropractic clinics and converting their pricing from pay-per-visit (e.g., ~$120/visit) to a flat recurring monthly membership (e.g., ~$200/month for unlimited adjustments) — deliberately borrowing the recurring-revenue economics common in software/SaaS and applying it to a traditionally transactional healthcare service, explicitly chosen because chiropractic adjustments have low marginal cost and most members won't over-use "unlimited" access enough to make it unprofitable.
- Another real example cited: unlimited monthly car wash subscription plans (e.g., pay $25/month instead of $15/wash) — profitable because water/soap marginal cost is negligible and actual usage frequency under "unlimited" remains low relative to the price paid.
- Additional untested but discussed candidate categories for the same subscription conversion: unlimited teeth whitening, and unlimited haircuts (framed as a good fit specifically for people who prefer very short, frequently-maintained hairstyles, since each visit is fast) — suggested go-to-market approach: partner with a single existing barbershop to pilot the unlimited-subscription model before rolling it out at scale (e.g., across many acquired barbershop locations), to validate real usage patterns and guard against a small number of high-frequency/high-cost customers eroding margins.
- Explicit caveat: this pattern doesn't generalize to every service — AMC's "Movie Pass" style subscription is referenced as a cautionary counterexample (most customers won't attend a movie theater often enough for unlimited access to pencil out for the operator, unlike a car wash or chiropractic adjustment).
