# Secrets to Making BIG Profits from Small Email Lists
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1-7Nkz6bTgU | Published: 2025-05-07

Interview with Tyler Denk, co-founder/CEO of Beehiiv, on niche newsletter monetization, growth tactics, local newsletters, and turning existing corporate email lists into standalone media businesses.

## Monetizing a small/niche newsletter beyond ads and paid subscriptions

- Key insight: small, hyper-niched newsletters (10-20K subscribers) can out-earn newsletters 10x their size by monetizing depth of audience relevance rather than raw scale (contrast: Morning Brew/Axios/The Skim/The Hustle chase pure scale, which is expensive and hard).
- Example — "Design Buddies" (a design community newsletter, ~10-15K subscribers, featured in Beehiiv's "Creator Spotlight"):
  - Runs paid "design cohorts" (structured ~2-week programs) four times a year, generating six figures in revenue from an audience that's already there.
  - Runs a recruiting/placement service: companies wanting vetted design talent pay a placement fee (cited: $20,000-$30,000 per placement) instead of using job boards, because the newsletter already has 15,000 pre-vetted, high-intent designers.
- Example — a YouTuber with ~100,000 subscribers around surf content: instead of ads/brand deals, hosts an in-person "surf school" event twice a year (~50 people, $5,000 ticket) — generates an estimated $250,000-$500,000/year from just two events, leveraging built-up parasocial trust with the audience to sell a high-ticket in-person experience.
- General framework given: assume roughly 1% of an audience will convert on any single monetization offer — so either (a) find and focus on the single highest-value use of the audience (e.g., one $5,000 event), or (b) build many different offers (ads, community, events, merch, services) so that in aggregate a much larger share of the audience converts on *something*.
- Important caveat: successfully running a niche community/newsletter almost always requires being genuinely embedded in that niche yourself (a designer for a design newsletter, etc.) — Denk explicitly says he personally shouldn't try to run a design community despite liking design, because it's not his community. Most people fail by chasing a perceived opportunity in a space where they have no real expertise or standing.

## Starting a newsletter from zero (bootstrapped path)

- Recommended sequence: get your first 50-75 people (friends, coworkers, an existing community you're part of) to subscribe for free → produce genuinely valuable content that's easy to share → explicitly ask each of them to share with one more person → this typically multiplies to 150-200 → then layer on tools like Beehiiv Boost (below) to scale further.
- Explicitly warns against relying on "growth hacking" (referral programs, paid ads, etc.) before content quality is proven — growth tactics amplify good content but can't substitute for it.

## Paid acquisition math and Beehiiv "Boost" (co-registration)

- Rough industry acquisition costs cited: a "loosely interested" niche subscriber (e.g., someone interested in design, US-based) costs roughly $2-4 per email via paid ads (Facebook/Instagram); a well-optimized program can sometimes get subscribers for $0.50-$1.50. Standing up a list of 10-15K targeted subscribers this way: roughly $40,000-$50,000 in ad spend.
- "Boost" mechanic: Beehiiv lets a newsletter operator recommend 2-3 other newsletters to new subscribers at signup; each partner newsletter pays the referring newsletter (example figure: ~$2) for every subscriber who also subscribes to them via that recommendation. If you're paying $2 to acquire a subscriber via ads and one Boost referral pays you back $2, you're break-even on acquisition before any other monetization, and any additional Boost signup is pure profit — creating a reinvestable growth flywheel (spend $1,000 in ads, generate enough offsetting Boost revenue to spend more).
- Denk regrets not adopting Boost/co-registration earlier due to an unfounded fear it would "distract" his subscribers or help competitors — testing showed no measurable drop in his own open/click rates after adopting it; it purely grew the overall subscriber base ("grew the pie").
- Not all acquired subscribers are equal — a subscriber acquired for $0.10 from a low-quality source who opens 1 in 20 emails can be far worse value than one acquired for $2.50 via Facebook who opens 1 in 2 emails; always normalize acquisition cost by engagement rate, not just raw CPA. (Learned over ~6 months running paid acquisition at Morning Brew, where the team spent ~$600,000/month on Google/YouTube/Facebook/Instagram/Snap acquisition.)
- Highest-quality acquisition channel found at Morning Brew: advertising inside OTHER newsletters (e.g., The Points Guy) — people already actively reading and clicking through email are the best prospects for subscribing to a new one. This principle (advertise where your target audience already has the relevant habit) is generalized: e.g., grow a podcast by appearing as a guest on other podcasts, rather than trying to convert non-podcast-listeners.
- Advice to experiment broadly with acquisition channels since novel/early channels are often underpriced: cited examples — advertising in NYC subway Wi-Fi splash screens (didn't work), back-of-Uber-seat tablet ad units (worked reasonably well), a physical billboard with no QR code/trackable link (personal anecdote from Chris Koerner, tracked via workarounds). When Facebook first launched Facebook Stories, Morning Brew was early to advertise there and acquired subscribers for $0.03-$0.04 each for a month or two before competition caught up and CPMs rose. A separate anecdote: an Instagram account manager acquired followers for $0.03-$0.06 each via early Instagram Story ads, and averaged $0.20-$0.30 revenue per follower via affiliate links in stories — described as one of Meta's biggest individual story-ad spenders at the time, until the pricing window closed as more advertisers adopted the format. General lesson: being early to a new ad platform/format before competitors adopt it yields outsized ROI, but the window always closes.

## Local newsletters (identified as the single biggest current trend)

- Denk describes seeing "10 new local newsletters" repeatedly surface on Twitter around Dec 2024-Jan 2025, several already selling out local-sponsor inventory for a full quarter or growing from ~100 friends to 5,000 community members within a quarter. Beehiiv launched a "Local Newsletter Accelerator" program in response.
- Two broad flavors observed: (1) consumer/lifestyle (restaurant guides, local events — e.g., LA-focused newsletters) and (2) local news/politics-focused.
- Why local newsletters monetize unusually well: hyper-local audiences are inherently well-defined for advertisers (anyone subscribed to an Annapolis, MD newsletter almost certainly lives near Annapolis), unlike large general newsletters where the actual demographic/interest profile of subscribers is hard to determine even with email-level data.
- Acquisition cost advantage: targeting a specific city/town via ads (example: Plano, TX) can cost roughly $0.50/subscriber vs. ~$150/subscriber for a general "business" audience on Facebook — while achievable ad CPMs/rates charged to local sponsors can be roughly double a general newsletter's rates because of the precise local targeting. Net effect: acquisition cost can be 1/3 to 1/2 of a general newsletter while sponsor revenue per subscriber is higher.
- Market size framing: no real oversaturation risk given ~100,000 cities/towns in the US and hundreds of thousands of neighborhoods, some with 20,000+ residents — room to go hyper-local below even the city level.
- Suggested local growth tactics: direct mail (example: ~$5,000 to send a targeted postcard about the newsletter to every household in a handful of zip codes); local business affiliate partnerships (e.g., give a stack of sign-up cards to popular local pizza places to hand to customers in exchange for editorial mentions — zero cash cost, pure customer-acquisition trade).
- Real example of a newsletter spinning off a physical business: "Naptown Scoop" (Annapolis, MD local newsletter, run by "Ryan") — has more subscribers than Annapolis has residents (draws people with ties to the area too) — after seeing a viral tweet from Denk about "luxury bathroom rental" as a business idea, bought a ~$20,000 trailer-mounted luxury bathroom unit and built a wedding/event bathroom-rental business, sourcing nearly all bookings directly through the newsletter's audience.

## Newsletter format/design trends noted

- Most business newsletter emails are visually unpolished; investing an hour or two into a genuinely well-designed template is seen as underused "alpha."
- Cadence (daily/weekly/monthly) and length should match the value proposition (bite-sized utility newsletters can be short; more narrative/investigative ones can be long).
- Noted trend: independent journalists leaving traditional media to run investigative/deep-dive newsletters on Beehiiv.
- Noted format innovation: newsletters that explain a topic entirely through illustrated/cartoon images rather than text.

## Converting an existing corporate/e-commerce email list into a standalone media business

- Core pitch: many established businesses sit on very large, underused email lists (customers, past orders) that they only use for basic promotional blasts — this is framed as an under-monetized asset ("a gold mine").
- Illustrative math from Chris Koerner's own e-commerce brand ("Texas Snacks," ~150,000-email list): even conservatively modeling only half the list as active daily-newsletter subscribers, at a "good" niche CPM, a daily tangential-content newsletter (e.g., Texas food/culture trends) built on top of the existing list could plausibly generate roughly $50,000-$60,000/month in standalone ad/sponsorship revenue — as a new revenue line layered on top of (not replacing) the existing promotional emails.
- Bigger real-world example discussed: a person acquiring a bankrupt company (for roughly half a million dollars) purely for its ~1 million active email addresses (planning an e-commerce relaunch) hadn't originally considered converting the list into a monetized daily newsletter; Denk estimates that alone could be a "mid-seven-figure business" if the ad inventory could be sold (acknowledged as the hardest part in practice — filling ad space, not building the list).
- **Explicit service/agency opportunity identified**: build an agency (or work solo, using either a writer network or AI content tools) that approaches businesses with large dormant email databases and offers to build a net-new content/media revenue stream on top of their existing list — monetized via ads/affiliates, generating new day-one revenue while also improving brand goodwill/NPS via more valuable content (versus constant discount-blast emails). Two strategic variants: (a) pure brand-building content with no ads (the "Yeti/Red Bull" model — content that builds affinity/trust without hard-selling, e.g., Yeti's outdoor/fishing YouTube videos that barely mention Yeti products), or (b) a hybrid that plays the long content game while still directly monetizing/attributing new ad revenue — Denk says few companies currently attempt the hybrid, calling it an open opportunity.

## Referral program design — the 80/20 rule

- Contrasting two models: Morning Brew's complex multi-tier referral rewards (mug, t-shirt, hat, backpack, bonus content at 5/10+ referrals) worked mainly because of deep brand fandom already built through tone/branding, not the tier structure itself.
- Milk Road's simpler model is held up as the actual 80/20 lesson: they built one high-value, one-day-to-create asset (a one-pager compiling predictions from 15 crypto experts) and gave it away for just ONE referral, removing nearly all friction — fully automated in Beehiiv with zero ongoing marginal cost (no physical swag/shipping).
- Core principle: most people won't refer 5-10 people even for a big prize, but very cheaply asking for just ONE referral (with a genuinely compelling, low-effort-to-create reward) captures far more of the audience, since the psychological barrier to "get the reward" and the actual sharing behavior are both trivial at n=1.
- Underlying condition for any referral program to work at all: the newsletter has to already be must-read/highly trusted — no referral mechanic manufactures that; quality and genuine fandom must come first.

## Caveats

- Niche community newsletters generally require the operator to have authentic standing/expertise in that community — imported outsiders trying to run a niche newsletter as a pure opportunity play tend to fail.
- Paid-acquisition math (the $40-50K to stand up a niche list) assumes real content/writing skill and engaging emails — it's explicitly not "just a spreadsheet" exercise.
- Converting a dormant corporate email list into an ad-monetized newsletter is conceptually attractive but the hardest practical step is reliably selling/filling the ad inventory, per Denk's own experience.
