# A Secret Hack to Re-Selling iPhones That You Get For Free
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e_eTLl3SbWY | Published: 2024-10-11

"HoldCo Bros" episode (Chris Koerner + Nik) covering a treasure-hunt marketing/business model, a real electronics-recycling fundraiser business Koerner ran personally, a perfume vending machine business update, two "hacky" consumer-advocacy chatbot ideas, and a commercial real estate (self-storage) primer.

## Idea 1: "Treasure hunt" business/marketing model
- Reference case: Project SkyDrop -- a real-world treasure hunt where 10 troy ounces of 24k gold (~$27,000 value) is hidden somewhere within a 500-mile-diameter circle that shrinks daily over 3 weeks down to a 1-foot pinpoint. Entry costs $20; paying members get daily emailed/texted aerial photo clues taken from progressively lower altitudes (earlier joiners get clues earlier in the day). Half of each entry fee reportedly funds a growing cash bounty. Low friction ($20), extremely high perceived payoff ($27k) -- framed as a "YOLO purchase."
- Comparable: Utahtreasurehunts.com, run by a couple of local realtors for several years -- hides a treasure chest with a cryptic clue poem, primarily for email list growth and social exposure rather than direct profit (organizers reportedly claim to not profit from it, which Koerner is skeptical of -- he argues most people who say a project "doesn't make money" are either bad at monetizing or undercounting revenue against inflated "cost of my time" accounting).
- Suggested replication approaches for others:
  1. Use any single high-value item you personally have (something in roughly the $1,000-30,000 range -- e.g., a signed baseball, a classic car, or even a Bitcoin cold-storage private key printed on paper) as the "treasure," set a low entry price (e.g., $20), and run the same shrinking-clue format. Caveats: you need pre-existing audience/distribution to get initial momentum given the high prize-to-entry-cost ratio, and you must hide the item well enough that it can't be trivially solved on day one (real example given: a radio station contest was ruined when a clever teenager solved the clue and located the prize on day two, killing weeks of planned engagement).
  2. Business/guerrilla-marketing variant: instead of buying a single expensive prize outright, partner with several local businesses who each contribute a product/service prize in exchange for exposure (e.g., a restaurant contributes "free pizza every week for a year," a nail salon contributes "free nails for a year") to build a treasure pool valued at $20-30k, funded by pairing the exposure opportunity with a fee charged to participating businesses -- this flips the cost structure so the organizer is profitable from day one rather than betting on enough ticket sales to cover the prize cost (explicitly compared to a negative customer-acquisition-cost model). Suggested scale: could run locally (e.g., DFW) with a handful of local Instagram influencers/business partners, repeated quarterly or monthly, rather than one big national event.
  3. Nonprofit/community fundraiser variant: coordinate a treasure hunt for a school, church, sports team, or nonprofit as a paid organizing service -- solicit local business contributions to fund the prize pool, generate community buzz/engagement, and charge a coordination fee. Referenced comparable business model: a person (mentioned via Michael Girdley's commentary) who makes over $1M/year running pancake-breakfast catering for nonprofit fundraising events at ~50% margins, working only ~80 days/year -- cited as proof that "run recurring local fundraiser logistics as a service" can be a real, scalable business.

## Idea 2: Electronics-recycling school/organization fundraiser (real business Koerner ran, worth copying)
- Concrete alternative to traditional fundraisers (cheesecake sales, cookie sales) that Koerner personally ran while coaching wrestling at Bob Jones High School (Madison, AL).
- Prior fundraiser baseline: wrestlers bought cheesecakes for ~$15 and resold door-to-door for $45-50 -- required real out-of-pocket capital and margin was eaten by "middlemen."
- New model: put a branded collection box in a school/team space, email parents asking them to donate old/unused electronics (old phones, keyboards, game consoles, etc.) instead of buying/selling anything, then personally list and sell the donated items on eBay, giving the organization a cut of proceeds.
- Why it works: contributors give away things they already own and have zero resale motivation for themselves (e.g., an old keyboard worth nothing, or an iPhone worth $150 -- "worth like six cheesecakes") -- there is no upfront capital outlay for the organization, and no one has to spend new money except the eventual eBay buyer.
- Incentive mechanism used: point system where the dollar value of a donated device (rather than just item count) determined the "points," which discouraged people from just dropping in worthless items and encouraged donating higher-value devices (e.g., a Nintendo Switch or iPhone) to compete for a prize.
- Explicitly framed as a template any parent/organizer can copy for essentially any community organization: schools, individual sports teams, churches, Girl Scouts/Boy Scouts, elementary/middle schools, etc. -- described as low/no-downside to test (worst case, you're left holding unsellable electronics, but you never spent your own money buying inventory).

## Perfume vending machine business update (Perfumatic/Perfuma)
- Context: after a viral Instagram/YouTube reel two-plus months prior generated ~2,500 leads, Koerner and Nik partnered with the overseas manufacturer to sell perfume vending machines in the US.
- Revenue: $61,000 in sales in August, $52,000 so far in September (tracking to beat August).
- In progress: negotiating exclusive North American distribution rights with the manufacturer; built and hired an actual sales team to qualify leads and handle follow-up (was actively hiring 2 more sales positions -- setters/closers -- at time of recording).
- Margin correction: initial gross margin was only ~34% because they were not marking up optional accessories (coin/bill acceptors, credit card readers, stands, TVs -- some worth an extra $1,000-2,000 each) at all, only marking up the base machine. After correcting pricing to apply the same margin to accessories as the base unit, gross margin improved to ~43% (short of their ~50% target, but a meaningful improvement).
- General lesson flagged: launching fast without fully dialing in pricing/margins on every SKU (including add-ons) can silently erode profitability -- worth auditing all revenue lines, not just the primary product, early on.

## Idea 3 & 4: "Consumer advocacy" chatbot ideas (speculative, indie-hacker-scale)
- Premise/pattern: many companies profit from friction they build into refund/dispute/cancellation processes, assuming most customers won't bother to claim what they're owed (examples cited: healthcare insurers blanket-denying claims expecting providers won't resubmit; ~10-20% of gift cards going unused industry-wide).
- Idea A -- Amazon late-delivery dispute bot: ~10% of Amazon orders arrive later than their promised delivery window (average American spends ~$1,600/year on Amazon); Amazon is described as unusually willing to refund or let customers keep items when a late delivery is disputed via chat, because of its customer-experience-first policy. Proposed product: a free consumer-facing chatbot/browser tool that connects to a user's Amazon account via API, monitors for late deliveries, and automatically disputes them via chat on the customer's behalf, monetizing by taking a percentage of whatever refund/credit it recovers. Cited historical B2B analog: a company called "71 Pounds" that used to automate disputing late FedEx shipments on behalf of shippers (who have a contractual refund guarantee but rarely bother filing the paperwork on ~$15 packages), also monetizing via a cut (~40%) of savings recovered. Also referenced: an existing consumer bill-negotiation company (unnamed, compared to Comcast/AT&T bill negotiators) that already does something similar for subscription pricing.
- Idea B -- AI voice-based subscription cancellation/negotiation agent: personal anecdote -- canceling an Amex Platinum card (~$750/year fee) took an hour of live retention-department negotiation script back-and-forth. Proposed: an AI voice agent that calls and handles cancellation/retention conversations on the customer's behalf (extending the existing chat-based negotiation-bot concept to phone calls, since the underlying voice AI tech now supports this), again monetizing via a percentage of money saved/recovered.
- General framework offered: look for any process where a company profits from customer friction around "claiming full value they're owed," and build automation (chat or voice AI) that removes that friction on the consumer's behalf for a cut of the recovered value.
- Caveat: explicitly described as a fun indie-hacker-scale project idea, not necessarily a large-revenue business ("I don't know how they're going to make a lot of money").

## Commercial real estate primer: self-storage (from a referenced guest, Lauren Rothberger)
- Average self-storage net margin cited: 30-42% (varies by source in the discussion).
- Pros of self-storage/commercial real estate generally: real-estate-backed (limited supply supports asset value, unlike a pure service business that can go to zero), predictable recurring cash flows, high barriers to entry (capital requirement), low labor intensity relative to other real estate.
- Cons: limited upside per individual facility (rent increases only go so far, no 3x-ing NOI overnight), capital-intensive to expand into more facilities, slow/steady returns, and a genuinely hard first deal for newcomers (typical entry: putting down ~$100k against a $1.5M+ loan on an asset class you don't yet understand).
- Market pricing context: brokered self-storage deals typically trade at 6-7% cap rates (cap rate = asset's income return rate); genuinely cheap/high-upside deals are rare on-market. Better cap rates are reportedly available off-market (a "friend of the pod," Connor, buys self-storage exclusively off-market) -- described as more available than off-market mobile home/RV park deals, but rarer than people assume.
- Recommended path to a first deal:
  1. Focus on your own local market (or at minimum a market you know better than average) for your first deal -- local knowledge (zoning changes, incoming/outgoing major employers) materially reduces risk that isn't visible on a spreadsheet.
  2. Do your first deal on-market (via a broker) rather than off-market, despite off-market typically offering better terms/pricing -- an on-market listing gives you a third party (the broker) implicitly vouching that the deal isn't an outlier/trap, which matters more for a first-time buyer who doesn't yet know what red flags to look for.
  3. Build market fluency before you need it: talk to brokers to learn what a good cap rate and normal due diligence process look like, and network with people in commercial real estate you encounter organically (church, kids' school, etc.) so you have deal flow and expertise access when you're ready.
  4. Once you land a first cash-flowing property, use its built-up equity to roll into acquiring additional properties -- described as slow but one of the most statistically proven paths to building millionaire-level net worth over time (contrasted explicitly against the "side hustle" mentality of chasing a fast $50k win, which doesn't compound the same way real estate equity does).
- Sourcing off-market deals (once you have some experience): "driving for dollars" (physically finding properties and talking to owners face to face) is the least efficient but most effective method; texting property owners directly ("I love your property, any interest in selling?") to get a call and then an in-person meeting quickly is the more efficient (though less personal) approach.
- Explicit caveat: this asset class specifically rewards patience and preparation over the "bias for action, launch today" instinct the hosts otherwise promote for online/side-hustle businesses -- jumping into a first commercial real estate deal without first building market knowledge is described as "a recipe for disaster."
