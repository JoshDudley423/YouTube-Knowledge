# $18k/Month in his 2nd Month doing WHAT!?
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hu8m5FUz3g0 | Published: 2024-12-03

Interview with Cinco Winston, founder of True Line Striping TX, a pavement/parking-lot line striping business in Waco, TX, a few months into operation.

## The business: parking lot line striping / pavement marking
- Services: new parking lot layouts, restriping faded lines, ADA-compliant stencils, fire lane markings, wheel stop installation, directional arrows, and sometimes sign-post/ADA sign installation.
- Customers: businesses needing to maintain curb appeal and stay compliant with fire code and ADA accessibility requirements (gas stations, dental/medical offices, restaurants, apartment complexes, auto shops, churches, new construction).
- Market observation: Koerner and Cinco both note most entrepreneurs (est. 80-90%) don't even know this industry exists; in Waco there was really only one large established competitor (40 years in business, also does crack fill/seal coating/street sweeping) plus a couple smaller line-striping players — judged not saturated. Cinco believes he could expand an hour away to Temple/Belton and "clean that whole market" too.
- Founder background: came from construction (worked up from assistant superintendent to project manager under a general contractor), wanted something "construction adjacent." Considered buying an existing striping franchise but decided it was cheaper and more flexible to launch independently instead.

## Startup costs (concrete numbers)
- 16x7 ft enclosed trailer (new): $7,000.
- Walk-behind line striping machine (new) — holds a 5-gallon paint bucket, has a Honda motor/pump, sprays paint stripes: $10,000.
- Used his personal truck (already owned, no truck payment) to tow the trailer.
- Total equipment investment: ~$17,000, paid in cash (no financing), though both the trailer and machine could be financed if desired.
- Additional stencil investment: roughly $2,000 accumulated over time (bought one at a time, as needed per job type — ADA, no parking, fire lane, numbers, visitor/reserved, directional arrows, drive-thru service stencils); stencils are a one-time cost per type and reused indefinitely (heavy-duty plastic, laid on the ground, not adhered).
- Ongoing consumable cost: paint. A 5-gallon bucket of solvent-based paint costs ~$150.
- Working out of his home; no shop overhead.

## Transition from W-2 to full-time
- Gave himself a 6-month runway; committed to not taking any money out of the business for the first 6 months while building it up to replace his W-2 income.
- Had zero customers and had not knocked a single door when he quit his job — gave his employer about a month's notice, with some informal early notice before that.
- Reached profitability within the first 2 months (faster than expected).

## Revenue trajectory (concrete numbers)
- Month 1 revenue: $7,000.
- Month 2 revenue: $18,000.
- Projected Month 3 (winter slowdown, seasonal industry): ~$14,000 for November and ~$14,000 for December.
- Total jobs completed at time of interview: roughly 18-20.

## Unit economics by job size
- Small job (e.g., a small gas station, 5-10 spots, one ADA stencil): job minimum typically $300-$600 (varies by operator); example: $400 charged, ~$100 in materials (2-3 gallons of paint at ~$50-75/gallon-equivalent, plus amortized ~$1 stencil cost) → about 75% gross margin; takes about 2 hours of labor (excluding drive time) → roughly $150 profit for 2 hours = ~$180/hr profit rate on the smallest jobs. Extrapolated (Koerner's math): at 8 hrs/day, 5 days/week doing only small jobs, that's roughly $360,000/year in profit potential with no employees.
- Medium job (e.g., a 30-35 space dental office restripe with some fire lane and 2 ADA spots): ~$2,000.
- Pricing method: quoted by linear foot of striping, not by square footage or spot count directly — a standard parking space line is about 18 feet long, so spot count converts to linear feet; restripe pricing ranges roughly $0.30-$0.80/linear foot (market dependent).
- New layout (vs. simple restripe) costs roughly 1.5-2x more than a restripe of the same size, mainly due to the added time/skill needed for layout work (measuring, chalk-lining, aligning) and typically requires a second person (can't be done solo) — not because painting itself takes twice as long.
- Large job examples: a new-layout tattoo parlor job at $5,000; a car wash new-build job accepted at $10,000 (largest proposal accepted so far); large jobs generally range $5,000-$20,000 and often include additional angled/pedestrian-zone markings and sign-post/ADA sign installs (sign installation is commonly bundled into striping services).
- A separate concrete example: a $6,000 restripe job requiring ~$3,000 of material (lower-margin, ~35% gross margin) taking about 2 full days.
- Apartment complex recurring restripes (300-400 stalls) run $8,000-$14,000 and are typically done every 3-4 years.
- High-traffic fast food restaurants (cited: Chick-fil-A, Raising Cane's) restripe twice a year — much more frequent than expected.
- Recurrence drivers: heavier vehicle traffic fades lines faster than weather/sun exposure alone; in snow regions, snow plows scrape paint off, increasing restripe frequency (raised as a potential complementary seasonal business: snow plowing in winter, striping in summer).

## Operational/technical details
- The striping machine is a 3-wheel walk-behind unit (2 rear wheels, 1 front caster wheel that locks) with a mounted spray gun; requires constant, even pressure to keep it tracking straight — any unevenness (including on sloped pavement) pulls it off-line; the operator uses a laser dot aimed at the paint-line edge and must stay fully focused the entire time.
- The same spray gun can be detached and used freehand to apply stencils (which just lay on the pavement, not adhered).
- The machine holds only one paint color at a time; switching colors requires flushing the system (traditionally with acetone). Cinco currently uses gasoline instead of acetone to flush the machine between colors (cheaper), running the flush fluid back through into a waste bucket for reuse/disposal, though it's not a perfectly clean flush. A color change costs about an hour of unbilled time; some operators in the industry charge ~$200 for a paint-change job requiring multiple colors; Cinco's stated plan is to eventually buy a second dedicated machine to avoid this bottleneck.
- Sequencing on a job: apply the wide-coverage stencils (e.g., blue ADA squares) first since they need longer to dry, then stripe the lines — by the time lines are done, the stencil paint has usually dried.
- Solvent-based paint (his choice) dries in roughly 2 hours; cones are used to block off a work area if a job finishes late at night before opening hours.
- Work must generally happen at night or after 5pm since parking lots are in active use during business hours, except for locations closed certain days (churches, some offices/restaurants).

## Customer acquisition
- First 2 customers: prior professional connections from his construction project-management work (small renovation clients he told about the new venture).
- Third customer (first true "cold" customer): an auto shop where he was already a customer (for his truck), pitched them directly with a discount.
- Discounting strategy for the first 3-4 weeks: heavily discounted early jobs (some effectively at cost, e.g., $200-300 for small jobs; others at 20-40% off) specifically to build a Google review page/reputation; stopped discounting once demand and confidence increased.
- Ongoing acquisition (~90% of customers): door-to-door/in-person cold outreach — walking into businesses (e.g., fast food restaurants) and asking to speak with the manager, handing out business cards, and building relationships even without an immediate sale.
- Remaining ~10% comes from Google/organic search ("parking lot striping" searches surfacing his Google Business listing).
- Estimated close rate on cold door-knocking (not necessarily same-visit): roughly 5-10% over time — described by both as fairly high for a cold sales motion.
- Stated reason the close rate is workable despite being "not extroverted" or naturally sales-oriented: unlike many home-service pitches, striping needs are visually obvious and objectively verifiable (visibly faded lines), the buyer is virtually certain not to own a $10,000 striping machine themselves, and businesses often have a maintenance budget line item to pull from, making it a less emotional purchase decision than, e.g., residential home services.
- Analogous case cited: a separate pond-cleaning business that only door-knocks houses with visibly dirty ponds — high close rate because visible need + demonstrated ability-to-pay (pond ownership) + near-certainty the owner won't DIY it are all pre-qualified by what's visible from the street. General framework Koerner is building: businesses where you can visually confirm "this prospect needs this and probably can't/won't do it themselves" convert dramatically better on cold outreach than typical cold sales.
- Regulatory/compliance angle as a soft sales lever: bringing up ADA/fire-code compliance risk helps but often doesn't move prospects to act until they receive an actual notice-of-violation from an inspector (compared to an HOA warning letter) — mentioned, but not a strong standalone closer.
- Referral partner idea raised (untested by Cinco but discussed): partner with asphalt/paving companies (who often don't want to do the off-hours striping work themselves), pressure-washing companies, or parking-lot trash/cleanup crews who are already inside these same properties — pay them a flat referral fee (e.g., $400/job) for a lead.
- Training: Cinco paid for a short (few days) hands-on training/mentorship with an established striper based in Oklahoma (chosen specifically because it was far enough away not to be a local competitor); that same company's course package also included marketing/sales technique videos, which was his only formal sales training/resource.

## Scaling plans and philosophy
- Preferred near-term scaling path: stay focused on striping specifically, build reputation and market share as "the line striping guy in Waco" before adding adjacent services, believing there's ample uncaptured local demand (new construction, existing owners not currently served) to eventually run multiple crews.
- Longer-term/adjacent expansion options mentioned: adding crack sealing/seal coating (2-3x higher barrier to entry — pricier equipment, more technical know-how) or commercial pressure washing (a path some competitors take); also noted an established local competitor treats striping as "a drop in the bucket" and mainly chases higher-value seal-coat jobs — implying striping-only focus is underserved.
- Advice for someone replicating this in another city: pay to train/shadow with an experienced operator in a non-competing market for a month or two (not just a few days) rather than learning everything on the fly, since mistakes on a customer's property must be fixed regardless of cost/time — cited as his own biggest time sink.
- General business-model framework proposed: any business that gets the majority of its revenue from cold calling/door-knocking will always have durable demand, precisely because doing that kind of outreach goes against most people's/competitors' natural inclination — so being willing to door-knock is itself a competitive moat, almost regardless of specific market research, since virtually no competitors are doing it. Note also the temptation to stop door-knocking once inbound (Google) leads start coming in — Cinco is advised to keep doing it anyway since it remains the more reliable (if less "efficient") channel.
- Caveats: roadway/street markings require different, more expensive insurance due to higher liability and were explicitly avoided by Cinco; this is a physically demanding, hands-on, largely nighttime-hours business requiring full personal involvement in sales, quoting, scheduling, execution, invoicing, and collections — not passive.
