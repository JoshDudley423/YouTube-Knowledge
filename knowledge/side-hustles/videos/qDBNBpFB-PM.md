# How to skip the line and build a $1M business in a week.
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qDBNBpFB-PM | Published: 2024-06-06

"HoldCo Bros" episode: Chris Koerner and Nik riff on monetizing existing social media audiences/influencers/artists rather than building content from scratch.

## Core framework: "if a creator is just selling t-shirts, they don't know what they're doing"
- Observation: many large social accounts (case study: "Nature Is Metal," an Instagram account with 6.1M+ followers showing wildlife-attack videos) monetize only via basic print-on-demand or in-house-inventory t-shirts/hats — described as almost always *not* the highest-and-best use of that audience.
- Reasoning: t-shirts/merch is the default answer for people without a "business brain" because it's the first idea that comes to mind; it also has a bad structural trade-off — print-your-own gives good margin but inventory/fulfillment headaches (hard to scale operationally, per Koerner's own experience with Send Eats and Texas Snacks); print-on-demand avoids inventory but destroys margin.
- Key idea: link-in-video merch mentions have terrible conversion (viewer has to manually type in a URL, no clickable link inside the video format itself).

## Business idea 1: Build a subscription/media-content funnel instead of merch, matched to audience *interest* (not just demographics)
- Principle: you can't sell audiences generic products just because they fit the demographic (e.g., don't sell pickleball paddles to Nature Is Metal's 30-40-year-old male viewers just because that's a good demo) — you must match product to *why* they're watching, not who they are broadly.
- Concrete pitch for Nature Is Metal specifically: build a "Netflix for animal-attack videos" — a $2-5/month subscription funnel where the free Instagram clip teases into "click here to see 13 more videos like this."
- Related monetization paths for a nature-content audience: affiliate/booking partnerships with African safari companies, Alaskan hunting/fishing tour operators, or big-box outdoor retailers (Bass Pro Shops, Dick's/Sports Chalet) — since the same viewers are pre-qualified as outdoor/hunting enthusiasts.
- Bigger framing: this audience could become a full media brand (compared to "the National Geographic of the next generation") rather than just a merch storefront.

## Business idea 2: Agency that helps under-monetized creators build real businesses off their audience
- Analogy: same market gap that digital marketing/SEO/PPC agencies filled ~10-15 years ago for "boomer" small businesses that had a website but no real marketing — that same gap now exists around creators/influencers who have an audience but no real monetization strategy beyond merch.
- Stated belief this is still early, not "played out," despite examples like KSI/Logan Paul already doing creator-led business launches.
- Target creator segment to avoid: comedians/comedy accounts — explicit claim that "people don't buy from funny people" when comedy *is* the brand (cited examples: Robert Sterling-style parody accounts) because it creates too much friction/ambiguity about whether the person is being sincere. Comedy as a secondary trait (alongside a serious value-prop brand) is fine; comedy as the entire brand undermines a sales pitch.
- General principle repeated: distribution matters more than product ("first-time founders focus on product, second-time founders focus on distribution"). Content/audience-building is framed as a distribution "cheat code" that then lets you be business-model-agnostic (choose whichever business you want once you have an audience to sell to).

## Business idea 3: "Set.Live"-style artist fan-capture mechanism, but with a lower-friction/better executed version, or full artist podcast production
- Origin: at a concert (Taking Back Sunday), between sets, the singer promoted a website ("set.live") pitching fans to submit their email for a chance at a personalized video/free shirt/tour invite — described as a real, if clunky, new "marketing channel."
- Critique/opportunity: too much friction — required opening a phone browser and typing a URL from memory during a live show; framework floated: SMS push-notification opt-in or an on-screen QR code would likely convert meaningfully more (estimated +20% of the audience) of a captive, high-intent concert crowd.
- Bigger pitch: help musicians (or any artist/audience-holder) launch and produce a podcast, since (a) they already have an engaged, trusting fanbase, (b) they already have audio/production equipment and crew, (c) podcast subscriber growth is easier than people think — cited figure: getting ~100 new Apple Podcasts subscribers in a single day is enough to hit the top 50 in a competitive category, and a band playing ~80 tour dates could ask fans from the stage to subscribe every night, compounding into a top-tier podcast. Business model: a producer/operator partners with the artist to produce/distribute the podcast, help them monetize via ads/merch cross-sell, in exchange for a cut (30% floated).
- Adjacent idea: apply the same "insider/host chemistry sells better than a lone expert" logic (referencing why "All-In" podcast works) to niche B2B industries — find the top 4-5 recognizable people in a boring/niche industry (example given: the sanitation/"SNIF" industry) and produce an "All-In for [niche industry]" podcast. Cited data point: a podcast getting only 500-1,000 downloads/episode in a niche vertical was reportedly earning $3,600 per episode from ad slots, because niche + high buyer intent commands premium CPMs — explicitly compared to Agora-style niche financial/health newsletters (~$500M+ company running 40-60 highly niche newsletters, subscriptions priced $2,000-3,000/year, at CPMs 10-20x a general-interest newsletter). Framed as "the niche is the rich."

## Caveats
- No hard revenue numbers are given for any of the three pitched ideas (Nature Is Metal subscription, artist fan-capture, niche podcast-agency) — these are speculative brainstorms, not validated case studies, unlike the $3,600/episode niche podcast data point and the "100 subscribers/day" podcast-ranking fact, which are stated as real.
- Note on growth hacks generally: "the effectiveness of a growth hack is directly correlated to its inverse lifespan — the more effective it is, the less time you have to milk it before it stops working" (used to explain why BuzzFeed-style "number seven will shock you" headlines no longer work).
