# Big Profits from Buying Craigslist Book Royalties
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_3qYySVAKdI | Published: 2024-04-29

Format: caller/interview episode with four separate guests and business ideas.

## 1. Nick — buying book and music royalties
- Business idea: buy fractional royalty interests in books and music as a passive, uncorrelated investment asset class. Thesis: returns are uncorrelated with the stock market (people cancel gym memberships before canceling a $10/month Spotify subscription), income is IP-protected (royalties run for life of the author/creator plus 70 years), and there are amortization tax benefits.
- Track record: ~10 deals invested, holds ~6-7 at a time, both personally and via a small friends-and-family fund; typical deal size $20,000-200,000. Blended portfolio yield: roughly 12%/year, "low double digits," fairly stable (likened to corporate-bond-level risk/volatility).
- Best deal (the anecdote in the title): found a literary agent's 7.5% royalty interest in the first six books of a bestselling crime-novel series (author: C.J. Box) via a **Craigslist post** ("buying oil and gas royalties, music royalties") in ~2016. Seller was a former literary agent for the author, moving to Alaska to fly planes/fish and needed liquidity (physically couldn't access mail to deposit paper royalty checks where he was moving). Bought for **$35,000** when it was paying **~$12,000/year** (a ~3x income multiple); today it pays **~$16,000/year**, boosted by the book series later getting picked up for TV adaptations (Big Sky, Joe Pickett) which drove sustained demand for the backlist.
- Sourcing: **Royalty Exchange** acts as a marketplace/"eBay" for these assets but pricing there is typically more expensive; the guest prefers off-market deals via brokers. Typical purchase multiples today: 5-7x annual income for attractive off-market deals, 8-10x for high-quality/in-demand assets — the original 3x Craigslist deal was described as unusually cheap.
- Risk/quality screening: buying "old and stabilized" catalog with proven recurring streaming revenue (steady Spotify listens) reduces risk of a total loser; the guest's fund has had flat-to-slightly-growing positions but no outright losers to date, attributed to their due diligence process.
- Contrast with active/influence-based royalty investing: cites Hipgnosis (a large royalty investment fund) that paid much higher multiples (15-16x) with an active promotion/placement team to try to boost song value — implied this strategy underperformed expectations, i.e., paying a premium for "we'll actively grow the asset" doesn't reliably pay off at scale.
- Market inefficiency / business opportunity noted: transferring royalty ownership is a slow, manual process (labels like Warner can take months to formally process an ownership change), which is the main barrier to a more efficient/liquid marketplace for these assets; the guest's own preferred approach if scaling this himself would be to raise a fund and allocate capital directly rather than try to build an exchange.
- AI/Suno angle discussed: guest is skeptical AI-generated music (e.g., Suno) will meaningfully disrupt royalty value in the near term — compares current AI music quality to early "fart app" era of the iPhone (novel/fun but not displacing professionally produced music); sees more near-term opportunity in using AI-generated songs/audio as marketing novelties (e.g., a custom Suno jingle used to grab attention in a cold email subject line/attachment) rather than as investable royalty assets themselves.

## 2. Brandon — growing a street-sweeping + parking-lot pressure-washing business (Southern California)
- Background: returning caller (was the very first caller on the podcast's first episode); bought an existing street-sweeping business ~1 year prior (in Ventura, ~1.5 hrs outside LA), serving residential, HOA, and construction clients.
- Growth trajectory since first appearing: revenue grew from ~$15-20k/month (at acquisition) to ~$28-30k/month (3 months prior) to **~$60,000/month** at time of this call, following advice to double down on Google reviews, SEO, and Google Ads (channels already working) plus new cold outreach (cold calling/emailing using lists from **Outscraper**, joining a local contractors' association).
- Financials: currently **8-12% net margins**, citing high capex (aging equipment inherited at purchase requiring reinvestment) as the main margin drag; a single sweeper truck replacement part can cost ~$15,000.
- New high-value adjacent opportunity discovered via cold outreach: **commercial parking-garage/parking-lot pressure washing**. A cold call led to a lead for pressure-washing a 7-story parking garage near LAX — quoted the client **$55,000** for the full job (validated against the client's own prior vendor pricing of ~$30,000 for half the garage 2 years earlier, plus confirmation from other pressure-washing contractors that $50-60k was the right range for the square footage), while it would cost him only **~$30,000-35,000 to subcontract out** — a potential ~$20-25k profit on one job, versus needing an entire month of typical street-sweeping revenue to generate similar profit. He was quoting via subcontracting the actual labor rather than doing it himself, to de-risk before investing in his own equipment.
- Business model insight (Chris's framing): this is a much higher-ticket, lower-frequency version of the same underlying service — sweeping/cleaning pavement — with roughly the same asymmetric-risk logic as diversifying into new but adjacent verticals: sweeping a garage himself might be worth ~$500-600, whereas the full pressure-wash/deep-clean job on the same property is worth ~100x that. Recommended subbing out the specialized work (pressure washing) entirely until enough deal volume justifies buying dedicated equipment (~$35,000-40,000 per truck+trailer setup, ~$75,000 for two crews) — pays for itself in roughly 2-3 jobs.
- Competitive dynamics: pressure washing overall is saturated with low-end/residential competitors (many inspired by viral TikTok content, buying cheap consumer pressure washers from Home Depot), but **commercial/high-rise parking garage pressure washing specifically has very little competition** because of the high capital barrier to entry (specialized equipment, ability to handle very large jobs) — Brandon reportedly called ~10 pressure-washing contractors to find someone able to handle the LAX garage job; most said it was too big for them, and the eventual subcontractor said there are only "three or four" companies in all of California capable of handling a job that size (that subcontractor personally does ~$7-8 million/year in revenue, 80% commercial).
- General principle stated: when an industry attracts 3x the normal rate of new entrants (e.g., due to a viral TikTok trend) but the addressable high-ticket sub-segment (jobs requiring a $50k+ specialized truck) doesn't proportionally grow because of its higher capital barrier, that sub-segment becomes relatively less crowded than an "unfashionable" industry (e.g., fencing) with normal entry/exit rates — i.e., look for a high-barrier niche inside an oversaturated, hyped industry.
- Recommended lead-gen approach for this niche: use **Reonomy** (a commercial real-estate data-scraping site) via a 1-week free trial (worked through a virtual assistant to extract maximum data during the trial window) to build a targeted list of parking garages/lots by county and size (e.g., over/under one acre), then have a local person (not an overseas VA) call the list directly to gauge demand.
- Recommended positioning/marketing: register a separate, purpose-specific domain (e.g., "[City] Parking Garage Pressure Washing.com") as a dedicated one-page landing site leveraging the parent company's existing credentials (30 years in business) explicitly as a subsidiary/specialist brand, rather than folding this into the general sweeping-company site — reasoning: a hyper-specific specialist framing ("we ONLY do parking garage pressure washing in LA County") converts far better on cold calls than a generic pressure-washing pitch, and can be tested cheaply with targeted Google Ads (low traffic volume but very low competition/CPC expected in this hyper-niche).
- Additional tactical suggestion: proactively mine the existing street-sweeping customer list for pressure-washing cross-sell opportunities — "you could have a whole other $60k/month of revenue already sitting in your email list."
- Strategic advice given on focus/"shiny object syndrome": stay in this business rather than pivot elsewhere; the underlying business (30-year track record) is statistically likely to remain viable for decades, is AI/automation-resistant (any future self-driving sweeper truck would only lower his own costs, not disrupt demand), and is in a growing, non-bubble-prone coastal California market — advised to keep compounding within this business (including eventually acquiring competitor businesses once organic growth plateaus) rather than chase unrelated new ventures.

## 3. Thomas — growing a B2B "business intelligence dashboard" agency
- Business idea: builds custom data dashboards that pull and unify metrics from multiple third-party platforms/APIs into one clean view — initial case study/flagship client is an ~$80 million AUM "experiential hospitality" real estate fund (short-term rental portfolio), where the dashboard was shown to be more accurate than the platforms' own native reporting (caught and corrected errors in the source platforms' own metrics).
- Unit economics: ~10 hours of labor per dashboard build for an average customer; pays a contracted "right-hand man" a flat **$1,000 per dashboard** regardless of actual hours worked (win-win flat-fee structure — analyst is incentivized to get faster/more efficient over time, is being groomed to also take on operational duties and potentially relocate to lower cost-of-living, targeting ~$120k/year income at volume). Roughly ~$250 additional tech/infrastructure cost per dashboard.
- Pricing model discussed: considering a **$3,000 upfront implementation fee** plus recurring revenue for maintenance/report automation/minor changes (~$399-499/month proposed, still being tested); incremental add-ons like connecting a new property/data source to an existing dashboard priced around **$500** per addition. Real estate fund clients did not balk at the $3,000 price (their costs get passed to their LPs anyway); a global staffing agency prospect did balk at that price point, suggesting real estate/fund clients are a stronger-fit vertical due to price insensitivity.
- Chris's specific recommendation: **test price discrimination by industry** using separate landing pages that funnel to the same booking calendar (e.g., charge $5,000 for a real-estate-fund-sourced lead vs. $2,000 for a different vertical) since willingness to pay clearly varies by industry.
- Suggested customer-acquisition approach: use **Apollo** to build a prospect list filtered by industry ("real estate") and job title ("general partner") to target smaller real estate funds/PE firms (rather than large well-known names) — but explicitly *not* to cold-DM the actual sales pitch on LinkedIn (oversaturated channel prospects likely ignore); instead find them through less-crowded channels like **Strava** (used as a low-competition way to identify and informally message the same target person, cross-referencing a name found via Apollo/cold email) to stand out from typical LinkedIn/email pitch volume that decision-makers routinely ignore.
- "Mini whale" framing: rather than chasing one huge flagship client, cast a wide net across ~100 similarly-sized prospects; statistically expect roughly 5-10 of them to turn out to be much larger ("white whale") accounts than initially apparent (e.g., a $50M fund that's actually a subsidiary of a $5B parent company operating in 34 markets) once you're in conversation with them.
- Recommended pricing test: anchor at a lower headline price with an add-on "audit" fee framed as a systems/data-quality assessment (e.g., $2,200 base + $1,000 audit, or $2,500 + $500 audit, instead of a flat $3,000) — expectation is most prospects will opt into the audit anyway (since an audit is functionally part of the required onboarding work regardless), while the lower headline price should improve close rates.
- Scaling around platform expertise: as the business becomes efficient on a specific underlying software platform (e.g., Cloudbeds for short-term rental management, ServiceTitan for home services), it becomes more efficient to specifically target other businesses using that same platform rather than targeting by industry — suggested tactic to find "everyone using platform X" was to post a scoping job on Upwork asking if a contractor can identify/scrape such a list, calling it "an asymmetric bet" (low cost/effort, uncertain but potentially high-value result).
- Margin protection strategy for the contracted analyst as volume scales: proactively agree on a sliding pay scale in advance (e.g., pay drops from $1,000 to $700/dashboard once turnaround time drops from 10 hours to 5 hours due to efficiency gains) so the business owner isn't stuck later having an awkward pay-cut conversation with someone who's become both very efficient and increasingly hard to replace due to accumulated platform-specific institutional knowledge.
- Free/low-cost lead-gen idea proposed live: post an offer to build a free dashboard for "a white whale" prospect as bait/proof-of-concept (also suggested doing this for the host's own real estate fund as a live example).

## 4. Clayton — turning a physical product idea (aerospace engineer/consultant) into an e-commerce business
- Background: aerospace engineer transitioning into product "tinkering," currently self-employed as a consultant to aerospace startups via his own LLC (deliberately not quitting his stable consulting income to pursue this).
- Specific product concept discussed live: a "space heater as alarm clock" — a programmable smart outlet (bought off Amazon) hooked to a space heater on a timer, so the room warms up right before waking (validated informally by the guest and his wife using an off-the-shelf DIY version).
- Key strategic reframe from the host: rather than trying to invent/patent new underlying technology (the guest's initial instinct — invent, patent, license IP), simply combine two generic, already-mass-manufactured commodity components (a smart plug + a space heater, both widely made by many Chinese manufacturers) into one branded, purpose-built bundle/box with instructions — competing on framing/positioning ("a better space heater that's also an alarm clock") rather than on novel IP; explicitly the same category of low-differentiation "e-commerce arbitrage" product (cites viral novelty items like a specialized pie slicer that reportedly sells "tens of millions of dollars" worth despite low genuine utility).
- Concrete low-cost validation/launch playbook suggested:
  1. Source components for free: contact multiple manufacturers on **Alibaba** and request free samples of both the smart-plug/timer and the space heater; most suppliers will send samples at no cost.
  2. Get custom packaging from a US-based custom box manufacturer, ordering minimum order quantity (~100-200 units) at roughly $1-2/box.
  3. Order matching quantities of both components, print simple instructions, and hand-assemble kits at home (avoid over-committing capital — explicitly advised against ordering a full shipping container's worth of inventory, which the guest initially considered).
  4. List on **Amazon FBA**: ship the minimum required quantity to Amazon's fulfillment/distribution centers (labeling requirements are described as a one-time "headache" that gets easier once your seller account/process is set up for future products too).
  5. In parallel (zero cost, immediate), list on **Facebook Marketplace and Craigslist** to get early qualitative signal on demand (buyers searching for "space heater" will organically discover the new listing) before the Amazon inventory even ships — explicitly framed as market validation, not a scalable sales channel (mostly local reach unless boosted with paid promotion).
  6. Seasonal timing note: since the product is winter-relevant, the ~6-month lead time before peak buying season (space heaters) is actually useful runway to properly test and refine before demand peaks.
  7. Micro-influencer marketing: find a creator (suggested targeting someone with 50k-200k followers) and offer a per-sale commission (e.g., $10/sale) for a short (20-30 second) demo video showing the product's fun use-case (compared to the Michael Scott "wake up to bacon smell" George Foreman grill bit from The Office) — expect most influencers to ignore the pitch, but this is low-cost/low-effort to attempt broadly ("better to ask forgiveness than permission" regarding fair-use content parody).
  8. Also test a dedicated single-product **Shopify** store (recommended hiring on Upwork for ~$400-500, using a purpose-built one-product Shopify theme rather than a generic multi-product store) run with Facebook ads, specifically to A/B test price points (host suggests deliberately testing widely different prices like $30, $80, $150 on identical ad creative/copy) since Shopify (unlike Amazon) supports running Facebook ad traffic effectively; for lower-priced items (~$30-50), Facebook ad customer-acquisition cost is likely to make profitable scaling difficult regardless of margin, making price-point testing important before committing ad spend at scale.
- Recommended research tool: **Jungle Scout** — provides estimated sales-volume data for existing Amazon listings/competitors, useful for gauging real demand for a proposed product category before investing.
- Recommended sourcing tool for domestic custom manufacturing/casings: **ThomasNet** — a directory for finding US-based manufacturers (suggested as an alternative to expensive injection molding, which requires high production runs to be cost effective) for smaller-run custom enclosures/casings.
- General approach validated by host: pursuing "boring," non-patentable, low-tech commodity-repackaging product ideas can still support meaningful revenue at real-world e-commerce scale (hundreds of thousands of units) before competitors/imitators catch up, even without any protectable IP — speed to market and packaging/positioning are the actual moat, not invention.
