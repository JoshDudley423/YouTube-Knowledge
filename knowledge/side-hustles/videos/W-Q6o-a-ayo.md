# A Secret Hack to Finding and Selling Home Service Leads
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W-Q6o-a-ayo | Published: 2024-09-25

Part 2 of a two-part brainstorming episode with Connor Gross (owns self-storage, recruiting, and e-commerce businesses; bizbrainstorms.com newsletter). Part 1: https://youtu.be/ZhVb1q4X64g. Covers several distinct business ideas, ranked at the end.

## Idea 1: "Workout honey" — a natural pre-workout/energy product
- Concept: raw honey + coarse sea salt flakes, consumed before a workout (Connor's personal habit) as a caffeine-free energy source — honey provides quick carbs/sugar, salt aids blood flow/muscle "pumps" (cited as linked to better gains in some research).
- Positioning strategy discussed at length: this is not a proprietary/technical product — it's about **packaging and framing**, not invention. Compare to an earlier "dog electrolyte" idea the hosts discussed: buying commodity "Lite Salt" (50% potassium/50% sodium, sold at Walmart) and rebranding/relabeling it for a pet audience sold via Chewy — the value is in repackaging and positioning a known commodity for a underserved niche, not novel formulation.
- Framed like an "RX Bar for pre-workouts" — a short, clean, natural ingredient list (just honey and salt) as the entire marketing hook, contrasted against processed gels/goo packets that some athletes find hard to digest.
- Distribution/marketing idea: ship product for free to race organizers to include in runner/cyclist race packets with a QR code, driving direct sales.
- Precedent cited for market size potential: LMNT (electrolyte brand) reportedly does ~$200M topline / ~$40M bottom line — shows there's real revenue ceiling in this space, though the likely exit path is limited to being acquired by a large CPG (P&G) or food conglomerate.
- Caveat raised: selling food products can be operationally harder than selling supplements online; recommends leaning toward positioning this as a supplement rather than a food product, and that it can work as a low-footprint "grab item" near a cash register (small packet format, gas station or checkout-counter friendly).

## Idea 2: Chia seed / lime juice / honey endurance drink (from "Born to Run")
- Based on the real traditional recovery/endurance drink used by the Tarahumara people of Mexico (referenced in the book "Born to Run"), consisting of chia seeds, water, lime juice, and honey.
- Product idea: create a powdered version (chia + powdered honey + lime flavoring), sold in single-serve packets (like an oatmeal packet) that the consumer adds water to themselves — avoids the cost/weight of shipping the water component.
- Marketing angle: authenticity/heritage story ("the same drink used for thousands of years to run hundreds of miles") as the core hook.
- Framed alongside "workout honey" as a natural, easy-to-digest, non-caffeinated energy category with growing consumer interest in avoiding heavy caffeine loads (Chris personally noted cutting back to one coffee/day after noticing afternoon energy crashes from overuse).

## Idea 3: Construction Journal — reverse-engineered as a business model to replicate
- Construction Journal (existing real company, not something to build from scratch, but analyzed as a model to copy into a narrower niche) is a paid database (~$179/month tier observed) that aggregates public building-permit data by location/type into a searchable, easy-to-use interface — as opposed to the difficult-to-navigate raw government permit websites.
- Estimated size: ~2,011 employees listed on LinkedIn; using an assumed $250,000-$300,000 revenue-per-employee benchmark for database/SaaS businesses (compared to $1M+/employee for giants like Google/Apple), the hosts estimate this could be roughly a **$60 million/year business**.
- Core insight: permits signal *future* spending intent, which is valuable to a wide range of downstream service providers. Concrete examples given:
  - A tree-removal permit signals likely upcoming development/construction — useful lead for general contractors, electricians, plumbers.
  - In Texas, any new pool requires a permit AND (per code) a fence around it — meaning pool permits are a near-guaranteed lead source for fencing contractors (illustrative example cited: a pool permit could be worth $500+ minimum to a fencer given an average fence job might run ~$12,000).
  - Sprinkler/irrigation permits are leads for sod/hydromulch companies.
  - Rockport, TX charges ~$50/inch of tree diameter removed and requires a permit — a direct lead source for stump-grinding companies.
- Proposed narrower business model: instead of building a comprehensive nationwide permit database like Construction Journal, build **narrow, single-vertical "wrapper" sites** targeting one specific high-value contractor type per permit signal (example named: "PoolLeads.com" targeting fencers off pool permit data) — apply the same programmatic SEO approach discussed in a prior episode, and replicate this model across the 5 most valuable lead types identified.
- Additional value-add spotted in Construction Journal's actual product: shows a company's active projects, key contacts, funding history, and project locations — useful even for finding intel on secretive/low-visibility developers who have no public website or LinkedIn presence. Suggested unexplored extension: bid/estimate assistance (helping a customer know if a quote — e.g., a $2,000 vs. $20,000 job bid — is reasonable) as an additional paid service layer.

## Tangent: paying for aggregated public/government data as a business model
- Real example cited: a Florida real estate developer paid $10,000 for a list/report aggregating new vehicle registration data by license plate in a target metro area, ahead of official census data being published — used to sanity-check official population-growth projections against real-time proxy data before making major development decisions.
- General principle: government/publicly-required data that's technically available but hard to access/use in raw form is a repeatable business opportunity — package it into an easily filterable, sellable format for people making high-stakes (multi-million-dollar) decisions who will pay well to save research time or catch bad assumptions.
- Related framing device repeated from other episodes: someone who already spent real money on non-obvious tools/data (e.g., $10,000 on a data spreadsheet, or upgraded their website 6 months ago) is often a *better* sales prospect than someone with none of that infrastructure — they've already shown willingness to invest and are more likely to invest again, vs. someone who's opted out of that kind of spending entirely (e.g., a business owner who refuses to have a website at all).

## Idea 4: "PikFu"-style ad/creative testing agency
- Referencing a business (PikFu) that runs quick paid focus-group-style testing (e.g., "which ad/logo resonates more with X audience") for a few dollars per response.
- Proposed business: package this into an agency service — market it to entrepreneurs/business owners as informal "creative testing" or "focus group" research (without necessarily disclosing the underlying tool), charging clients meaningfully more than the ~$13 PikFu cost per test for packaged, presented results.
- Estimated market: guessed roughly 1-5% of entrepreneurs have heard of PikFu, and maybe ~20% of entrepreneurs run paid ads at all — implying a large pool (framed as ~75% of paid-ad-running entrepreneurs) who've never heard of or used this kind of pre-testing.

## Idea 5: Paid market/product validation research reports (a "diligence as a service" business)
- Concept: sell in-depth custom research/validation reports to people about to make a major business decision (e.g., "should I launch this product," "should I buy this franchise/business") — positioned as a way to de-risk a large capital commitment.
- Two customer profiles proposed:
  1. A white-collar professional about to invest a large sum (e.g., $500,000) into a franchise (example: a pressure-washing franchise) who pays a smaller fee (e.g., ~$5,000) for independent diligence/validation before committing — "diligence as a service."
  2. A mid-size company (not big enough to have in-house market research staff) wanting to validate a new product idea (new shoe, food, or beverage line).
- Proposed pricing/model: charge a custom client a high fee (example figures discussed: $25,000 for a full ~50-page custom report, potentially treated as a loss-leader or break-even), then repackage/resell the same underlying research publicly a few months later at a lower recurring price (e.g., ~$500/year) to a broader audience — building the report once and monetizing it multiple ways.
- Cited existing practice from a real operator: buys businesses across unrelated categories (candy, insurance, and — jokingly — "even Apple stock," compared to Warren Buffett's diversified holding style) by intensively interviewing domain experts before every acquisition to quickly become knowledgeable enough to invest responsibly (concrete example: talked to numerous pool-industry experts before buying a pool installation business in Arizona) — the research report business is essentially productizing that same expert-interview diligence process for a fee.
- E-commerce-specific variant proposed: for ~$10,000, a customer would receive product design mockups, a basic Shopify landing page, a small paid ad test campaign, and reported cost-per-click/add-to-cart metrics (with checkout intentionally disabled) — validating real demand before the customer commits to manufacturing/inventory.
- Framing lesson: when pitching an "I can do anything" research service, be specific about concrete, real deliverables (e.g., "I will call all your self-storage competitors and get updated daily pricing," "I will run a validation ad for your product idea") rather than an open-ended offer, since customers struggle to specify what they'd actually pay for without a concrete example.

## Idea 6: Asset-light gym space for independent personal trainers ("ClassPass for personal trainers")
- Origin: Connor's girlfriend is a certified personal trainer tied to a single gym's membership/space/clients — she can't easily bring her own clients elsewhere without paying separately for space.
- Two variants discussed:
  1. **Capital-heavy version**: build a dedicated small-footprint gym designed specifically for personal-trainer/client sessions rather than general public use — smaller square footage than a big-box gym (example cited: Chelsea Piers in Manhattan, 60,000 sq ft), with fewer treadmills (since trainer-led sessions rarely use treadmills for a full session) and more high-end/niche equipment (e.g., Panatta plate-loaded equipment, cryotherapy machines) to justify premium trainer pricing and boost client retention (client becomes attached to the premium equipment/experience, not just the trainer). Proposed pricing: charge trainers a monthly base fee (~$400/month, benchmarked against a ~$250/month general public gym membership figure cited) plus a small per-client fee (~$10-15) to prevent trainers overusing the space with unlimited guests.
  2. **Asset-light version (identified as the smarter model)**: don't build a gym at all — instead broker access between independent personal trainers and existing gyms/budget gyms that have idle off-peak capacity (mentioned as a parallel to a swim-school business that rents out existing country club/hotel pools rather than building its own pool). Structure it like a recurring "class pass" subscription for personal trainers, giving them access to a network of partner gyms during off-peak hours (e.g., 10am-4pm, avoiding the 7-9am and 5-8pm rush windows most people actually train in) in exchange for a per-client or subscription fee paid to the platform, with the gym getting a cut plus incidental new membership exposure from higher-income trainer clientele.
- Related idea referenced from Chris's own past: he tweeted about converting an unused 2,100 sq ft backyard shop into a training space for youth-sports personal trainers (baseball, softball, soccer) in a semi-rural DFW-suburb area, since gyms like CrossFit/24-Hour Fitness don't accommodate youth sports training and there's no dedicated venue for these trainers — the tweet got ~1M views with ~95% positive "this would work where I live" responses, suggesting broad, geography-independent demand for a dedicated home for underserved personal-training niches.
- Key trade-off named: peak commercial gym demand and typical personal-training client availability both cluster in early morning/evening hours, so an off-peak/asset-light rental model works best with trainers whose clients can train mid-day (e.g., by offering a lower mid-day rate as an incentive/positioning hook: "access to your city's best trainers at a fraction of the cost — the catch is it's at 2pm").

## Caveats / risks raised
- Several ideas (workout honey, chia drink) are explicitly framed as "packaging and framing" plays on existing/commodity ingredients, not defensible IP — hosts acknowledge most investors would dismiss these as "nothing proprietary," while arguing that's precisely why they're accessible for someone to actually execute.
- Selling physical food/supplement products carries shelf-life and regulatory unknowns not fully resolved in the discussion (e.g., raw honey shelf life, food safety) — flagged as open questions, not answered.
- The exit market for a food-focused product is narrower (mainly large CPG or food-conglomerate acquirers) versus a supplement being sold direct-to-consumer online.
- The hosts explicitly note personal bias affected their idea rankings (e.g., not personally using gyms, or being unwilling to pay for the ad-testing agency's output themselves) — a reminder that founder-market fit and being your own ideal customer matters for conviction, even when a concept seems sound on paper.
