# How to Sell a $10 Cable for $10,000
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Bx1k8G5qfjg | Published: 2024-10-04

## Print shop industry as an overlooked opportunity
- Print shops are described as unsexy, capex-heavy (expensive printing equipment = real barrier to entry), which is why they're both under-competed and hard to start from scratch — making them good acquisition targets instead.
- Case study: a coached "boomer" print shop owner in San Antonio does ~$1M+/year revenue, doing custom quotes over the phone.
- He's informally rolling up competitors: when nearby print shop owners retire, he buys their customer base (not equipment or building, not employees) via low-multiple, seller-financed deals — essentially just acquiring a recurring customer list, similar to buying an accounting practice's client book.
- Typical print shop economics cited: most only make ~$50k/year profit, trading at low multiples (~2-2.5x).

## Idea 1: Interactive quote-generator ("widget") agency for print shops
- Problem: most print shops (an estimated 23,000 in the US; ~90% have a website but ~90% of those are static/non-interactive) still generate custom price quotes manually by phone — the San Antonio owner spent up to 8 hours/day doing this; a basic price-sheet spreadsheet handoff to a staffer cut that time by ~80%, but it's still manual.
- Business model: hire a virtual assistant (e.g., via Upwork) to build one interactive quote-generation web widget, then copy/replicate that same tool across many print shop websites.
- Pricing observed: custom agency quotes for a full site rebuild ran as high as $20k-30k; the print shop owner said he'd happily pay ~$5k just for an interactive widget (not a full redesign) — actual cost to build one is far less than that.
- Suggested pricing to shop owners: $2,000-4,000 one-time plus a small recurring monthly maintenance fee.
- Framed as functioning like a lead magnet: the quote tool requires an email to reveal pricing, turning site visitors into warm leads (could optimize toward lead-gen vs. time-savings depending on target).
- Positioning suggestion: niche the agency brand specifically to "interactive websites for print shops on WordPress" for efficiency, then expand to adjacent blue-collar niches over time (auto body, barber shops, restaurants).
- **Customer acquisition tactic — "ethical bait and switch"**: scrape print shop websites (e.g., using BuiltWith to find WordPress sites) via bot or VA, submit a generic message through each site's contact form pretending to be a customer wanting a quote ("I wanted a quote on business cards but didn't see a quote generator on your site"). When the owner replies, pivot into the actual sales pitch for the widget/service. Framed as intentionally creating a warm inbound-feeling lead rather than a cold outbound pitch. Acknowledged this will irritate some recipients but is presented as a net-positive tactic.
- Alternative/complementary idea (programmatic SEO): build SEO-driven landing pages targeting "[type of print job] pricing in [city]" search queries at scale, capture leads, and sell those leads to print shops or a large multi-location print franchise (e.g., Minuteman Press) or a major retailer (e.g., Vista Print) rather than trying to build/maintain a whole network of small shop clients — selling to 1-2 large buyers is far easier than to thousands.

## Idea 2: "TAM/demand validation as a service" for business buyers/searchers
- Problem framing: when someone is evaluating a business to buy (e.g., a searcher/private equity buyer), the scariest unknown is the growth ceiling/total addressable market upside, not just the current state of the business.
- Precedent cited: the hosts once tested demand for an RV park acquisition by posting Facebook Marketplace ads at various rental price points to see response before committing to a price, which validated they could triple existing rents.
- Proposed service: run a short (e.g., one-month) demand test for a business a searcher/PE firm is considering buying — e.g., stand up simple ads or a landing page and measure inbound lead volume at a given price point — to project realistic growth/upside, then package that into the buyer's underwriting/loan packet (relevant because banks assess a "quality of earnings" (QoE) report and the buyer's ability to grow the business).
- Suggested monetization/positioning: low-ticket entry price (e.g., ~$500) to validate growth potential, which can convert into a much larger ongoing engagement (e.g., $5,000/month) to actually execute the growth/lead-gen work post-acquisition — framed as a foot-in-the-door "growth marketing agency for searchers."
- Caveat/limitation: doesn't apply well to all business types — works best for niches where demand testing is straightforward (e.g., a specific service in a specific geography) versus something like an HVAC company where testing methodology is less obvious.

## Idea 3: Group Purchasing Organization (GPO) for niche/blue-collar industries
- Concept: organize many small, independent businesses in the same industry to negotiate bulk purchasing discounts (e.g., on ink/supplies) collectively, since individually none has enough volume for leverage.
- Concrete mechanic proposed using print shops as the example: gather ~100 print shops (low friction — they all already buy ink, so it's an opt-in "yes/no" email, no sales call needed), negotiate bulk ink pricing with one of the ~3-5 major ink manufacturers, and either (a) have the manufacturer pay you a cut of the negotiated discount directly, or (b) route purchases through your own site so you control margin capture.
- Illustrative numbers: if an average shop spends $10k/month on ink, a negotiated bulk deal might bring that down to $8k/month, with the organizer capturing $500/month of the $2k savings.
- Simplicity note: an "honesty system" of manufacturer-shared visibility into member purchases is easier to start with than building full e-commerce order/fulfillment plumbing; only build the transactional website once it's proven at scale.
- Distinguished from healthcare-style GPOs (see below) which negotiate revenue/reimbursement rates rather than purchasing discounts.

## Idea 4: Collective-bargaining/negotiation service for independent healthcare providers
- Problem: independent providers (dentists, physical therapists, family practices not affiliated with a hospital system) individually have little leverage negotiating reimbursement rates with insurance companies (United Health, Aetna, Blue Cross Blue Shield, etc.); insurers can simply route patients to other providers.
- Proposed idea: organize local groups of same-specialty independent providers (e.g., "local Boise dentist group") into a collective bargaining body that negotiates reimbursement rates together, charging a membership fee to join/organize.
- Must be regional/geographic, not national — reimbursement rates for the same insurer vary by market/cost of living.
- Distinguished as negotiating revenue (what providers get paid) rather than expenses (what they pay for supplies), which is the reverse of a typical purchasing GPO.
- Extended into a general GPO monetization model example (using a hypothetical Shopify apparel-store GPO for agency services): get quoted pricing individually (e.g., $3k/month per store for agency services), then negotiate a bulk rate for a pooled group (e.g., $50k/month for 50 stores, a 66% savings vs. paying separately), and charge members a percentage of the savings achieved (example used: 30% of savings).
- Caveat: not every member of a pooled group will actually want the negotiated service, which complicates pricing/re-negotiation if commitment drops (e.g., only 12 of 50 opt in).

## Idea 5: Selling into a niche where regulatory barriers create pricing power (airline parts example)
- Anecdote: airline seat manufacturers pay reportedly around $6,000 per unit for a simple electrical cable/plug connecting a seat's heads-up display, because there's essentially one supplier and that supplier must be FAA-registered to sell into the industry — high regulatory barrier to entry limits competition, letting the single supplier charge extreme markups; the seat manufacturer then reportedly marks that up further before passing cost to the airline (e.g., Boeing).
- Framed more broadly: many super-niche B2B components have a de facto monopoly supplier simply because "they're the only ones thinking of doing it," and any regulatory hurdle (like FAA registration) discourages new entrants, sustaining outsized margins.
- Broader idea layered on top: build a way to surface these kinds of insider, non-googleable insights from people deep inside specific industries (people with 15-20+ years in a niche corporate role who will never start their own business but see obvious unmet opportunities) — e.g., a marketplace/community connecting industry insiders sharing pain points with people willing to pay for that inside knowledge, compared to how Hampton or CLG connect people with paid access to expert/insider knowledge.

## General idea-generation framework discussed
- Debate over sequencing: for someone who is "idea agnostic" (not mission-driven to one specific idea), the suggested framework is to start with a distribution channel you already have access to (a network, an existing audience, an existing customer base) and work backward to find/build the product — rather than starting with an idea and searching for distribution afterward.
- If buying an existing business instead of starting one, this sequencing doesn't apply the same way.
