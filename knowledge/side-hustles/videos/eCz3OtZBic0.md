# I've Started 75+ Businesses: Here's What Actually Matters
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eCz3OtZBic0 | Published: 2025-05-08

Chris Koerner's retrospective on running businesses from six to eight figures in revenue -- mostly CEO/operator lessons rather than new business ideas, but includes concrete numbers and specific past ventures.

## Businesses referenced (with numbers)
- **Phone Restore** (his first business, college): iPhone repair shop started as a University of Alabama senior after seeing another repair shop and reading about LSU students doing $30k/month with a similar store. Signed a lease with a personal guarantee. Grew to 4 locations before being sold/merged with a competitor.
  - Growth-tactic anecdote: printed custom wristbands (2 cents each, ordered off Google search results) with his shop's ad on them and distributed them to college bars -- students who broke phones while drunk would bring in the wristband, materially changing the business's trajectory.
  - A tornado outbreak (April 27, 2011) destroyed part of a store right before students left for the semester, dropping revenue to zero; recovered partly by liquidating previously-unlisted trade-in iPhones on eBay.
  - Post-sale, the acquiring partners agreed to pay him 1/3 of profits in perpetuity but stopped after one month once they learned his other business was doing well -- a real financial/legal dispute, not resolved amicably in the telling.
  - Tested (and got burned by) an iPhone-repair mall kiosk format -- concrete cautionary anecdote: punctured a customer's overheating phone battery with a screwdriver mid-repair, causing it to smoke/hiss in the middle of a mall; had to buy the customer a new phone and she lost her data.
- **LCD Cycle** (second major business, growing "very fast"): revenue trajectory cited as $2M year one, $4.8M year two, $8.8M year three. Funded in part by an unsecured, no-paperwork loan from a mentor (John Nolan, a wealthy church acquaintance) who asked only that Koerner donate what he thought was fair to Nolan's nonprofit instead of taking equity or interest.
  - Turned down a $1M-for-20%-equity term sheet from a Birmingham, AL venture capital fund after praying about it and getting a "no" he didn't understand at the time; 8 years later, re-reading the term sheet, he identified a standard liquidation preference clause that would have taken "first fruits" of profit in a downturn -- retrospectively glad he declined, since the business later shrank.
  - Later scaled explosively with two new investor/partners on a mobile-home/RV-park roll-up business, secured a $50M term sheet (100x bigger than prior term sheets) -- but the two partners abruptly forced him out of the deal, telling him directly "we know this is wrong, we know you're probably going to sue us, but we're going to do it anyway." He had barely vetted these partners' character/history beforehand. Went on afterward to buy more RV/mobile home parks with better-vetted partners.
- **Hartland Bread** (DFW-based artisanal bread maker, 5 ingredients only): approached the owner (a church acquaintance) with a no-money-down revenue-share deal -- Koerner would build and pay for an online sales channel and take a cut of what he sold; owner had zero downside. Launched right before COVID and sales "went crazy."
- **Send Eats**: e-commerce fulfillment/logistics company for food brands, grown out of the Hartland Bread e-commerce success. Leased a 10,000 sq ft warehouse, 32 employees, reached $300k/month topline -- but was NOT profitable (low-margin logistics competing effectively against Amazon-scale players; described as stuck in "the messy middle," neither able to out-cheap Amazon nor position as a premium niche service).
  - Had one customer responsible for 85% of Send Eats' revenue ($250k/month topline, also unprofitable) and roughly 85% of its staffing need. The customer's owner was abusive/toxic to Koerner and his team. Despite the extreme customer concentration risk, Koerner explicitly chose not to preemptively "fire" the risky customer earlier -- instead invested in the relationship to try to grow beyond it -- until the personal toll became unsustainable (this coincided with a family medical crisis), at which point he fired both the customer and the employees whose jobs depended on fulfilling that account.
- **Bitcoin mining facility**: bought an off-market, under-market facility around peak 2021 (when China banned Bitcoin mining, pushing mining capacity to the US) -- grew it from $0 to $10M profitable revenue in 3 months, and to ~$15M within 12 months, bootstrapped. Explicitly flagged in hindsight as a business he "should not have started" despite the extraordinary short-term returns -- attributes the results to fortunate market timing rather than skill, and frames it as a "just because you could doesn't mean you should" cautionary example.
- **Better Exit**: his RV and mobile home park acquisition company (context for the $50M term-sheet partner conflict above).
- Overall stated track record: 75+ businesses started over 15 years; some six-figure, many seven-figure, some eight-figure; many failed or were abandoned.

## Operating principles / lessons (the actual "what matters" content)
1. **"MIH gene" (Make It Happen)**: treat every problem as figureoutable; don't allow yourself the option to give up. Illustrated by learning to repair iPhones via YouTube in real time as customers waited, and by liquidating unlisted trade-in inventory on eBay after a tornado zeroed out revenue overnight.
2. **"Know the weeds"**: as owner/CEO you must personally learn the operational details of the business (how to quote jobs, negotiate with subcontractors, what price points and margins to target, how to use the operating software -- e.g., cites learning Jobber for his tree-trimming business) -- but you don't need to stay in the weeds forever, or the business won't scale. Explicitly contrasts two well-known pieces of advice from other operators (Michael Girdley: "hire smart people, leave them alone" vs. Andrew Wilkinson: "your job as CEO is to do nothing but set strategy/culture and delegate") and concludes the right answer "depends" on business size -- at the small-to-mid-size businesses most listeners run, rockstar hires still need regular hands-on check-ins or they "die on the vine," unlike at public-company scale.
3. **Insulate your employees from stress**: as CEO, absorb the worst problems yourself rather than passing anxiety down; illustrated by an employee (later revealed to be the actual thief) falsely reporting a break-in/robbery at Phone Restore -- Koerner had to manage the crisis calmly without immediately knowing the employee was lying.
4. **Protect the business itself, not just people**: don't "move fast and break things" recklessly (contra Zuckerberg) -- apply "calculated pressure" instead, since some mistakes (e.g., partnering with unvetted people) are not reversible. Directly tied to the $50M-deal partner betrayal -- a due-diligence failure he attributes to not vetting his partners' track record/character before partnering.
5. **Invent a rival / manufacture a chip on your shoulder**: even a synthetic sense of rivalry (e.g., former business partners who stopped paying agreed profit-share) can become useful fuel for growth -- credits this dynamic partly for LCD Cycle's early growth curve.
6. **Capitalize the business properly**: running out of capital ends the business regardless of sales; take funding from people who genuinely have your best interest at heart (contrast: an unbiased mentor's advice vs. a slick-but-conflicted VC term sheet).
7. **Protect company culture from toxic people, even good-hearted ones**: describes having to fire a personally likable but destructive employee (driving away strong performers) despite personal/social ties (a fellow churchgoer) -- "your business will be the lowest common denominator of your most toxic employee."
8. **Don't wait for a rockstar employee to ask for a raise** -- proactively reward top performers before they have to ask, since a checked-out rockstar is very hard to re-engage.
9. **Test everything except drugs**: bias toward experimentation (mall kiosk test, wristband marketing test, etc.) as a general growth principle, tempered by the "calculated pressure" caveat above.
10. **Beware of pride at the top and despair at the bottom**: cites Nick Huber's public "everything's awesome" tweet (later followed by a difficult 12 months) as a case study -- feeling invincible is itself a warning signal, just as feeling like everything is falling apart is often close to a turning point upward. Self-awareness of where you are in that cycle is valuable, even though it doesn't tell you what's coming next.
11. On divided founder focus (using Elon Musk/multiple companies as the extreme example): splitting attention across multiple ventures likely caps each one below its full potential (his estimate: maybe 60% of potential per company) -- not necessarily wrong, but founders/investors should go in aware of that tradeoff rather than pretending it doesn't exist.

## Caveats
- This video is retrospective/leadership-focused, not a how-to for starting a specific new business -- no launch playbook or financial specifics beyond the anecdotal revenue figures above.
- Customer concentration (85% of revenue/staff from one client) is explicitly acknowledged as high risk; Koerner's own choice was to lean into serving that customer better rather than diversify away from it, until the relationship became personally untenable -- presented as a judgment call, not a universal recommendation.
