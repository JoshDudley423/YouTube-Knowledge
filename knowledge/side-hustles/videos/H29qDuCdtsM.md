# Secrets to Making $10k/Season with Porch Flowers
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H29qDuCdtsM | Published: 2025-02-12

Guest: Darryl Knight, owner of Front Porch Flora (frontporchflora.com), a Long Island seasonal front-porch-planter subscription business, interviewed by Chris Koerner.

## The business idea
- Seasonal flower/planter subscription service: customer pays a seasonal fee for pre-arranged flower planters at their front porch (typically two planters per house), which are swapped out **on a fixed schedule 4x/year** (not based on how the plants look) rather than a one-off purchase.
- Two fulfillment models tried:
  1. **Original (failed) model**: partner with greenhouses to grow arrangements using an invented product called a "plant pie"/"combo" tray (shallow, self-pruning root design, ~2 inches of soil, roots in about a week "like sod for flowers," made of plastic) — greenhouse fulfills the order and delivers to a nursery, customer picks up at the nursery. Failed because: (a) the plant pie tray didn't fit standard greenhouse shipping shelving (built for square/rectangular 4-inch pots, 15 per tray), so greenhouses wouldn't adopt it; (b) nurseries would have needed extra staff to verify pickup payment/QR codes — too much operational friction for partners.
  2. **Current model (what actually scaled)**: Darryl buys plants directly from greenhouses himself, pre-assembles arrangements into a removable plastic **insert** (fits inside a purchased planter, sits on a base of mulch) at his own house, then a driver swaps the old insert for a new pre-built one on-site — no digging, no soil work in the field. The company owns and provides the outer planter (~$30 planter from Lowe's) for free as long as the customer subscribes; if they cancel, the company collects the planter back.
  3. A minority of "more affluent" customers keep their own decorative planters (no standard insert fits), requiring an on-site truck visit and manual planting each swap — Darryl is actively trying to convert these customers to the standardized insert model because it's far less labor.
  4. Seasonal add-ons launched based on viewer feedback from watching another TKO episode: front-porch pumpkin displays (sold ~14 packages in a couple of weeks in year one, converted several into recurring Christmas customers) and Christmas "tree" displays (grow bags with braced trees, wrapped in ribbon, placed on porch steps) — the Christmas tree offering was likely underpriced in its first year and a free-cleanup/removal was offered (vs. charging for removal like the pumpkins).

## Concrete numbers
- Pricing (per planter, per season): Small (up to 12") = $90; Medium = ~$115-120 (partially garbled in transcript, contextually between small and large); Large = $145; anything beyond that is custom pricing.
- Typical customer spends $500-$800/year (most customers subscribe to all 4 seasons; ~100 total customers).
- Margin: ~$60 net profit per planter (calculated directly from cost of soil/mulch/plant material + labor to build and deliver, then a targeted margin on top).
- Season-level profit: roughly $10,000 net profit per season (his estimate, at ~100 customers), which he called a "low ball" figure.
- Customer churn: ~20 customers lost total over almost 5 years; most churn comes from planters given as one-off gifts (e.g., for a parent) where the giftee isn't invested in renewing; he's personally let go of only 2 customers.
- Labor to fulfill one season for the ~100-customer base: ~16 hours to pre-build all inserts (2 days) + ~40 hours of driving/swapping = ~56 hours of total labor per season, spread over roughly 3 months (makes it hard to retain part-time seasonal labor since hours aren't steady week to week).
- At a hypothetical $25/hour wage (~$1,350-1,450 with payroll taxes for 56 hours), even fully outsourcing the labor while keeping ~$10K seasonal profit implies strong margins are achievable even without doing the work personally.
- Employee retention challenge: had one older employee for 2 years (physically demanding work — bending/kneeling — became too hard for her, especially in cold weather) and one younger employee for 2 years; best-fit hires are people who are semi-retired or only want part-time/seasonal work, since the workload isn't steady enough for full-time staff.
- Started business in 2020; runs it alongside a full-time job as a police officer.
- Customer acquisition channel history: originally mostly Facebook ads/organic + friends and family (skewed toward an older/Boomer demographic); has since shifted toward Instagram, converting more Millennial customers; also just started (as of recording) a paid ad in a local affluent-neighborhood "Stroll"-style community magazine (results not yet known).

## Steps/process described
1. Source plants directly from greenhouses (not through a distributor/nursery middleman).
2. Pre-build seasonal insert arrangements in batch at home/shop rather than on-site.
3. Swap inserts on a fixed 4x/year calendar regardless of how the current planting looks (schedule-driven, not condition-driven), to keep logistics predictable.
4. Offer flexible subscription lengths (2, 3, or 4 seasons) so customers can choose (e.g., pool-area planters might only need summer coverage).
5. Reuse some seasonal plant stock across seasons when viable (e.g., certain petunias can carry over) but most spent plants are discarded, not reused (e.g., spring pansies wilt in heat and aren't repurposed; bulb plants like tulips were dropped from the lineup because their bloom window is too short relative to cost).
6. Rotate plant varieties season to season so repeat customers get variety rather than the exact same look every cycle — explicit differentiator versus buying generic flowers at a grocery store (his stated main "competitor").
7. Pricing approach: calculate cost of materials (soil/mulch/plants) + labor to build and deliver, then add a target margin — he explicitly said a rigid formula based on planter size never worked well in practice.
8. Growth funnel idea (not yet executed as of the episode): partner with real estate agents, who traditionally give home-sellers a one-time closing gift — pitch a seasonal flower subscription instead, since it keeps the agent "front of mind" with the client 4 times a year at a small fraction of a typical real estate commission (example: Long Island average home value ~$500K implies a ~$15K average commission, making a ~$500 flower gift/investment trivial by comparison).
9. Flower/planter business explicitly framed as "a great foot-in-the-door" into bigger, higher-ticket landscaping projects — he has separately expanded into larger landscape jobs (2021-2024 escalating from small requests to bigger contracts) after building trust with planter customers who saw his design sense.

## Tools/platforms named
- Facebook ads / organic Facebook posting (early customer acquisition)
- Instagram (current primary organic acquisition channel, skews younger)
- Facebook Marketplace (used opportunistically to test-sell pumpkin packages)
- A local "Stroll"-style community magazine ad product (regional, subsidized-by-advertisers residential magazine — not confirmed nationwide)
- frontporchflora.com / Instagram / Facebook (his own business's channels)

## Caveats / risks
- Vertically integrating (growing his own plants, he owns 2 acres) was considered but rejected as "too much headache" while still holding a full-time job.
- Scalability is uneven: the pre-built insert/swap model is described as "very scalable," but the subset of custom, non-insert-compatible planters for affluent customers requires much more on-site labor and does not scale the same way.
- Business is highly seasonal, making steady part-time/hourly staffing difficult — labor needs spike in short windows, and workers who need reliable income tend to leave during slow periods (late summer lull cited specifically).
- Winter is called out as his most operationally stressful season — he is "almost not able to get it done before Christmas," i.e., near capacity at current scale while working a full-time job on the side.
- He self-describes as being in the "messy middle" — not clearly committed to scaling hard (which would require converting more customers to the insert model and likely hiring more reliably) nor staying small; flagged as a real strategic tension for a seasonal service business run as a side hustle.
- Gifted subscriptions (bought for a family member rather than by the end user) have meaningfully higher churn than self-purchased subscriptions.
- The original "plant pie" partnership model failed specifically due to a physical standardization mismatch with existing greenhouse/nursery shipping infrastructure — a cautionary example of designing around a novel product without confirming it fits supply-chain norms.
