# He Built a Simple App and Sold It for $200 Million
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0pJEW-sFDXk | Published: 2025-11-07

Interview with Dan Porter (built Draw Something, sold for $200M; founded Overtime sports media; co-created 67 Water) — mostly founder philosophy/product-design lessons rather than a single side-hustle "how-to," but contains concrete tactical takeaways.

## Draw Something: what made it work (product mechanics, not a replicable side-hustle per se, but instructive design principles)

- Company (OMGPOP) had tried the same drawing/guessing game concept twice before as an open-lobby, play-with-strangers Facebook/web game; it flopped both times because open matchmaking with strangers led to toxic behavior (people drawing inappropriate images, being rude).
- Fix: restricted the mobile version to only playing with existing friends (from your contacts/social graph) — this "solved" the toxicity problem not by moderating behavior but by removing anonymity, and created built-in virality via friend invites.
- No win/lose mechanic — instead a "streak" mechanic between two specific players, framed as collaborative rather than competitive; inspired directly by watching two kids play catch in a park and offering ice cream if they could keep a catch streak going.
- A live-drawing "replay" feature (built by developer Will Chen, not Porter's own idea) let the recipient watch the picture being drawn stroke-by-stroke rather than just seeing a static final image — made the game feel "alive." Porter says the game would not have hit the same scale without this specific feature.
- Porter explicitly frames it as 4 essential ingredients that all had to be present simultaneously (friends-only play, streak mechanic, live drawing playback, simplicity/finger-based tactile interaction) — removing any single one, in his view, would have killed the product's breakout success entirely, not just reduced it proportionally.
- Result numbers: went to #1 in the App Store and stayed there every day for 6 months; #1 game in 60 countries; ~25 million daily players at peak; ~250 million total downloads; sold to Zynga for $200 million.
- Near-miss detail: the game initially "bombed" on release; developers noticed unusually high server load and realized there was a technical bug preventing people from actually playing it (i.e., demand existed but was blocked) — fixing that bug is what let it shoot to #1. Porter frames this as "one tweak away from $200 million."

## Overtime (youth/next-gen sports media company)

- Founded on the observation (from Porter's prior job at talent agency WME) that young people had stopped watching live televised sports but were heavily engaged with YouTubers — bet there was an opportunity to build a sports brand for that audience despite having zero broadcast/highlight rights to any professional league.
- Correcting a common misconception: Overtime's early content model was NOT user-generated content (UGC). They built proprietary iPhone camera/tagging software and paid a deployed network of roughly 800 independent filmers ~$20 each to attend specific games and film/tag footage using that software, which Overtime then published — an owned, paid content-sourcing pipeline, not organic fan uploads.
- Some of the strongest filmers were promoted into full-time roles at the company (some started filming for Overtime as 16-year-olds).
- Over time, Overtime reduced dependence on the filmer network by launching its own owned sports leagues (basketball, football, girls' basketball).
- Current scale cited: ~250 million people watch an Overtime-made video every week; 110+ million followers across platforms.

## 67 Water (meme-to-consumer-product brand)

- Built a bottled water brand around an internet meme/slang ("6 7"), co-founded with athlete Talen Horton-Tucker.
- Chose water specifically because it required no product testing/tasting/formulation — enabled turning the whole product around in 8 weeks.
- Traction: tens of thousands of units sold; 41,000-person waitlist; described as one of the most-followed water brands.
- Product development process: interns visit gas stations/bodegas in New York and talk directly to shoppers (including middle-schoolers buying drinks) about what they want (e.g., flavor requests) — described as informal "customer development" rather than formal market research, directly driving product decisions (e.g., deciding to add a flavor).
- Distribution strategy: deliberately launching/focusing distribution in Kentucky (co-founder's home state) rather than the default startup launch markets (LA/SF/NYC), reasoning that those default markets are oversaturated and harder to get real traction in.

## General advice on building/creator economy (useful for someone starting from scratch)

- On becoming a creator today with no existing audience: pick a niche you can "own" — most broad categories (finance, cooking, health/fitness) are crowded, but there's "always room for people who are creative" within them; noted that ~40% of Gen Z say they want to be a YouTuber, i.e., very high competition, so differentiation matters more than category choice.
- On AI content vs. human creators: believes AI accelerates creators' output but doesn't replace the specifically human ability to make idiosyncratic connections/resonance; expects AI and human creativity to be additive rather than a full replacement.
- On starting something outside your expertise: cites a friend who moved from internet/fashion industries into starting a sparkling-water brand with zero category experience, framing "I don't know anything about it, but I'm an entrepreneur — that's what I do, I learn about stuff" as a genuine asset (an "outsider" perspective can be a structural advantage rather than a handicap), and reasons that an "expert" might have gotten hung up on adding features (e.g., XP/experience points in Draw Something, which competing designers at Zynga assumed was a deliberate, important design choice — but was simply something Porter forgot to include).
- On research vs. building: argues actually building/shipping the product IS the market research — repeatedly emphasizes shipping a first "premise" quickly, observing real user behavior, and iterating, rather than extensive upfront research or user interviews.
- On focus vs. breadth: personally very obsessive/hands-on (anecdote: spending 12+ hours himself re-wiring home ethernet rather than hiring an electrician) — argues that being "all in" and personally obsessed with a business is what makes it excel, skeptical of people who claim to successfully run several businesses simultaneously without that level of personal obsession.
- On passion vs. profit: argues you don't need to monetize your literal hobbies/interests (e.g., poetry) — instead, look for passion in things you're good at and that have real market demand (accounting, sales, marketing, etc.); competence often generates passion, not just the reverse.

## Caveats

- This episode is framed around narrative/philosophy and one specific mega-outcome (a $200M app sale) rather than a low-cost, replicable side-hustle blueprint — there's no direct "here's how you copy this" plan; the actionable takeaways are about product-design principles (streak mechanics, friends-only virality loops, live/authentic feedback features), the value of outsider thinking, and rapid build-and-observe iteration rather than a specific business template a beginner could execute step by step.
