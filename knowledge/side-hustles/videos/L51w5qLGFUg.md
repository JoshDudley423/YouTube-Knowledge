# How to Make $60k per Weekend with an Auto Fair
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L51w5qLGFUg | Published: 2024-09-07

- Core idea: run a **"Sierra Auto Fair"-style event** — a weekend car-selling fair held in an otherwise-idle parking lot (e.g., a community college or high school lot unused on weekends), acting as **middleman/venue operator** between people who want to sell a car and people who want to buy one.
- Real-world model referenced (Sierra AutoFair, named after a community college): sellers pay **$149** to reserve a numbered parking spot; they fill out a form, scan a QR code, park the car, leave keys with an attendant, and return ~2 days later to retrieve the car (or it sells). Buyers attend **for free**, browse, and can test-drive if the seller left keys. Sierra AutoFair does not get involved in the actual purchase/payment (cash, cashier's check, etc. is between buyer and seller) — it only collects the $149 listing fee and tracks sales for its own marketing/testimonials.
- Revenue math implied by title: framed as a way to generate roughly **$60k in a single weekend** at scale (149/car x large number of cars), though the hosts note this is likely the result of years of organic growth, not a first-event outcome.
- Business structure risk flagged: this is a **three-sided marketplace** (buyers, sellers, venue) — harder to balance than a typical two-sided marketplace; risk of lopsided turnout (e.g., 100 cars/5 buyers or 500 buyers/10 cars) is a real operational risk, unlike an online marketplace where supply/demand mismatches are less visible/embarrassing.
- Suggested go-to-market / cold-start strategy for launching a copycat in a new market:
  - Start small: aim for ~20 cars first, ~2 months lead time.
  - Charge sellers a fully-refundable deposit (e.g., $100-$200) refunded only if they actually show up — ensures real commitment/supply before marketing to buyers.
  - Source sellers directly by messaging people already listing cars on **Facebook Marketplace** and **AutoTrader.com** (private-party only), prioritizing high-demand, easy-to-sell vehicles (e.g., Honda Civics, Ford F-150s) rather than niche/hard-to-sell cars — because the point is to move inventory, not run a static car show.
  - Alternate distribution idea: for high-demand listings (e.g., an F-150 on Marketplace), offer the seller a refundable fee and pitch consolidating all their multiple interested buyers into one time slot at the fair, creating a bidding/FOMO dynamic instead of one-on-one home visits.
  - Demand-side generation: post in local Facebook buy/sell/trade car groups, run Facebook ads, or door-knock/flyer the neighborhood; expect the first event to be a loss-leading investment (guessed at "a couple thousand dollars") before word-of-mouth and an established Facebook group make subsequent events self-sustaining.
  - Distribution partnership idea: piggyback on high school fundraiser coupon books (the kind with local business coupons) by buying a coupon/ad spot to reach the local community that's already primed to browse and buy.
- Monetization add-ons discussed (to "de-risk" and add revenue streams beyond the flat listing fee):
  - Partner with local auto body shops/mechanics as event sponsors: provide one mechanic on-site for a few hours to offer paid quick inspections (e.g., ~$50/inspection) to buyers — mechanic gets warm leads for full repair work, event gets a sponsor.
  - Same "sponsor + service" model could apply to other verticals: an "Apple electronics fair" (iPads/MacBooks/iPhones only, above a certain generation) sponsored by Geek Squad or a local phone-repair shop for on-site diagnostics.
  - Other potential community sponsors: local moving companies (buyers/sellers are often in a "life change" moment), used car dealerships (could gain foot traffic even without directly selling), banks/credit unions (offer private-party auto financing on site), mobile oil-change/tire/brake services offering add-on services to attendees.
  - Lead-generation angle: capture buyer registrant contact info (name/email/phone) and resell as leads to banks, auto detailers, repair shops, etc. (multiple relisting of the same lead).
- Real-world comparable business mentioned: a lot in **Huntsville, Alabama** — a 3-acre unattended gravel strip along a highway where sellers pay **$200/month** to park a car with a for-sale sign; no attendant, buyers just show up and call the number on the sign. Framed as a low-effort way to monetize raw roadside land.
- Alternate lower-capital variant floated: instead of buying highway land, **lease ~20 "least desirable" parking spots** at the edge of a large retail lot (e.g., Walmart, Hobby Lobby) for ~$500/month (similar to how a Dutch Bros coffee kiosk leases lot space) and let people park cars for sale there — cheaper to test than owning land, but flagged as carrying more liability risk (theft/break-ins in an unsupervised, all-hours setting) versus a bounded-hours fair model; suggested doing a short month-to-month trial lease to validate demand before committing further.
- Overall caveats: uncertain legal/liability questions raised but not resolved — whether city permits are needed to "conduct commerce" in a lot, what liability the organizer bears for buyer/seller disputes or fraud, and what rent arrangement (if any) an operator would need with the host property (school, retailer, developer); the hosts explicitly note they don't know how Sierra AutoFair handles the venue/rent relationship. Best suited to **high-ticket physical goods that benefit from in-person inspection** (cars, furniture, electronics) rather than low-value items like a typical craft fair.
