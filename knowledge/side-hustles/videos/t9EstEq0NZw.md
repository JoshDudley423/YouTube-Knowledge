# How To Successfully Acquire a SaaS Business: Key Strategies and Tips
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t9EstEq0NZw | Published: 2024-02-27

Live call-in Q&A episode ("Koerner's Corner") — Chris Koerner takes questions from 9 different callers on different businesses. Despite the title, it covers many unrelated topics. Notes organized by caller/topic.

## Payment processing for a B2B services business (invoicing/one-time payments)
- For simple setup: use **Stripe** (has full recurring billing features, but expensive — high effective fees).
- For lower cost but more setup effort: use **Authorized.net** as the payment gateway plus a separate payment processor, specifically seeking **"interchange plus" pricing** — this passes through the real (non-negotiable) card network cost plus a small processor markup, rather than Stripe/Shopify-style flat markup. Debit cards cost almost nothing under interchange-plus (~0.1%), while Amex runs 2-3%+; since most customers pay with debit/non-Amex cards, interchange-plus can be much cheaper at volume. Target: aim for a quarter-percent (0.25%) or less markup over interchange.
- For "no website, no orders online" businesses: use **Card.com** (a payment-link/invoicing widget) or **HoneyBook** to send a simple payment link without needing a full website; alternative is PayPal but its flat fees are comparable to Stripe's (not cheap). If a custom simple site/interface is needed, post the job on **Upwork**.

## Cold email agency connecting staffing/recruiting firms with employers (caller "Corey")
- Business: cold-email agency whose clients are staffing/recruiting companies; the same cold-email infrastructure is used both to acquire the staffing-company clients AND to find them candidate-placement opportunities (e.g., connecting a recruiter specializing in legal or medical talent with businesses that need that niche talent).
- Proposed pricing (untested at time of call): $500 flat setup/onboarding/tech fee for month one, plus $300 per booked meeting.
- Recruiting-industry commission benchmark cited by caller: staffing/recruiting firms typically earn 15-20% of a placed candidate's first-year salary (candidate salaries $50-100K), which is why staffing/recruiting was chosen as a "high-ticket" niche to target.
- Tools used: **Smartlead** (email sending), Microsoft (email accounts/inboxes), **ListKit** (lead sourcing).
- Chris's advice on vertical selection: run a structured split test — pick 5 target industries, send identical email copy/subject lines to each (varying only the named industry, e.g., "we specialize only in finding talent for the [X] industry"), 200 emails/day per industry (1,000/day total) for at least 2 weeks (10 business days) to get a meaningful sample. Explicit warning: don't be fooled by vanity metrics — open rate does NOT correlate with response rate, and response rate does NOT correlate with actual booked-meeting/paid-conversion rate; the only real metric after 2 weeks is who actually pays. After finding a working vertical, niche the whole brand/website/messaging down to that one industry (e.g., rename the company and site specifically around it) rather than staying generalist. For rigorous optimization, could extend the test to 100 industries over 6 months; for a "good enough" answer, 2 weeks across 5 industries suffices.

## Buying an existing espresso catering business (email question)
- Scenario: a 32-year employee-turned-buyer is considering purchasing his boss's espresso catering business (travels to offices/schools for "employee appreciation" events, charges ~$400 per 2-hour on-site session), where quality is "middle of the road" vs. competitors, but has strong word-of-mouth loyalty built over 32 years, client count unclear (estimated "dozens to multiple dozens").
- Key risk flagged: the business is likely heavily owner-dependent — much of the repeat business may exist because customers like/trust the specific owner personally, not the brand, so a change in ownership (even to an existing employee) risks losing that repeat revenue even though the buyer already knows the operational details (which de-risks ~60% of typical acquisition risk, in Chris's estimate).
- Mitigation strategy suggested: run a 6-12 month "owner transition" plan before taking over — shadow the current owner on every client interaction, meet all his contacts directly, and proactively tell recurring/repeat customers a transition plan is underway, so by the actual handover it's "a surprise to no one." References Dave Ramsey's company deliberately building "non-Dave revenue" (people who buy without ever having interacted with the founder) — today cited at ~70% of Ramsey's ~$400M company's revenue — as the model for de-risking founder/owner dependency.
- On post-purchase improvements: don't necessarily need dramatic changes — focus first on customer service and re-branding yourself as "the person who cares" and wants to live up to the reputation, rather than assuming you must fix quality/marketing immediately.

## Growing an income tax prep side business (caller "Anthony")
- Client profile: self-employed individuals (newly self-employed Uber drivers, real estate agents, healthcare professionals), not businesses requiring EA/CPA credentials.
- Numbers: average gross revenue per client ~$350-400/year; near-zero churn (retained essentially all of 15 clients over several seasons).
- Existing side deal: enrolled in an Intuit revenue-share pilot program (Intuit drives traffic, 50/50 revenue split) — too new to have data yet.
- Chris's client-acquisition advice:
  1. Identify the exact life moment when a prospect becomes a likely customer (e.g., the day someone becomes an Uber driver or real estate agent, or the moment they receive a 1099 and get confused about taxes) and target outreach to that moment.
  2. Cold-call real estate **brokerages** (not individual agents) in the geography with your highest existing client concentration — pitch yourself as a real-estate-specialist tax preparer with a strong retention track record, and ask the broker directly if any of their agents need this; a single broker relationship can refer 5-50 clients versus calling agents one by one.
  3. Use Zillow (or realtor.com) sorted by most-recently-added agent or by lowest review count to identify brand-new agents; specifically look for new agents who already have a disproportionately high number of reviews for their tenure (e.g., new agents who already have 7 reviews when the local average is 5 total) — these are likely high performers who will have higher, more complex income and can be charged more.
  4. Same tactic can extend to healthcare licensing niches: **BCBAs (Board Certified Behavior Analysts)**, the credential required to open an autism clinic, are publicly listed online with license renewal dates and are a fast-growing (double-digit YoY), well-compensated profession whose members are rarely cold-called with tax services — same logic applies to dentists and other publicly-licensed healthcare professionals.

## Buying a small SaaS/micro-app product on Acquire.com or Flippa (caller: contract software engineer)
- Target profile to look for: low ticket price ($1-30K acquisition range, generating $500-1,000/month), very low churn, and — critically — an owner who has **not raised the price in a long time (or ever)**, suggesting the product may be underpriced relative to the value customers get from it. Verify good existing reviews.
- Due diligence approach: ask the seller for an anonymized customer list (even just partial emails), pick 5 customers at random, and interview them directly (posing as doing "an audit" for the owner) about how integral the product is to their workflow — versus being something they simply forgot to cancel.
- Post-acquisition playbook: raise prices in a structured A/B test — e.g., take 10% of the customer base from $5/mo to $30/mo and observe churn (10-20% churn = "you've got a winner"); take another 10% slice to $50/mo and see if churn jumps sharply (e.g., 80%) — that tells you the price ceiling/"pressure point." You generally can't know the right answer without actually raising prices and observing real churn.
- For ad-supported (AdSense) content sites as an alternative to SaaS acquisitions: fine to acquire these too — the same "raise the price" logic applies, but instead of raising subscription price you swap the low-paying AdSense network for a more boutique/niche ad-tech platform that pays more per impression, since AdSense is described as "the lowest payer out there."
- Person/resource cited as the source of most of this playbook: **James Camp** (active on Twitter/X), who specifically buys small SaaS products and executes this exact "raise the price" strategy.

## Starting a roofing repair company via a 50/50 crew partnership (caller "Jack")
- Setup: partnering 50/50 with a coworker who has an existing, unlicensed roofing repair crew (previously run informally by the coworker's late stepfather on a cost-plus basis — charging homeowners a markup on top of materials + labor, e.g., competitor charges $75/square vs. this crew's informal $125-ish markup approach). Buyer has already registered an LLC, applied for a home-improvement license, priced insurance in Louisiana, and registered a domain, planning to operate in both Louisiana and Mississippi.
- Chris's advice:
  1. Put a clear written operating agreement in place for the 50/50 partnership, including a pre-agreed third-party mediator (ideally someone neither partner knows) for future disputes — do this proactively, not after a conflict arises.
  2. Don't rely on SEO for near-term leads — roofing is one of the most competitive/high-ticket home-services verticals (alongside pools and home building), with entire companies dedicated solely to roofing lead-gen; SEO could take 3-9 months of investment to pay off. However, having an affordable, reliable crew already in place removes an estimated 70% of the typical startup headache in this business.
  3. Don't hire a dedicated salesperson at the outset — the founders themselves should do all early sales (even if not naturally skilled at it) in order to personally learn the customer objections and pain points before ever training/hiring a salesperson; get at least 5 jobs done under the company's belt first. A skilled experienced roofing salesperson is unlikely to leave a comfortable job for an unproven startup — expect to eventually need to hire and train someone "young, hungry" rather than poach a veteran.
  4. Referral idea specifically mentioned: building a referral relationship pipeline with real estate agents/realtors for roofing leads (a live audience member, John Moore, is cited as having built exactly this kind of realtor-referral system for roofing leads in Utah).

## Scaling B2B sales for a law-enforcement/security incident-reporting SaaS (caller "Gene")
- Product: field incident reporting software for police departments and private security groups (e.g., casino security) — used to document slips/falls, counterfeit money, lost-and-found, and other liability-relevant incidents. Competes against pen-and-paper, Microsoft Word, or old/outdated legacy web systems.
- Numbers: pricing $500-1,000/month per customer; near-zero churn (highly embedded in daily workflow); sales cycle 30-60 days from first contact (often closes immediately after an in-person demo), plus another ~30 days for implementation; gross margin ~70% (cloud infrastructure cost); minimal ongoing support burden (a few support requests/year per account).
- Segment comparison: private-sector customers (e.g., casino security groups, especially a small casino with an attached hotel) are easier/faster to sell and cash-flow than government agencies (police departments), which involve longer sales cycles, legacy-data migration, and more integration work — casinos are the higher-priority segment to pursue right now despite there being far more police agencies overall as a market.
- Original customer acquisition: warm relationship from a prior job (called an old contact and asked them to beta test).
- Chris's proposed scaled outbound tactic (framed explicitly as unconventional but effective): send physical direct mail to owners/GMs of target businesses (list sourced via a VA or **Outscraper**) in an intentionally alarming envelope (bold red text reading something like "LAWSUIT ENCLOSED" or "YOU'RE BEING SUED") to guarantee it gets opened; inside, defuse with humor ("just kidding, but this could happen") before pivoting to a real liability pitch that lists their likely current bad practices (pen-and-paper, Word docs, no cloud backup) and the liability exposure of a single lost/mishandled incident record, ending with a clear call to action (phone/QR code/website). Most recipients won't respond immediately — the follow-up plan is to cold-call/repeatedly call in the following 2-4 weeks, explicitly referencing and apologizing for the letter, to convert the "planted" fear into a sales conversation. Rationale: manufacture urgency/timing around a problem the prospect didn't previously think about.

## Seasonal/cultural home decorating service — Ramadan, Diwali, Lunar New Year interior decor (caller "Raj")
- Idea: an interior decorating service targeting culturally specific holidays (Ramadan, Lunar New Year) as a sequential ~2-quarter seasonal business, alongside existing Christmas-lights-style seasonal decor businesses (comp cited: people doing exterior Christmas lights can earn ~$200K/year).
- Target demographic cited: fast-growing (~5%/year), higher-income (~$100K average) Asian-American population in the caller's DFW-area market (Irving specifically mentioned as dense with potential customers).
- Chris's core objection: this is an **unvalidated, invented demand** — people currently decorate for these holidays themselves; there's no evidence anyone is currently *paying* for this service. His rule of thumb: only invent a brand-new, unproven category of demand when going for a massive venture-scale swing (e.g., Uber-style); for a smaller bootstrapped business, it's much safer to serve existing proven demand. Something people already *do* is not the same as something people will *pay* for.
- Validation method prescribed: directly ask prospective customers three questions in sequence and don't skip the third — (1) would you pay for this, (2) how much would you pay, and (3) **will you pay me right now** — because people (especially friends/family) will say yes to the first two questions out of politeness but balk when actually asked to commit money on the spot; only the third question reveals true intent.
- On Christmas lights specifically as a comparison: Chris is lukewarm on it because making real money in that niche requires securing a **municipal contract**, which takes as much time/effort/relationship-building as landing a contract for a **year-round** (non-seasonal) service — so if you're going to do that work, better to do it for a recurring, non-seasonal offering unless you can find an underserved market with low existing competition for Christmas lights specifically.

## Mobile Tesla wheel repair / detailing business (caller "Rick")
- Comp business observed: an independent operator in the St. Louis metro area does mobile wheel/rim repair (fixing curb rash, cosmetic damage) at customers' homes/workplaces, in ~1-1.5 hours, average ticket ~$300; he is booked "24/7," can't hire/retain help, and has effectively given up trying to scale beyond himself.
- Demand drivers cited: people keeping cars longer (12-15 years) amid high new-car prices/interest rates; Teslas specifically ship with black-painted rims that show curb rash immediately, and the Tesla owner community is described as unusually brand-loyal/engaged ("rabid"), generating strong organic word-of-mouth (this operator's main lead source: a local Tesla Owners Club Facebook group).
- Secondary revenue path for this operator: relationships with used-car dealers who pay a smaller amount to cosmetically refresh trade-in vehicles (curb rash, minor rim damage) when he has slow periods; but most of his business/best margin comes from individual private owners found organically.
- Chris's take: strong endorsement — recommends starting niched specifically to Tesla (e.g., a name like "Bolt Wheel Repair") to ride the fan-base's organic virality, then quietly expanding to all vehicles later by just changing site copy (brand name doesn't need to change). Notes this space is comparable to how pressure washing became a recognized service category, and floats it may still be under-exploited ("the secret's out" but supply hasn't caught up).
- Related unbuilt idea floated in the same call: a mobile car-washing service specifically targeting Tesla owners waiting at Supercharger stations (typically 20-35+ minute charging sessions) — pitch: "I'll wash your car for $25 while you charge," using a two-bucket rinseless wash system (e.g., "Optimum No Rinse") since many Tesla/EV owners avoid automatic car washes out of fear of damaging the vehicle, meaning cars sit dirty longer than owners would like; estimated one worker could realistically wash up to ~10 cars/day at a single Supercharger location before facing any pushback from property management. Also flagged that mobile tire replacement/repair is a separately growing, increasingly franchised (van-based) category that a wheel-repair operator could expand into once an established customer base exists.

## Caveats across the episode
- Most of these are single-caller anecdotes/plans, not case studies with confirmed outcomes — pricing models (e.g., the cold-email agency's $500+$300 structure, the seasonal decor idea) were explicitly untested at time of recording.
- Chris repeatedly stresses validating actual willingness-to-pay (not just interest) before building/investing further, and flags SEO/organic strategies as slow (3-9+ months) in competitive niches like roofing.
