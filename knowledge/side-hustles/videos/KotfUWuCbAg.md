# How to Start My Favorite Car Business Idea for $0
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KotfUWuCbAg | Published: 2024-09-27

Podcast-style brainstorm (Chris Kerner + co-host Nick, "Hold Cob Bros" segment) covering a pricing-transparency platform idea, a car-buying concierge idea (the episode's headline idea), an AI-education course idea, and a "buy distressed businesses" idea.

**1. Pricing-transparency platform ("Glassdoor for [X] industry")**
- Premise (user-submitted idea, credited to a viewer): opaque, wildly inconsistent pricing in industries like custom home building (examples cited: $400/sq ft in California vs. $98/sq ft in the Southeast, neither including land) and healthcare (e.g. kidney-donation surgery cost varies hugely by hospital with no comparison tool). A Glassdoor-style site where users anonymously submit real prices paid, by market/region, would create pricing transparency and let buyers avoid overpaying due to information asymmetry.
- Monetization discussed: sell ads to businesses in that vertical, or sell buyer leads.
- Content-sourcing/moat strategy discussed: since user-submitted content is the hard part, seed the database using a virtual assistant (VA) to cold-call every home builder listed (phone numbers often ring the owner directly at small builders) posing as a potential customer, asking "what are you charging per square foot right now," and logging price ranges/inclusions into a database (compared to Plaid's strategy of manually building tedious, hard-to-replicate API integrations as a moat). Alternative: have a VA post the same question in many local Facebook groups.

**2. Car-buying concierge / negotiation-as-a-service (the "favorite" idea, main focus)**
- Core insight: buying a car (or getting quotes for a custom-build home, described as the same category of problem) is a high-friction, information-asymmetric, high-ticket purchase people hate doing themselves.
- Proposed service: hire a cheap VA (~$6/hour, "good VA with a good accent," acting purely as an inbound-style caller, not a salesperson) to call ~15 dealerships for a specific car (their example: Toyota Sienna), ask detailed questions (condition, smoking history, features, price), compile results into a spreadsheet for the customer, then have the VA call back the top ~3-5 dealerships to negotiate against each other by name ("this dealership has this car for $3,000 less, what can you do?"). Customer's only remaining task: drive to the winning dealership and buy at the pre-negotiated price.
- Estimated time/cost: ~4 hours of VA labor (research + negotiation) at ~$6/hr = ~$25 in labor cost, for research+negotiation on a car (example priced around $30,000).
- **Proposed pricing**: flat fee (not commission on savings, since a completed sale isn't guaranteed) -- floated figures were ~$50-$500 flat fee, or alternatively ~25% of the amount saved off list price if the customer does buy (structured with $0 upfront). They favor a flat fee over a pure percentage-of-savings deal because there's no guarantee the customer follows through and buys.
- **Differentiator vs. existing options** (TrueCar, Edmunds, CarMax -- described as "no-haggle" fixed-price/margin models): this service does active negotiation on the buyer's behalf, and importantly the buyer never has to give their own phone number/email to dealerships (avoids the well-known problem of being bombarded with follow-up calls/texts for months) -- the VA uses a separate Google Voice number local to the buyer's area.
- Automation potential noted: much of the initial back-and-forth (checking availability, price) could be automated via AI email/chat exchanges (e.g. Zapier integrations) with a human VA only stepping in by phone once the list is narrowed to the final few cars.
- Framed as generalizable beyond cars to any high-ticket, opaque-pricing purchase requiring negotiation.
- Tangential "revenge" bit (not a business, an aside): if scammed by someone on Craigslist, submit their phone number to LendingTree.com or post a fake "free Golden Retriever puppies" ad on Facebook Marketplace with the scammer's number/address to generate spam calls to them.

**3. AI-education mini-course for business owners**
- Premise: AI-automation agencies face an "education gap" -- business owners don't understand basic AI concepts (LLMs vs. diffusion models vs. agents) well enough to even engage with an automation service.
- Proposed product: a 5-part video course (3-5 minutes per video, ~15-25 minutes total, written at a 5th-grade reading level) teaching: (1) what AI is, (2) types of AI tools (chatbots, LLMs, autonomous agents), (3) how to identify your tech stack and export your business data, (4) how to identify AI-automation opportunities in your business, (5) how to assess sophistication needed (from using ChatGPT directly to building autonomous agents).
- Production approach: record as a talking-head video, overlay with animations (via an animator or AI animation tools); estimated cost $500-$1,000 for a modest version, up to $2,000-$5,000 for high-end animation.
- Given prompt for generating the course content via ChatGPT (paraphrased/complete in transcript) -- host reports the AI output was specific enough to reference exact tools/websites.
- **Pricing/monetization plan**: price at or near your Facebook-ad cost-per-acquisition (CPA) so the course's price offsets ad spend, effectively making customer acquisition ~free; suggested example: start at $20, test CPA (e.g. $40), then raise/lower price to converge CPA near the sale price. Explicitly pitched as a **lead magnet** (a paid one, described as "strategically introduced friction") for a higher-ticket AI consulting offer: after buyers take the course, use automated check-ins ("did you implement anything?") to get them on a call and sell $3,000/month, one-hour-a-week AI consulting -- projected as ~10 clients = $30,000/month for 5-10 hours/week of consulting work.
- Can be niched by industry (e.g., "make it for plumbers" -- separate ad campaigns/course versions per vertical).
- Side commentary on prompting technique: recommends the "meta-prompt" trick -- ask ChatGPT "here's my goal and my content, how should I ask you this?" before asking the real question, and providing rich context (not one-line questions) for better answers.

**4. Buying distressed/closing businesses cheaply**
- Anecdote: a guest (Katherine, "Nikonomics" podcast) built an e-commerce productivity-tools brand (bestself.co -- journals, checklists) to ~$8.5M/year revenue, sold it in 2021 to a private equity fund; the PE fund mismanaged/pivoted it and shut it down by early 2023, offering to sell it back to her (for a price she didn't disclose, guessed at "a couple hundred thousand," paid largely via inventory value); post-buyback revenue settled around $5M but is described as far more profitable than at its $8.5M peak.
- Idea sparked: systematically find and acquire distressed/overlooked businesses "for pennies on the dollar" (bankruptcies, retiring owners, forced sales).
- **Sourcing tactics discussed**:
  - Outreach to CPAs and tax-prep firms asking to be introduced to their "ugliest," most-troubled client who's a viable business (e.g. specifying revenue >$2M, has a track record, hit temporary trouble) -- explicitly qualify out tiny businesses.
  - Target businesses that closed without selling (no buyer found) specifically for their **customer lists**, which retain value even with no other assets -- outreach to CPAs asking about clients who closed in the last 5 years, or responding to "business closing" posts (their example: someone giving away free wood because their father's cabinetry business was closing due to health issues -- cabinetry businesses can scale to 8 figures at ~30% margins). Approach: acquire (or get for free) the customer list, then approach a competing business in the same trade and sell them access to "call these orphaned customers as if you're the closed business's continuation," taking a 10-20% cut of any resulting revenue.
  - Proposed marketing channel: a "We Buy Ugly Businesses" campaign explicitly modeled on the "We Buy Ugly Houses" wholesaling/bandit-sign model (a known, decades-old, franchised marketing approach). Back-of-envelope market-sizing argument for why this addressable market could be larger than the ugly-houses model: ~32 million business owners among ~330 million Americans (~10% of the population, ~20% of adults) is a bigger base than the estimated ~10% of houses that are "ugly" targets. Suggested targeting by zip code income data (e.g., zip codes with $75K+ average earnings) as a proxy for business-owner density.
  - Monetization/exit for leads generated this way: rather than operating acquired businesses directly, resell each qualified lead to relevant buyers -- e.g., a cabinetry business's customer list sold to a competitor, or a HVAC business with $1M+ EBITDA referred to a business broker for a cut (25% of the broker's commission) -- effectively acting as a business-for-sale lead brokerage.

No specific validated revenue numbers are given for the car-concierge, AI-course, or ugly-business ideas -- these are stated as live brainstorms/untested hypotheses, explicitly flagged by the hosts as needing real-world testing ("we'll have to see").
