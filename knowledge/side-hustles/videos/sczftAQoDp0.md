# Why Hasn't Anyone Copied This Business Idea Yet?
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sczftAQoDp0 | Published: 2025-03-31

Brainstorm episode (Chris Koerner + Nik/Cofounders) riffing on middleman/no-inventory business models. Mostly raw ideas, not tested businesses — treat as idea seeds, not proven playbooks.

## Idea: "Never Touch Plastic" niche + marketplace
- Premise: combine two trends (sourdough baking + microplastics anxiety) into a product angle.
- Product 1: ultra-premium sourdough starter marketed as never having touched plastic. Cost to produce is very low (starter dried into powder, sold in small quantities), margin comes from premium fear-based positioning ("your grandkids will be eating your microplastics").
- Product 2 (bigger idea): turn "Never Touch Plastic" into a marketplace/Etsy-style platform for plastic-free products across niches (food, clothing, etc.), or a community (Reddit-style) around the theme.
- Tools named: ShareTribe — lets you launch your own marketplace (like your own eBay) with no coding, cited at ~$100/month.
- Monetization add-on: certification program — brands pay the site owner to send products to third-party labs that test/measure microplastic content (parts-per-million), then display a "certified" badge.
- Simpler alternative version: skip the marketplace/community entirely — build an affiliate/programmatic-SEO landing page aggregating plastic-free products (e.g., Berkey water filters) with affiliate links; use AI "deep research" to compile the list of qualifying products, then automate outreach to those brands to secure affiliate links. Estimated ~6 months to a year to build meaningful passive income this way.

## Idea: Piggybacking on USPS's subsidized rates
- Core insight: USPS is government-subsidized and non-profitable, making it artificially cheap vs. private carriers — a "free" marketing channel most founders overlook.
- Cost comparison cited: FedEx ~$1+/postcard vs. USPS ~$0.33-0.36/postcard at retail, dropping to ~$0.20-0.29/postcard with a bulk mail permit.
- Worked CAC example: sending 10,000 postcards via FedEx (~$10,000 total) vs. USPS (~$300 total) at a 1% conversion rate (10 customers) with $100 LTV per customer: FedEx CAC = $1,000/customer (LTV:CAC = 0.1, unviable); USPS CAC = $30/customer (LTV:CAC = 3.33, healthy — target ratio is 3+).
- Regulatory point: physical mail has far fewer anti-spam restrictions than email (no opt-out/CAN-SPAM-style constraints), so it's a comparatively "unlimited" outbound channel.
- Referenced prior venture "Chunky Mailers" (now operating as "Jumbo Mail Marketing"): outsourced direct-mail service for small/medium businesses that puts a flyer/ad plus a small physical item (e.g., a disposable toothbrush) inside a padded envelope, on the thesis that people throw away flat mail/postcards but not packages with something inside, increasing open rates.

## Idea: Monetizing postal carriers / "driving for dollars" as a data/lead-gen layer
- "Driving for dollars" (real estate wholesaling technique): drive neighborhoods, spot distressed/ugly houses, look up tax records, contact the owner about buying off-market.
- Scaling tactic described: previously paid people (sourced via Craigslist) per photographed distressed house; there are apps that geotag a photo to the house's address automatically. Approach USPS mail carriers directly at the post office before their routes start, offer ~$10 per qualifying house photo texted in, since carriers already see every house on their route daily.
- Bigger conceptual idea (unbuilt): sell access to postal carriers' route knowledge/observations as a data product — e.g., surveys or sentiment data (foreclosures spotted, unkempt yards, houses for sale) — riffed on as a "government revenue-generation" idea, not an existing business.

## Idea: AI-generated synthetic survey data (data/market-research angle)
- Observation: survey panel data (e.g., from a company like Qualtrics) suffers from biased/self-selected respondent pools and financially-incentivized dishonest answers.
- Pitch: aggregate existing survey data, feed it into an AI model, and generate synthetic survey responses for a given demographic profile ("36-year-old in Omaha making $74K, Christian, Republican, 3 kids...") with claimed ~80% reasonable accuracy, sold at a large discount to what incumbents like Qualtrics charge (framed as "90% of the features for 90% less cost").
- Adjacent (more speculative, not really an actionable business as stated): consumers opting their personal "AI agent" into being paid for its owner's data/survey answers instead of being paid directly.

## Idea: Piggybacking dialysis clinics for premium plasma transfusions (longevity market)
- Premise: plasma transfusion/plasma exchange is trending in the longevity space (cites Bryan Johnson swapping plasma with his son); science on efficacy is contested/unproven, discussed as a business angle regardless of scientific validity.
- Pitch: dialysis clinics already own the necessary equipment (centrifuge-type separation) and clinical staff, and are often not running at full capacity — approach an existing licensed dialysis clinic to offer premium (cash-pay) plasma transfusion sessions using slack capacity/extended hours, rather than building new licensed infrastructure.
- Go-to-market/testing suggestion: generate organic PR around the polarizing/controversial angle to gauge demand; target customer acquisition through existing high-end med spas — specifically medspas' top ~20% of customers (cash-pay regulars, e.g., people already on TRT/hormone therapy) — offering pre-sale of a scheduled mobile "plasma transfusion day" (e.g., a mobile dialysis unit visiting the medspa for a block of hours), with an early-booking discount (30% mentioned) to test demand.
- Suggested business model: pure middleman/affiliate — do not get licensed or hold any medical liability yourself; broker the connection between a licensed dialysis provider and a medspa/sauna-cold-plunge retailer's customer list, taking an affiliate/referral fee per booking, leaving licensing, staffing, and fulfillment entirely to the dialysis provider.
- Other suggested customer acquisition channels for this same "premium plasma" pitch: high-end sauna and cold-plunge equipment distributors' customer lists (people who spend $10K-$50K+ on a sauna are framed as likely candidates), and endurance cyclists (referencing Lance Armstrong-style blood doping, framed as legal for non-competitive athletes).

## Caveats raised
- Plasma transfusion's anti-aging benefit is explicitly noted as scientifically unproven/contested ("some science says it helps you live longer, some says not really").
- Direct-to-consumer healthcare services (like plasma transfusion) carry real licensure, insurance, and liability burden if you operate them yourself — the hosts explicitly frame this as a reason to stay a middleman/broker rather than the service provider.
- Most of these are unlaunched brainstormed ideas, not case studies with real revenue numbers (unlike other Koerner Office episodes) — no one in this episode reports having built or profited from these specific concepts.
