# How to Make $15k/M on FB Marketplace Selling This ONE Item
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4NJHK5KdASc | Published: 2025-11-24

Follow-up interview with Alex Nelson ("The Shelf Dude," shelfdude.com), who builds and sells custom garage shelving for stacked storage totes, sold exclusively via Facebook Marketplace. Original interview was episode 55 (Aug 2024).

## The product/business
- Product: custom wood shelving units sized specifically to hold stacked plastic storage totes (the common black-and-yellow big-box-store totes), so each tote can be individually pulled out instead of unstacking a whole pile.
- Origin story: built the first one for his own garage (idea seen via an Instagram post his wife forwarded, of a similar product used for something else); started selling to fund an MBA program.
- Grew from evenings/weekends (borrowing a friend's truck for deliveries) to full-time by May 2024, working 8 hours/day, once volume justified quitting his day job. Previously reported net profit ~$15,000/month (from the original Aug 2024 episode, cited again here).

## Pricing and unit economics
- Standard pricing: $40 per tote-space (i.e., a shelf sized for 20 totes = $800; wood/wheels/structure only, totes sold separately by the customer).
- Price is intentionally varied across different listings for the same core product to test price sensitivity — e.g., listing the same shelf at $40, $32, or $25 per tote space simultaneously to see which converts, rather than assuming one fixed price is correct. Reports some customers say a price feels too cheap and would pay more; framed as "value is in the eye of the beholder."
- On a ~$1,000 shelf (via the installer network's larger sizing, e.g. 5x5), estimated total expenses are ~$150, implying a large gross margin.
- Installer/referral network economics: 70/30 revenue split with independent installers who fulfill leads Alex forwards from other markets — installer keeps 70% (e.g., $700 of a $1,000 job), Alex keeps 30% ($300). On the installer's $700 take with ~$150 in materials cost, that's roughly 80% margin for the installer before accounting for their own labor/time.
- Historical volume: roughly 1,000 shelves delivered lifetime at time of this recording.

## Facebook Marketplace tactics
- **Listing volume/quantity is the core growth lever**: listings are free, so "make as many as you can" — vary wording/photos and let repetition/A-B testing surface which listing performs best, then double down on the winner. Use ChatGPT to help generate listing variation ideas.
- **Photos over video** drove his early growth — specifically before/after shots (cluttered stacked-tote garage vs. the finished shelf setup); he was not doing video content in the early days despite being active on Instagram Reels elsewhere.
- **Follow-up messaging discipline**: constant manual follow-up via Facebook Messenger to leads ("Is this still available?" / "Are you still interested?") repeated over time — described as a major, ongoing time investment, especially in the early days.
- **Boosting posts**: originally tried boosting and saw little benefit — theorized that Facebook's boost mostly amplifies a post to your existing friend network rather than reaching new/external people, unlike running an actual paid ad which reaches outside your network. Host counters with his own recent example (an appliance-rental Marketplace test where $20 of ad spend turned ~2 organic inquiries into ~30 inquiries) and argues boosting is likely more effective now than when Alex started, given more competitors have entered the space — boosting can push a listing higher in search results in a crowded market and can support holding a higher price point (more eyeballs = ability to charge more) even as competitors undercut on price.
- **In-person closing has a very high close rate**: Alex reports "100%" close rate on in-home measuring visits ("Can you come measure for me?" leads directly to a sale) — attributed to being physically present in the customer's space and building trust/connection, though he notes it's hard to get a customer's phone number to set up that visit via cold Marketplace messaging alone.
- Sales-psychology tip (from the host, referencing a separate land-clearing business contact "Alex" who clears rural land): explicitly paint the emotional "after" picture for the customer (how relieved/happy they'll feel once done) rather than just quoting price/specs — reported to increase close rates once the operator started doing this deliberately.

## Business evolution / diversification
- Digital plans: sells downloadable PDF building plans for people who want to build the shelves themselves (originally self-made PDFs, since upgraded via a hired plans designer).
- **Installer network ("Shelf Dude Pro")**: because the core shelf product is a one-time, non-recurring purchase (durable, doesn't wear out, moves with the homeowner), Alex built a network of independent installers in other geographic markets who receive forwarded leads on a 70/30 split. Two tiers: a free tier (occasional lead-sharing) and a paid tier (greater visibility on the shelfdude.com site — name, location, service territory, "exclusivity," plus a hat/shirt).
- **Educational course**: an 8-module paid course teaching the entire playbook (product build, Facebook Marketplace sales tactics, etc.) for people wanting to replicate the business from scratch.
- Considering: a standalone garage-organization/cleanup service (separate from shelf-building, would require hiring employees since labor-per-job varies more); a book about why people get disorganized; new product variations (e.g., a bed frame designed to hold totes underneath, made for his daughter, not yet listed for sale).
- Untapped idea discussed but not yet implemented by Alex: build a customer database (~1,000 past shelf customers) and upsell ~10% into a recurring annual "spring cleaning"/garage organization service (~$600/year each ≈ $60,000/year in mostly-labor recurring revenue), and separately build a referral network by paying *competing* Facebook Marketplace sellers (e.g., other shelf-builders) a flat referral fee (example: $100/lead) for garage-cleanup leads they don't want to fulfill themselves — effectively monetizing rather than competing with rivals.

## General "sell anything on Facebook Marketplace" advice
- Core mindset: just try it — post real photos of what you're selling and iterate based on feedback (price objections, requested variants) rather than being afraid to start. Framed most people's failure to try as fear-driven, not opportunity-driven.
- Marketplace listings cost nothing (absent paid boosting), so volume and iteration are close to free — "cast a big net" of listing variations, then concentrate on whichever performs.
- Selling *services* on Facebook Marketplace works despite Facebook's general lean toward physical goods — same iterative posting approach applies.
- General resale/arbitrage opportunity noted while browsing: Marketplace surfaces highly random, low-competition items (free items to flip, unusual crafts, homemade food, a tiny-home trailer at $9,500 vs. $80,000 for a finished tiny home) — hosts describe a broader "thrift store flipping" pattern (people lining up at thrift/donation stores like Deseret Industries to buy inventory to resell on Marketplace) as a viable, low-barrier side hustle model in general, though it requires the mindset/habit of actively looking for arbitrage opportunities.
- Caution raised on hand-made craft items sold cheaply on Marketplace: host does quick napkin math on a $30 handmade item that took 2 hours to make (~$15/hour) and notes that's below minimum retail wage (~$17/hour cited for Walmart) — implying sellers should account for their time cost and look for ways to produce faster or charge more, not just any sale.
