# How to Passively Earn $8k/year from Buying a $16k Book Royalty
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Wm3_z9ylGpo | Published: 2024-06-09

- Idea: buy book and music royalty rights (fractional intellectual-property income streams) as a passive investment asset class, from a guest (Nick) who does this personally and via a small friends-and-family fund.
- Guest's track record: ~10 deals done personally, plus ~6-7 through a friends-and-family fund. Typical check size per deal: $20,000 to $200,000+.
- Investment thesis for royalties vs. stocks/real estate:
  - Returns are largely uncorrelated to the broader market (people cancel gym memberships in a downturn before canceling a $10/month Spotify subscription).
  - Legal protection: copyright income runs for the life of the author/artist plus 70 years.
  - Some favorable tax treatment mentioned (not detailed).
  - Industry tailwind: growing pressure/support for artists/creators getting paid directly rather than large rights-holding companies capturing the value.
  - Risk/return profile compared to a corporate bond: not very volatile.
- Yield numbers: blended portfolio yield roughly ~12%/year (low double digits), described as fairly stable with slight growth. No outright losers yet in the portfolio — worst performers have been flat to slightly growing, not negative.
- Headline deal (best performer): bought a 7.5% net-income interest in the first six books of a bestselling crime-fiction series (author identified as C.J. Box; the "Joe Pickett" series, which spawned TV adaptations including "Big Sky") off Craigslist in ~2016 for $35,000.
  - Seller was the original literary agent (not the author) who held that 7.5% stake from being first agent; he was moving to Alaska (no mail service there) and needed to liquidate/get a check rather than deal with royalty payments.
  - At purchase, the royalty was paying ~$122,000/year to the whole interest holder pool, meaning the buyer's cut represented roughly a 3x purchase multiple (a very cheap price relative to income).
  - Currently pays the buyer ~$16,000/year (paid out twice a year), up from ~$12,000/year at time of purchase — attributed to the ongoing series' popularity (new annual bestseller entries) plus a TV series adaptation boosting visibility across the franchise.
- Sourcing method for that deal: posted a "wanted" ad on Craigslist for oil/gas and music royalties; a seller responded directly. Used Upwork-sourced attorneys for legal/due-diligence help since the buyer had no prior experience with this asset class. Received ~120 pounds of paper documentation for due diligence.
- Typical pricing in the market today: attractively priced deals go for roughly 5-7x multiple of annual income; high-quality/desirable catalogs trade closer to 8-10x multiple. The 3x Craigslist deal was called an outlier/lucky find, not representative.
- Where to source deals: Royalty Exchange (described as "an eBay for music/book royalties") has significant deal flow but tends to be pricier since it's a public marketplace; the guest's fund primarily sources off-market deals through brokers to get better pricing. Royalty Exchange itself is described as having a lot of "junk" — need due diligence to separate stable, recurring-revenue assets (e.g., a song that's been in steady Spotify playlist rotation) from decaying/one-hit assets.
- Due diligence framework mentioned: assess whether income is durable recurring revenue (e.g., steady Spotify streaming) versus one-time spikes; look at "decay curve" of the asset's revenue trend.
- Concept referenced: the "Lindy effect" — the longer something has already existed, the longer it's statistically likely to keep existing/generating revenue. Applied as an investment heuristic: buying a long-established catalog (e.g., "Bob Dylan") is a safer, lower-return bet; buying a brand-new viral artist's catalog is a higher-risk, higher-potential-return gamble.
- Market inefficiency / friction noted: transferring fractional royalty ownership (e.g., a partial interest in a song's producer or writer share) is a slow, manual, multi-month administrative process because these rights weren't originally designed to change hands (has to go through rights administrators/labels like Warner). This friction is cited as the reason more efficient marketplaces/exchanges/crowd-investing platforms for royalties haven't fully succeeded yet — deal flow and back-office transfer complexity, not lack of investor interest, is the bottleneck. Guest says if he built a company in this space he would raise capital and run it as a fund model rather than try to build an exchange.
- On funds/strategies that pay premium multiples (15-16x) to acquire rights with the intent of actively promoting/re-marketing the asset to grow its income: guest is skeptical this "active promotion" strategy has worked out as well as those funds expected — implies overpaying relative to the actual achievable lift from promotion, especially at scale (limited team bandwidth to meaningfully promote many properties at once).
- AI angle discussed (not a specific side-hustle recommendation, more speculative commentary): AI music generation tools (e.g., "Suno," referred to phonetically as "sunno" in transcript) let anyone legally create and upload AI-generated songs to Spotify and monetize them, but both guest and host believe current AI-generated music quality is well below professionally produced music and expect a long runway before it's competitive — compared to the "last 5% of self-driving car disruption taking as long as the first 95%." A tangential idea floated: using a custom AI-generated song (e.g., via Suno) as a novelty/attention-grabbing hook in place of a normal cold-email subject line, not necessarily to sell the song itself but to stand out — potentially effective especially with recipients unfamiliar with AI music tools (e.g., older demographics).
- Caveats: this asset class requires meaningful capital per deal ($20K-$200K+ cited), non-trivial due diligence/legal review, and off-market sourcing relationships to get good pricing; publicly listed marketplace deals (Royalty Exchange) are typically already priced up. No formalized/streamlined retail on-ramp exists yet due to transfer-of-rights administrative friction.
