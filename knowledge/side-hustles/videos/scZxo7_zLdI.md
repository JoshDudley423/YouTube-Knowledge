# This $15K/Person Grant Feels Like a Cheat Code
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=scZxo7_zLdI | Published: 2025-08-13

Interview with "Hector," who builds/scales trade schools funded by government workforce grants (WIOA-type grants referenced as "WA").

## The core idea: government-funded trade schools
- States pay trade schools $5,000–$15,000 per enrolled student (varies by state/region/trade), covering 100% of tuition for the student. Student pays nothing; government reimburses the school.
- Grant targets laid-off workers, people on food stamps/SNAP, justice-system-impacted individuals, warehouse/retail/fast-food workers — the goal is moving them off government benefits into livable-wage trades so they pay taxes instead.
- Region-by-region, even within the same state, payout varies. Example given: central Texas maxes at $7,500/student; other Texas/Arizona regions go up to $15,000; grant amount also depends on how in-demand the trade is.
- Regional trade demand varies: Houston/Galveston = marine (underwater welding, boat repair); West Texas = oil/gas trucking, wind turbine tech, CDL.

## Business model / unit economics
- **White-labeling courses**: buy pre-built curriculum from another school for ~$800–$1,000 (or cheaper per-course, e.g. $500), slap your school's branding on it, keep the rest of the grant payout as margin.
- Example: buy a course for $500, charge the general public $3,000 — "still big margins," and "you sell one a week, that replaces somebody's income."
- Hardware cost is minimal: VR training goggles ~$200–$300, or just a laptop (some nonprofit students didn't even have laptops — giving away free laptops boosted enrollment/login rates significantly).
- Nursing school case study (Georgia): owner was undercharging at $2,000/student flat fee, doing ~10-12 students/month (~$20-25K/month). After repricing to bundle courses + job placement guarantee and using the grant, priced at $9,999 and volume held — revenue went from ~$20-25K/month to ~$150K/month.
- IT school case study (Arizona): owner created his own IT course (no white-label cost, near-zero marginal cost to resell), charges students $5,000–$10,000, AND runs a staffing arm (owned by his wife) placing grads at companies like Microsoft, T-Mobile, Verizon, collecting $7,500–$15,000 per placement from employers/grants. ~$2.5-2.8M/year on the school side, ~$1.5M/year on placement — ~$5M/year combined, run by a 5-person team (4 offshore).
- Founder's own first school (online-only, Texas): scaled to $2.4M in year one on grants alone, with $0 spent on marketing (grassroots outreach via churches and nonprofits instead).
- Adult daycare case study: business owner had 30 CNAs, only 5 certified; grant pays ~$7,500 per staff member certified. Standing up an internal school to certify his own existing employees was projected to add ~$300K/year in revenue while solving his own staffing/turnover pain point (CNAs paid $16-18/hr with turnover due to grueling work).
- Separate "employer wage subsidy" grant (distinct from the training grant): if you hire a graduate of one of these grant-funded programs, workforce offices will often pay the employer up to 100% of the new hire's training wages (up to ~$20/hr, cited as up to "$300 an hour" total in one aggressive framing) for a trial/"test drive" period — worst case 50% of wages reimbursed. Purpose: let employers try out workers risk-free; if it doesn't work out, employer still got free labor and worker gets resume experience.
- Cold-calling test: founder cold-called 10 HVAC companies offering this free-labor program; 7 never called back, the 3 who answered declined ("we're fine"), despite it being literally free certified labor with workers' comp included. Awareness of this program among small business owners is effectively zero.

## Process / how to start
1. Find a white-label course provider in your target trade (or create your own if you have expertise).
2. Register as a training provider with your state/regional workforce development office (this is the "become a training provider" registration step).
3. Line up referral partners: churches, workforce development centers, chambers of commerce, nonprofits, women's shelters — anyone in regular contact with people who'd want free trade education. Community/nonprofit partnerships were the founder's #1 free acquisition channel.
4. Build sales infrastructure: CRM, sales scripts, funnels, email campaigns — most existing trade-school owners are tradespeople, not business/sales people, and lose enrollments simply because they don't capture leads or follow up.
5. Existing business owners: can "bolt on" a school to an existing operation to (a) certify/train current uncertified staff for grant reimbursement, (b) get paid to train excess hires and then place ("farm out") the surplus to competitors or other employers, and (c) add "we have our own training pipeline" as an M&A value-add when eventually selling the business.
6. In slow/restrictive states, you can still start selling courses to the general public (cash, Buy-Now-Pay-Later via Stripe, or payment plans) while waiting for state grant approval, then flip to grant-funded once approved.

## High-demand trades/niches named
Nursing/medical assisting, phlebotomy, CDL/truck driving, HVAC, electrical, plumbing, pest control technician, solar technician, forklift repair technician, pool technician, tree technician, dog grooming (qualifies as a "vet technician" adjacent credential — example operator charges $250/trim for doodles, booked 6-8 weeks out), CNA/certified nursing assistant, home health aide, IT help desk, IT tech sales (enterprise sales roles at companies like T-Mobile), telematics technician (GPS/fleet tech installation), garage door repair technician, flight attendant school, window tech, blue-collar staffing/placement. General rule: "anything with the word technician in it" tends to be underserved and well-paid but not commercialized/marketed.

## State difficulty tiers (for opening a school)
- Easy/fast approval: Texas, Arizona, Montana, Arkansas, Missouri.
- Hard/slow (politics, can take ~2 years to get first enrollment approved): Florida, California, New York, Washington.
- Others: moderate, roughly 6-month wait.
- Contrarian angle raised: hard states = fewer competitors, so it may be worth "grinding for 2 years" in a slow state because once approved there's little competition.

## Delivery format
- Most trades can be taught online/remote via video + VR goggles for the classroom portion, with certification exams and a short in-person "externship" (e.g., ~2 weeks) for hands-on/needle-based tasks (phlebotomy, CNA procedures).
- Welding cited as a trade that hasn't been cracked for remote/hybrid delivery — must be in person.

## Caveats / risks
- Regulatory compliance matters: e.g., handling refrigerant (Freon) legally requires an EPA 608 certification; OSHA is increasingly checking job sites for proper credentials, and some commercial contracts require certified credentials.
- The grant system itself is described as bureaucratic and confusing by design — most individuals who walk into a workforce office and ask about the grant get handed ~18 forms and give up; navigating this bureaucracy (route to grant funding, paperwork, which office to talk to) is presented as the real "moat"/edge of this business, not the curriculum itself.
- Grant amounts and rules are set per-region/per-state and change based on local labor shortages — no single national number; must research locally.
- Payment risk in one case: the Georgia nursing school owner previously ran informal long-term payment plans with students expecting repayment once they got jobs; only ~50% actually paid back — cited as a reason to use the grant/upfront reimbursement model instead of trusting borrower repayment.
- Not every business/trade qualifies or has quick approval — some states/regions take years to get a first cohort approved.
