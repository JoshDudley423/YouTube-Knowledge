# Marcus Lemonis Opens Up: Depression, Regrets, & Losing Millions
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OwK2x62VwoQ | Published: 2025-11-27

Long-form personal interview with Marcus Lemonis (star of "The Profit," CEO of Camping World, chairman of Beyond/Bed Bath & Beyond). Mostly personal/psychological reflection, not a how-to guide, but contains several concrete pieces of business/investing philosophy and one specific current venture. Not a "side hustle idea" video in the usual sense — sparse on numbers.

## Deal-structuring philosophy (relevant to anyone taking on advisors/investors or mentoring others)
- Lemonis has put money/time into 100+ small businesses (mostly outside his "big business" stack); fewer than 10% "worked out" by his own account. Lesson: expect a very low hit rate when informally backing small businesses, even with deep expertise.
- Increasingly avoids taking formal equity in small businesses he helps, specifically to avoid K-1 tax complexity and ongoing entanglement — instead gives cash (e.g., "$55,000 to do these specific things") with no contract, framing it as "doing you a solid" rather than an investment, and lets the recipient decide whether/how to pay it back later (sometimes taking repayment with interest when offered, to reinforce accountability in the relationship, rather than declining and creating a lopsided dynamic).
- Signals he looks for before informally backing someone: they ask lots of questions, communicate proactively without being prompted, share information unprompted, and are unusually transparent (a sign of "nothing to hide").
- Structuring advice echoed by Koerner: when someone offers you significant equity for ongoing involvement you won't actually sustain, negotiate down both the equity/time commitment (e.g., counter 10 hrs/week for 10% equity with "1% equity for 30 minutes/week") and be explicit up front that your involvement will taper sharply after the initial period — most relationship breakdowns with informal mentors/investors stem from unstated expectations about ongoing time commitment.
- On business partnerships generally: keep as much distance as possible in formal partnerships (Lemonis has had ~15 business partners, many close friends/family, with mostly poor results) and generally avoid doing business with people you have personal relationships with.

## Advice for aspiring entrepreneurs (the closest thing to actionable "how to start" content)
- Pick one thing and start — avoid endless research/analysis paralysis ("dabbling, watching content, ChatGPT-ing it" instead of doing).
- Never risk your family's financial security on a first business — no 401k withdrawals, no second mortgages regardless of what a guru/book suggests. Start with something low-risk/low-capital (a "lemonade stand" equivalent) precisely because it's low-risk with real (if small) potential return — Lemonis's own first business was reselling candy wholesale through 7-Eleven as a kid.
- Recommended sequencing: build a first side hustle while keeping your main income, add a second and third side hustle, and only make a full leap to entrepreneurship once cumulative side-hustle cash flow supports it. Koerner calls this the "wing walker's code": never let go of one railing until you're already holding the next one, and make sure the next one is solid.
- On failure and public risk: Lemonis argues you should not self-regulate/hide your gifts out of social anxiety or fear of embarrassment — if you have a communication or business gift, "you have an obligation" to use it publicly rather than withhold it out of personal discomfort, though he distinguishes this from reckless self-endangerment.
- On operating philosophy: "the business is always more important than the business owner" — a founder's real success is measured by what happens to the business after they leave (culture, succession, whether it survives without them), not performance while they're personally in the seat. Also states employees matter more than customers in his internal philosophy ("if you treat your employees right, they'll treat your customers right") — though he clarifies front-line staff should still treat the customer as paramount in their own role.
- On self-awareness on camera/in public performance: excessive self-monitoring/self-consciousness kills authenticity and audience connection; making fun of yourself lowers audience expectations and builds goodwill/forgiveness.

## Current major venture referenced (not a side-hustle-scale idea, but a stated business direction)
- Lemonis is chairman of the company that owns Overstock.com, now rebranded as Bed Bath & Beyond (also owns Buy Buy Baby). Stated goal: build an "everything home" platform bundling fintech, insurance, home buying/selling, home insurance, home financing, home decorating, and lawn services under one roof, aimed at making homeownership less intimidating/more attainable.
- Wants to put home records (title deed, survey, mortgage, insurance, permits, full history) on blockchain so a home's full documented history/value is preserved and transferable — protecting owners against disaster (e.g., wildfire) or unexpected death, and making the asset more valuable/financeable at resale due to complete documentation. Undecided on which blockchain to use. Connected to his prior investment in tZERO (a tokenization/blockchain platform originally tied to Overstock founder Patrick Byrne, ICO around 2019).

## Caveats
- This episode is almost entirely a personal/psychological interview (depression, anxiety, faith, regrets about not having children, reflections on "The Profit") — treat the business content above as supplementary philosophy rather than a replicable side-hustle blueprint. No revenue numbers, costs, or step-by-step process for any specific side hustle are given.
- Lemonis explicitly frames business ownership as generally high-risk and low-probability of success ("likelihood of it working out is very low... like the warning label on a pack of cigarettes") — encourages self-belief and calculated risk, not blind optimism.
