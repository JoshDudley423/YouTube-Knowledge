# Everyone Chases Trends. Resell This Instead to Actually Make Money
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W5oUjuoHWhA | Published: 2025-09-10

Second interview with Shannon Jean (liquidation/reselling expert, shannonjean.com/mastermind) — a live tour of real liquidation auction listings (govdeals.com, gsaauctions.gov, Costco/Costco-adjacent wholesale auction sites) illustrating the "boring products" thesis with fresh examples and member success updates.

## Core repeated thesis: buy backward, and boring beats sexy
- Central rule: **know your resale price before you bid** — work backward from what you can actually sell an item for, not forward from the sticker/MSRP or a "deal" price. Never buy something without already knowing (via research/testing) what it will sell for.
- Boring/unglamorous categories consistently get less competitive bidding than "sexy" categories, producing steeper discounts — repeated principle from the earlier episode, reinforced with new live examples.
- MSRP/retail list price on a manifest is often inflated/unreliable ("the retail price is a lie") — when uncertain, share the manifest with someone knowledgeable in that specific product category (a personal contact/network) to sanity-check real value before bidding, rather than trusting the sheet.

## New example categories walked through live
- **Plumbing fixtures/toilets (Winston Water Cooler lot)**: a poorly-titled listing ("one pallet of plumbing fixtures") sold for $750 total for 10 high-end toilets (individually retailing $520-$823, e.g., a "Kohler" unit priced around $750 retail) — works out to ~$50/unit. Vague/boring titles are a buying signal (less competition), but the same logic means your own resale listings need detailed, keyword-rich titles/descriptions for SEO once you're the seller. Suggested buyer: house flippers, remodelers, contractors, or resell on Facebook Marketplace at half retail for a $3,000-$4,000 profit.
- **Pipe/valve fittings**: a lot with a stated distributor price of $27,775 (256 brand-new units) sold for $1,500.
- **Wooden train sets/toys (Hearth & Hand brand, via Target liquidation)**: 240 units, Target's own list price ~$130/unit (current sold comps on eBay ~$80-90), lot sold for $2,600 total (~$11/unit). Timed for the run-up to the holidays (deal seen in September, expecting fulfillment into October); confirmed via Google Trends that the brand's search interest was climbing seasonally.
- **Pool floats/hammocks ("Big Joe," via Costco liquidation)**: Costco retail ~$40/unit (14% margin per Costco's typical model), lot of 39 units with $1,560 total retail value sold for $429 (~$11/unit). Flagged as a small, non-life-changing deal but a good simple addition to Facebook Marketplace inventory; noted to buy geographically close to avoid high shipping costs eating the margin on lower-value bulky goods.
- **Furniture truckloads** (called out as a category doing especially well despite being physically harder to handle — needs help moving):
  - A Costco wholesale-auction truckload of upholstered furniture and dining sets (11 sets, customer returns graded "AB," Costco retail value $11,440) sold for $585 (~$53/unit average). Advice: prefer darker-colored return furniture over light colors since returns/customer damage shows less; check whether pallets are shrink-wrapped (protects/reveals condition) before bidding.
  - Walker Edison (an online furniture brand, previously discussed on the channel as an "online IKEA" competitor) had a 16-pallet (~250-unit) truckload of outdoor/patio furniture with a stated retail value of $123,824; even conservatively assuming only 50% of retail is realistically achievable (~$60,000) and further halving that resale estimate to ~$30,000 for a non-brand seller, the lot sold for $7,240 (all-in cost ~$10,000 including ~$3,000 shipping) — a plausible path to recouping the $10,000 investment and profiting.
  - General freight-cost principle: don't automatically reject a deal because shipping looks expensive — treat freight as part of cost of goods and evaluate the full math (e.g., would you spend $2,500 in shipping to net $5,500 profit? If yes, it's still a good deal).
- **Baby formula/Pedialyte (GSA-adjacent listing, Puerto Rico)**: 486 cans of Similac-brand instant formula, no bids, listed near a November 2025 expiration date, buyable for ~$500 total (~$1/unit) versus Facebook Marketplace comps of $9-$20/unit — suggested approach: list transparently with the expiration date disclosed and let the market self-select buyers comfortable with a soon-to-expire (but often still usable) date.

## Advanced/niche high-capital examples (illustrative of the category's breadth, not entry-level deals)
- **Tesla Supercharger station equipment** (6-unit lot via a company called Government Remarketing, sold via a GovDeals-adjacent auction, no reserve, opening bid $10,000; comparable new units found selling for ~$4,500 each retail): illustrates that ordinary Tesla destination/supercharger hardware occasionally hits the surplus market; site owners can potentially monetize by hosting chargers and marking up the power cost within Tesla's allowed limits, similar to a gas station model.
- **Full-motion flight simulators** (a Blackhawk helicopter military/civilian pilot training simulator, cost $18 million to build, sold via GSA auction at Fort Campbell, KY — was at $322,300 with 7 bidders and climbing under "popcorn bidding," where a late bid extends the auction rather than allowing last-second sniping): flagged as a potential attraction-business asset (compared to a real existing business, DriveATank.com, that lets customers drive and shoot tanks in Texas — one of few states where that's legal).
- **Military KC-10 aircraft** (originally $52 million aircraft, GSA auction, Arizona, reserve $500,000, some units non-flyable/non-FAA-compliant): floated as raw material for an aviation-themed Airbnb (especially units with wings already removed for ease of transport) or cargo-logistics repurposing.
- **Decommissioned government/municipal electric buses** (DC Department of Transportation circulator program shutdown; new-flyer diesel-electric hybrid buses, clear title, running condition, sold in the $2,500-$25,000 range despite the underlying federal program reportedly costing "hundreds of millions" to run before being discontinued): suggested resale angle — contact private limo/bus tour companies (which normally pay $90,000-$300,000+ for a comparable new bus) and offer access to these units at a steep discount; noted a secondary market of off-grid/DIY buyers who strip the large battery packs (likely worth far more than a $15,000 Tesla battery pack) for home power-wall systems, then scrap the shell.
- **Helicopters without engines/rotors**: regularly available via GSA-type sites for $1,000-$5,000 — suggested as static play/climbing structures for a kids' construction- or vehicle-themed activity business.
- **Government real estate** (realestatesales.gov / GSA real estate auctions): examples browsed included the Penfield Reef Lighthouse (Fairfield, CT — built 1874, 1,568 sq ft, private island/tower with dock, bidding at $370,000 with 27 bids after starting at $100,000, ending within 17 hours of the recording) — floated as an Airbnb play, citing comparable lighthouse Airbnb listings renting for $300-$600/night (meaning a ~$2,000/month mortgage on a $370,000 purchase could be covered by 4-5 bookings/month); a former Air Force Space Surveillance Station in Rhode Island (100 acres, bidding at $65,000) — pointed out that buyers might overlook the acreage value while focused on the unusual building; and a county jail annex listed for $1 million.

## Member success stories cited as proof of the model (Facebook Marketplace as the near-universal sales channel)
- A Sacramento-based mastermind member (referenced across both Shannon episodes, "Joe") who started with pressure washers: sold 600 units over ~10 months; more recently bought his first truckload of outdoor/patio furniture and recouped ~70% of that investment within 24 hours, hit full profitability within 72 hours, and sold out the entire load. Set a stated goal of $100,000 profit for the following year, scaling the same simple playbook (Facebook Marketplace only, no other sales channel).
- A separate example: sold furniture directly out of a residential driveway; struggled with initial garage-sale traffic until posting in local Facebook groups, after which a Labor Day driveway sale generated $2,000 in a single day.
- A beginner example: won a $500 auction bid, flipped two chairs alone for $750 each (Costco retail $1,150, general retail $1,500) — recovered the entire investment including shipping from the very first items sold.
- Common thread emphasized: sellers scale from onesie-twosie Facebook Marketplace flips → multi-pallet buys → truckloads → eventually a recognized local reputation/storefront, while capturing buyer contact info (email/phone) along the way to build a repeat-customer list for future liquidation drops — "you want to run it like a business" even at modest ($5,000/month) goals.
- Logistics note: a full semi-truckload generally cannot be delivered directly to a residential address — buyers typically need a freight forwarder to break the truckload into smaller pallet-sized deliveries.

## Platform notes reiterated
- **GovDeals.com**: privately-run platform through which state/local (not federal) government agencies (school districts, cities, police departments) liquidate surplus — police cruisers, fire trucks, ambulances, and buses appear regularly, often at steep discounts, since agencies mainly want disposal, not maximum price.
- **GSAAuctions.gov**: the actual federal government's surplus auction platform (General Services Administration) — used for federal assets specifically (military aircraft, real estate, large-scale specialized equipment). Distinctly different ownership/scope from GovDeals despite similar browsing experience.
- Sellers on these platforms are generally responsive to direct questions (phone numbers for the responsible government contact are often listed) — worth calling to clarify condition, specs, or correct listing errors (example: a supercharger lot mistakenly labeled "solar," corrected via direct contact with the seller).

## Caveats / risks raised
- Buyers instinctively invent reasons *not* to buy something (assuming it's defective, mislabeled, or too complicated) — Shannon's advice is to actually read the full listing details (title status, working condition, VIN numbers, etc.) rather than assume the worst, since many listings are in genuinely good working condition.
- Highly specialized/large assets (aircraft, superchargers, flight simulators) require real domain knowledge, storage/logistics capacity, and a credible resale channel — these are explicitly framed as advanced plays, not recommended starting points for a beginner reseller.
- Not every appealing "found" deal has an obvious monetization path (e.g., the Tesla Supercharger lot, the KC-10 aircraft) — Shannon acknowledges not always knowing exactly how he'd monetize something, framing it instead as evidence of the sheer breadth of opportunity in this space rather than a guaranteed playbook.
- Products near their expiration date (e.g., baby formula) carry real disclosure obligations and buyer skepticism to manage transparently, not conceal.
