# Laid Off 2 Days Before Christmas, Now He Makes $60k/Month Reselling
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uWjU8ctaHIc | Published: 2026-02-22

Interview with Jim Rowe (X: @resellerrowe), a liquidation/reselling entrepreneur in rural Ellensburg, WA (pop. ~25,000), who scaled from restaurant equipment reselling to a general liquidation retail store doing ~$60k/month.

## Core business: liquidation reselling / general resale storefront
- Model: buy liquidation pallets, restaurant equipment, auction lots, and Costco/retail returns for pennies on the dollar, resell individually via Facebook Marketplace, Facebook Live, and a physical storefront. Sell literally anything — furniture, appliances, exercise equipment, shoes, clothing (sorted into bins by size), pressure washers, a chicken coop, digital pianos, industrial hardware, even a used pickup truck.
- Started with a single $1,200 liquidation pallet purchase, rolled 100% of profit into the next purchase, repeated ("keep rolling") rather than reinvesting outside capital.
- First month in a rented tow-truck shop (October 2024): ~$60,000 in sales, roughly half profit.

## Origin story / path to this business
- Background: owned a tool company, worked in diamond wholesaling, ran a glass/window replacement company, then owned/operated 10 restaurants (one brand, various concepts) for ~18 years starting 2002.
- COVID shut down all restaurants; while starting a "new business each year" to fund his daughter's college (to teach her business isn't free), started a junk removal business, then began reselling usable furniture people gave away for junk removal (inspired by a Ryan Pineda-style "couch flipping" YouTube video) — loaded straight off customers' curbs onto a trailer, photographed, and listed on Marketplace without unloading.
- While selling off restaurants, accumulated excess/repair-spare restaurant equipment in a warehouse; began reselling it, then buying more via GovDeals to resell.
- Sales channel hack for restaurant equipment: partnered with Cisco and US Foods sales reps (who already visit restaurant owners/chefs daily) — offered them a 10% commission on any customer referral that led to a sale. Most reps declined payment and referred customers for free/as relationship-building goodwill (problem-solving is part of their own sales role). Worked through ~20 reps to find 3 reliable, high-value referral sources ("80/20 rule," closer to 85/15 in practice).
- Pivotal networking moment: commented on a liquidation-reselling expert's (Shannon [Jean], apparently a known figure in this space) content; he suggested trying B-Stock and other liquidation auction platforms (Costco returns, GovDeals) instead of just restaurant equipment.

## How the physical retail location was built (and the "sell what you have" MVP philosophy)
- First live sale test: put Costco-return furniture (recliners, loveseats) in a storage-unit parking lot, went live on Facebook to his existing restaurant-customer base (not a business page — his personal following built over years), demoed products in person (wife did live demos) — made $3,000 profit in a single Saturday with zero platform fees, versus needing ~$30,000 in restaurant sales to net the same $3,000.
- Leased an old tow-truck shop (after losing a better retail space to a competing bidder) for $3,200/month on a 3-year lease, chosen for its highly-trafficked location (one block from the town's only major retail store, a Fred Meyer). Put the handful of items he owned out on the sidewalk with an old neon "open" sign — no formal setup, no signage beyond a repurposed blinking reader-board sign from a former restaurant.
- Lesson learned the hard way: the shop's roll-up bay door had to stay open for customers to stop in, even in ~35°F weather, since drive-by visibility (not comfort) drove traffic. Building was too small once inventory grew, requiring ~30-40 minutes each morning/evening to move stock outside/inside.
- After ~5-6 months at $60k+/month, moved to a 6,000 sq ft former Sears store in downtown Ellensburg at $4,500/month rent. Sales initially dipped due to loss of high-visibility, drive-by "curiosity" traffic — required deliberately ramping up marketing (flyers, Facebook posts) to recover. A better-looking, "real" retail storefront was also explicitly valued as more attractive to a future buyer of the business than a scrappy tow-shop operation, even if the tow-shop was profitable.
- Marketing tactic: posted a semi-joking "Jim's thinking about going out of business" Facebook post during a slow stretch — it drove the store's best single day ever (~$15,000 in sales) as customers rushed in to check; he now plans to repeat a version of this "closing" hook periodically.

## Key numbers cited
- Rent: $3,200/mo (tow shop, ~small space) -> $4,500/mo (6,000 sq ft ex-Sears store).
- First month revenue: ~$60,000 (about half profit); sustained $60k+/month for 5-6 consecutive months.
- Notable flip: won an auction lot of 1,000 aluminum industrial hinges for ~$2,800 total (no other bidders); scrap value alone would have been ~$4,500 (his stated worst-case floor); resold 200 of them directly back to the manufacturer's channel (via manufacturer reps) at $50/hinge = $10,000 from just 200 units, a ~25x return on the pro-rated cost of those units; still holds 700 hinges unsold.
- Won a 1989 Ford F450 utility/service truck (crane, welder, toolbox) at GovDeals auction for ~$8,100 (bid $100 over the next-highest of many bidders); resold it days later for $14,000 by posting the (GovDeals-watermarked) auction photos to his personal Facebook.
- Costco shed example: budgeted a max bid of $1,500 (retail price) for a personally-needed shed, won for less, then sold the rest of that furniture lot.
- Recent small flip: sold three used digital pianos and a golf bag from a single old Marketplace listing for a net profit of $1,100 (people keep reaching out to that same evergreen ad).
- Sold his prior window-installation business era: quoted new windows at $1,000 each (competitors often ~$500), cost per window from supplier ~$150; made $52,000 in his first week going door-to-door with flyers after his boss refused a raise and told him to "just start your own company."

## Sourcing and platforms named
- **B-Stock** (called "Betock" in transcript) — recommended as a strong, beginner-friendly liquidation auction platform with built-in educational resources; sign up with a business license.
- **GovDeals** — government surplus auction site (also used to buy used vehicles/equipment; photos carry a GovDeals watermark, useful signal when reposting for resale).
- Facebook Marketplace and Facebook Live — primary sales channels; Live selling to an existing warm audience has no platform fees and higher trust/closing ease vs. cold platforms like Whatnot or Marketplace (where you must first educate buyers about your brand).
- eBay — used as a pricing/comps reference (e.g., found that individual Dyson vacuum replacement parts sell for more on eBay than the price of a whole broken-but-cleanable donor vacuum from a liquidation pallet).

## Tactical advice given (generalizable)
- "It's not the buying, it's the selling" — start by learning to sell using items you already own before buying inventory; don't go into debt to learn the buying side.
- There's a buyer for everything, including visibly damaged goods (e.g., a cracked patio table) — the seller's own taste/standards ("I wouldn't buy this") are irrelevant; price/remarket to find that item's real buyer instead of getting stuck on hard-to-move items.
- On lowball offers: don't reject a lowballer outright — pivot them ("I'd love to help — I get lightly used versions in sometimes, want me to reach out when I have one in your range?") to keep them as a future warm lead rather than losing the relationship.
- If an item isn't selling in one local market, try re-listing it in a different geographic market/audience where the item fits better (e.g., a high-end wine cooler doesn't sell to farmers who want a chest freezer for meat, but sells fine in a wealthier suburban market) — he maintains satellite drop points (in-laws' Seattle-area house, a partner's shop in the Tri-Cities) to cross-list slow-moving inventory into better-fit markets, and builds long-term repeat customers by personally delivering purchases even long distances.
- Offering a warranty on a stagnant listing can unstick sales fast — cited example: a Kentucky reseller with 3 identical unsold ovens for months added a warranty to the listing and sold one within 2 days.
- For restaurant equipment specifically: prefer gas-powered appliances over electric (many buyer locations lack the electrical capacity for large electric equipment); expect items to be dirty/greasy, requiring cleanup before resale.
- For a struggling local service business (e.g., a stagnant window-replacement company), checking the Meta Ads Library for competitors' running ad creative/format and directly copying the winning approach (before/after images to a landing page contact form, not "instant form" leads) reportedly tripled lead flow within a week.
- On budgeting for auction-buying: set and stick to a hard buying budget agreed with a spouse/partner to avoid over-purchasing inventory faster than you can sell it — "buying is fun, but you have to sell it."
- Offered to personally help find liquidation auctions for anyone in the US who reaches out (via X/Twitter, @resellerrowe), suggesting the auction landscape and networks (e.g., a community built around the "Shannon [Jean]" reselling figure) are broadly accessible nationwide, not just B-Stock/GovDeals-specific to his region.

## Caveats
- Business relies heavily on a specific low-competition, high-visibility physical location ("location, location, location") — moving to a nicer but less-trafficked storefront caused a real, if temporary, sales dip until marketing was increased to compensate.
- Some flips (e.g., the 1,000 hinges) required cold-calling multiple manufacturer reps before finding one willing to help broker a resale — success wasn't guaranteed on the first attempt.
- The interviewee notes explicitly that most people who just watch/hear this content won't act on it without more structured support (plug for the host's paid community, TKOwners.com) — not a claim that the model is turnkey without effort.
