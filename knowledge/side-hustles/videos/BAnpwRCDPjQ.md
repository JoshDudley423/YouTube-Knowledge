# He Watched This Channel and Started a $2,600/Week Side Hustle
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BAnpwRCDPjQ | Published: 2026-01-22

Interview with Tim (Erlanger, Kentucky; W-2 IT/AV background), who launched "I Dig It RC" — a mobile trailer business letting kids and adults pay to operate remote-control excavators, front loaders, and 4x4 crawler vehicles in a sandpit — after seeing a viral Koerner short (~20M views) about a similar café concept in Dubai. Now expanding into a permanent retail location. Extremely concrete numbers/process throughout.

## The idea and origin
- Original inspiration: a Dubai café combining coffee with a sandbox of remote-controlled excavators/diggers/cranes. Tim adapted it to a mobile trailer format for U.S. fairs/festivals rather than a fixed café.
- What made this specific idea appealing to Tim over other ideas: low barrier to entry and low financial risk relative to potential upside — running the numbers, he calculated that even 50% capacity utilization would make it "a home run."
- Was working a stable W-2 job the whole time (was laid off for ~2 months during this period, then got another W-2 job) and ran this as a side hustle; explicitly credits Koerner's "you can stack smaller businesses" framing (rather than needing one billion-dollar idea) for giving him confidence to start.

## Trailer version: setup, costs, and operations
- Startup cost: ~$10,000–$11,000 total. Breakdown: ~$6,000 for a new utility trailer (couldn't find a workable used one in time, given he'd already committed to event bookings before the trailer existed); ~$500 each for 2 excavators; ~$400 each for 2 front loaders; ~$250 each for 2 dump trucks (~$500 total — these underperformed and were mostly abandoned/given away because they didn't handle the sand pit terrain well); ~$2,000 on 4x4 RC vehicles and other marketing materials; ~$200 on website/back-end software setup.
- Pricing evolution: launched at $5 per 10-minute session, but found parents got bored/restless waiting 10 minutes, which capped throughput. Switched to $5 per 5 minutes — this doubled the business's revenue despite some pushback that it seemed like a price increase per minute.
- Private party pricing: $399 for a 2-hour party (compared favorably by Tim to typical kids' birthday party costs).
- Capacity: trailer accommodates up to ~10 simultaneous players (equipment includes 3 excavators, 2 front loaders, a large tower crane, and ~7 RC trucks/tractors/vehicles); beyond 10 it gets logistically chaotic due to traffic within the pit (though the excavators themselves are heavy metal and undamaged by collisions).
- Staffing: run entirely by Tim and his wife — swapping batteries, taking payments, monitoring timers, righting flipped-over 4x4 vehicles (a frequent occurrence since the small RC cars are geared fast and prone to flipping).
- Fair/event costs: paid $100–$550 for a week at a given county fair, depending on expected crowd size (organizers price based on prior-year attendance); larger, higher-demand events (bigger church carnivals, certain school events) were much harder to get into and charge significantly more. Weather is the biggest swing factor in per-event revenue since these are outdoor events.
- Revenue results across the season: worst event (first one, deliberately chosen as a small county fair to learn) sold only ~10 sessions (~$50, a net loss once his own kids' concession spending is subtracted). Typical/average event days brought $200–$300 in revenue; best single day was ~$800. Best full week (5-day fair, "Alexandria Fair") brought in ~$2,600 (the title figure), attributed mainly to a favorable, disposable-income-heavy crowd (a rural county fair built around a horse show). A private booked event for Choice Hotels' corporate tailgate (paid a flat $499 for ~3 hours, offered free to attendees) was their busiest single session, running through all their spare batteries.
- Break-even/profitability timeline: sold 1,700 sessions at $5 each plus 7 private parties at $399 over the summer, enough to pay off nearly all invested capital before starting the retail buildout; full payback of the original ~$10-11K trailer investment took roughly 6–8 months (through most of a full season including a surprisingly strong fall event season).
- Marketing: relied heavily on repeat exposure at recurring local county fairs (customers recognized them across multiple fairs) plus handed out ~1,500–2,000 branded stickers and refrigerator magnets (his wife's idea — a "business card" that people keep rather than discard) at events. Now also running paid ads directly to his email newsletter rather than relying on social algorithms.
- Custom software: built entirely in Replit (a "vibe coding" tool) by Tim himself — despite no formal software background (though he has a control-systems/QA testing background, which he credits for prompting the AI effectively). The app runs session timers on a mounted TV display (countdown per named customer, color-coded warning near time's-up, automated "time's up" flash) which offloads the job of policing kids' time onto their own parents watching the screen; also functions as a point-of-sale system, tracks inventory, financials, gift cards/memberships, event requests pipeline, and even sends the business's email newsletter (via SendGrid) — effectively a homemade micro CRM/ERP. Built with the explicit intent of making the whole operation "franchisable" — a new franchisee could get a version of the same software reset for their location with no separate dev investment.

## Retail store expansion (in progress at filming)
- Startup cost for the physical store: ~$18,000 (Tim self-performed some renovation work to cut costs, which earned a 50% reduction on his first 4 months' rent from the landlord). Monthly rent: $1,000 (favorable location — a small town, Erlanger, KY, near a popular local brunch spot). The city gave Tim a construction/build-out grant reimbursing 50% of build-out spend, since local government actively wants the business there (they reached out directly after seeing his social media).
- Layout: splitting into two zones — a "construction" excavator/sandpit area and a separate 4x4 "rock crawling"/mountain area (found the two vehicle types don't mix well together in one pit). Planned capacity: ~17 simultaneous active sessions (4 excavators, 2 dump trucks, another crane, 2 front loaders in construction; 6–7 4x4 vehicles on the "mountain").
- New pricing model planned for the store: $15 for 15 minutes, with per-minute cost dropping as customers buy more time; plus memberships (basic tier: discounts + 1 free session/month; unlimited tier: 1 free session every day) as a recurring-revenue mechanism.
- Staffing plan: 2 employees at full capacity (one on the counter, one managing sessions/battery swaps); batteries need swapping roughly every hour of active machine use.
- Hours: planned to open 7 days/week, 10am–8pm (70 hours/week) initially, with Tim and his wife covering shifts themselves before hiring, given both work from home otherwise.
- Break-even/profitability modeling (Tim's own numbers, not independently audited): break-even estimated at ~12% of maximum theoretical session capacity utilization. Illustrative "sold-out" math (co-calculated live in the interview): 70 hours/week × 60 min × 17 simultaneous $1/minute sessions ≈ $10,000/day gross at 100% capacity; with daily overhead estimated at ~$600–$1,000/day (staff ~$50/hr × 10 hrs = $500, rent ~$100/day, insurance/AC etc.), even a conservative 20% utilization rate was projected to net roughly $300,000/year in profit. Host explicitly frames this as unaudited back-of-envelope math, not a verified outcome.
- Competitive landscape referenced: similar concepts exist in the UK, Taiwan, and a newer, well-marketed competitor in Canada ("Diggers Cafe," reportedly investor-backed with a more polished build-out). Tim's own store is smaller and skips full food service (only non-refrigerated items like coffee/baked goods) to avoid food-service regulatory complexity.
- Planned additional revenue lines: an outdoor RC course and "fast lap" course on ~0.5–0.75 acres of unused adjoining land, allowing customers to bring their own RC vehicles (separate rental vs. bring-your-own pricing); hosting RC competitions; an affiliate partnership with a ramp/terrain equipment maker ("Mountain Blocks") for a large (~30 ft) crawling course feature.

## Customer insights
- Demographic split roughly 50/50 between minors and adults (30–70 year old men) — a notable and unexpected finding. At county fairs, adult heavy-equipment operators (e.g., dirt-track machine operators) frequently paid to play at the end of their own shifts, since the RC controls mirror real Caterpillar excavator/loader controls — framed as both fun and a form of light operator training/familiarization.
- Word-of-mouth/repeat-attendance effect noted across multiple fairs in the same region (customers recognized the business from prior events).

## Caveats and lessons
- Execution mistake acknowledged: the trailer's walls were built too high (assuming digging required deep sand, when ~1 foot of sand sufficed), which blocked passersby from seeing the activity inside and hurt walk-up foot traffic/impulse conversion — a lower-walled design would likely have converted more of the "up-and-down" foot traffic at fairs.
- All financial figures (session counts, per-event revenue, break-even percentages, projected $300K profit ceiling) are self-reported by the business owner and not independently audited.
- The business model depends heavily on outdoor/seasonal event circuits (weather-sensitive) for the trailer phase; the store is a bet that a fixed retail model with membership recurring revenue will be more stable, but is new/unproven at time of filming (opening in February).
- Not a truly passive side hustle: required in-person operation by the owner and spouse across many event days/nights while both held full-time jobs.
