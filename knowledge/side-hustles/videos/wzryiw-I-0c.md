# Secrets to Starting a Dumpster Rental Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzryiw-I-0c | Published: 2024-05-15

Chris Koerner coaches a guest ("Bailey," in Salt Lake City, Utah) who is 6 weeks into launching a residential/commercial roll-off dumpster rental business, on marketing and pricing strategy.

## The business / startup numbers
- Started from scratch (not an acquisition): flew to Texas, bought a truck plus 11 dumpsters, drove them ~26 hours back to Salt Lake City.
- Cost per dumpster: ~$5,500-$6,000 all-in after painting/decals.
- Rental rate: ~$350/week for a standard residential rental (20 cubic yard dumpsters); a 3-day residential booking was discounted to $299 in one example.
- Business model origin: guest had been flipping houses for 3-4 years, rented dumpsters constantly, ran the numbers, and realized the rental economics were attractive enough to start the service himself.
- Operationally simple to run: at this stage, staffed with one full-time driver and one full-time office manager/scheduler. Main downside stated explicitly: it's very capital-intensive (dumpster cost + truck + yard rental).
- Fixed monthly costs mentioned: yard rental ~$1,200/month, plus payroll (driver + office manager) and equipment loan payments. Once 1.5-2 bookings/day are hit, fixed costs are covered and margins improve significantly beyond that.
- Ordered 12 more dumpsters (bringing total to 23) to keep up with demand as weather warmed.

## Demand patterns
- Highly variable by day of week: Wednesday-Friday are very busy (sometimes hitting 100% dumpster utilization/all units out), while Monday-Tuesday are often near 0% booked — driven by homeowners wanting a warm weekend to do a project (garage cleanout, deck teardown, small renovation, or moving).
- Weather-dependent: demand spikes noticeably on warm days.
- ~90% of current customers are one-off homeowners; very few repeat customers.

## Commercial vs. residential dumpster economics
- Commercial/monthly contract example: one client (does commercial buildouts) pays $550/month for one dumpster including one scheduled empty; an extra swap/empty before that costs $200.
- Key insight: a commercial monthly customer takes the *same amount of driver/labor work* as a single residential drop-off/pickup, but pays much more ($550/month vs. $299 for a 3-day residential job) — the incremental margin is "pure profit" since labor cost is identical.
- Trade-off: committing a dumpster to a month-long commercial contract ties up equipment that could otherwise be turned 2-3x/week for residential jobs — commercial is more profitable per unit of labor but residential turns more total revenue per dumpster when equipment is scarce. Guest's long-term plan: shift the equipment mix increasingly toward commercial as the fleet grows, while keeping some residential mix to keep cash flow steady (a pure-commercial-only book would leave him "booked out" and unable to pay bills in the near term).
- You don't have to only rent dumpsters — they can be sold outright (e.g., to another dumpster company, or to a business like a metal fabrication shop needing a permanent on-site dumpster for recycling), with the seller optionally retained to service/empty it on a recurring basis; but the guest says most of the business is rental, not sales.

## Current customer acquisition (at time of filming)
- ~90% of leads come from Google Ads, spending ~$200/day, which the guest attributes to producing ~1.5-2 rental bookings/day (i.e., ~$700-$1,000/day in projected revenue from that spend, though noted this needs to hold up as ad spend scales).
- Noted that scaling ad spend further increases cost-per-click and cost-per-conversion (diminishing returns), but because most of the cost structure is fixed, incremental bookings above the breakeven point are highly profitable even as ad efficiency declines.
- Cold-calling roofers, remediation companies, and home builders (200-300 calls made) yielded no traction — most already have an established dumpster vendor relationship, and larger dumpster operators can undercut on price due to scale.
- Some early SEO/Google Map Pack ranking traction; also networking with realtors and moving companies for referrals.
- Considered (early-stage, one call made) partnering with local junk-hauling companies for referrals: large franchise junk-removal operators (1-800-GOT-JUNK, Junk King) often have their own dumpsters, but smaller/mid-size junk removal operators without dumpsters will sometimes upsell a customer into a junk-removal job instead of referring them out — noted these operators tend to run on thin margins and might accept a lower cut to make a referral work.

## Advice given (Koerner's recommendations)
1. **Hire a GMB (Google My Business) / local-SEO agency** to grow organic Google Map Pack ranking rather than relying solely on paid Google Ads — framed as a good long-term investment (typically $1,000-$1,500/month) because progress is directly measurable (ranking position), unlike many agency services where it's unclear if they're "even working."
2. **Experiment with aggressive dynamic/surge pricing** on the website — since it's cold traffic converting once, there's no customer memory/backlash risk from frequently changing prices. Suggested testing steep discounts during known-low-demand periods (Monday/Tuesday) to see if volume increases enough to offset lower price, and even testing price increases on high-demand days to see if conversion holds. Rationale (with a hotel-room analogy): an idle asset (empty dumpster in the yard, like an empty hotel room) earns nothing, so even a heavily discounted, break-even booking is better than idle capacity — plus it generates a customer email/contact for remarketing, and (specific to dumpsters) gets a highly visible branded unit ("moving billboard" — the dumpsters are painted bright blue with decals, a QR code and phone number) placed around town, which can generate neighbor referral calls.
3. **Use idle driver time for near-breakeven deliveries** on slow days rather than leaving the driver idle, since he's paid full-time regardless — treat marginal bookings as worthwhile even at thin/no margin because of the branding/remarketing upside.
4. **Add a personality-driven homepage video** (Dollar Shave Club-style viral commercial cited as the case-study reference) showing the founder's personality (e.g., climbing on a stack of dumpsters, joking about having 26 dumpsters to fill) — rationale: dumpster rental is a commodity, so a landing page that makes a cold visitor like/trust the specific founder can meaningfully lift conversion rate on cold traffic, where "every little tweak" to the landing page matters a lot.
5. **Tell the founder's origin story in marketing copy/outreach** (e.g., "I'm a Utah entrepreneur who drove to Texas and bought a truck and 11 dumpsters to better serve this valley") — advice that storytelling in cold outreach and on-site copy builds personal connection and outperforms generic "faceless business" messaging; Koerner cites his own practice of telling employees' personal stories (e.g., "James, my tree biz operator") in cold outreach with good results.
6. **Pause outbound cold-calling for a couple weeks** and redirect that time/budget into organic local digital placements: Nextdoor, local Facebook groups (buy/sell groups, mom groups), KSL (Utah classifieds), Craigslist, and Facebook Marketplace — test this channel shift before returning to cold calling.
7. **Build a scraped list of local handymen and small general contractors** (rather than only targeting large remediation/roofing/home-builder companies) for cold outreach (call, text, email, or website contact forms) — flagged as likely "lower-hanging fruit" than large companies, with the caveat that many small handymen/contractors have no website or Google Maps presence and rely purely on referrals, making them harder to find/scrape.
8. Continue pursuing residential and commercial realtor relationships for referrals — described as already "on the right track," just deprioritize temporarily while testing the above.

## Caveats
- Capital intensity is the single biggest downside named by the guest himself — ~$5,500-$6,000 per unit is a heavy upfront cost before any revenue.
- Heavy reliance (90%) on a single paid channel (Google Ads) at ~$200/day is a real concentration risk; cold calling and networking had not yet produced meaningful results after hundreds of calls.
- Commercial contracts, while more profitable per unit of labor, reduce fleet flexibility/utilization — a business that goes all-commercial too early could face a cash crunch from being "booked out" on long-term low-frequency contracts.
