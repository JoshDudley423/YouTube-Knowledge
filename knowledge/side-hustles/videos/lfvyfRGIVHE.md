# How to Make $1.6K/Day on Facebook Marketplace
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lfvyfRGIVHE | Published: 2026-03-15

Interview with Matt Howe, a former high school math teacher (Jackson, Michigan) who built a Facebook Marketplace lawnmower-flipping side hustle into a full equipment dealership (Howe Equipment) doing $1.1M+ in a single month.

## The business idea
- Buy undervalued used equipment (starting with couches, appliances, church pews, then riding/zero-turn lawnmowers, then trailers, then larger equipment like ATVs/side-by-sides) from individuals and dealers, and resell at or near market value — explicitly NOT a fix-and-flip model; the core skill is finding underpriced items and moving them fast, not repairing them.
- Scaled from personal flipping into an actual equipment dealership selling both new (from manufacturers like Toro, Trailer Line, Bad Boy) and used inventory, plus service/repair.
- Also built (and later stepped back from) a side project called "Family of Flippers," teaching/partnering with other people to flip using his capital/setup on a profit-split basis.

## Numbers
- Was earning ~$40-45K/year as a teacher before starting.
- First deal: a used church pew bought at an estate sale, resold for a few hundred dollars — described as his first realization that flipping was viable.
- Notable early deal: an enclosed 16-ft trailer listed for $300 (seller just wanted it gone, had been sitting for years) that he resold for $3,000 — a 10x flip, cited repeatedly as illustrating the value of speed (won by responding and dispatching a buyer within ~15 minutes of the listing going up).
- First summer flipping mowers alongside teaching: bought and sold ~30 zero-turn mowers, profiting $20,000-30,000 that summer alone; first mower profit was ~$100, second was ~$500 ("that's when the lightbulb really went off").
- Replaced his teaching income "instantaneously" once he started flipping mowers seriously.
- Rule of thumb used when entering any new resale category for the first time: assume roughly 15-30% margin is achievable, and bigger-ticket items (e.g., $2,500 mower vs. $300 item) are preferable for the same time/effort since the same skillset applies regardless of item value.
- After roughly a year of flipping (5-10 mowers done personally before hiring a driver), scaled into a real operation: hired a driver/picker (1099 basis, target pay ~$20-25/hr for using his truck, ~$40-60/hr if using their own truck) and a mechanic ("Bill," originally paid ~$14/hr, later charged out at $125/hr shop rate once formalized under the dealership).
- Sourced a high-volume wholesale channel: a dealer (Wanamaker's, the largest Simplicity dealer in the nation) who didn't want to deal with used trade-in inventory; Matt bought 20-30 mowers/month from this single source at $1,000-2,500 margin each.
- New equipment margins at the dealership: 18-22% when "done right" (good dealers); dealers not optimizing well typically see 10-15%. Follows MAP (Minimum Advertised Price) pricing rather than discounting, favoring volume over maximizing margin per unit ("try to make 80% of the money and move it within a week").
- Zero-turn mowers that sell new for $12,000-20,000 can often be bought used in great condition for a fraction of that — a large part of why this category has attractive margins.
- Recent example deals (at time of recording): bought a Polaris Ranger needing engine work for $4,500-5,000, sold for $10,000 after repair (~$4,000 profit); bought a traded-in side-by-side for $3,750, listed to sell around $8,000; bought a Kubota side-by-side for $8,000 (needed work), listed for $13,000.
- Dealership's best month ever: $1.1 million in sales in a single month; overall dealership net margin ~20-25% gross, but because ~90% of the business is sales (not service, which is typically higher-margin/healthier mix), net profit at the bottom line is more like 7-12% — described as normal/healthy for a well-run dealership with 60% sales / 40% service mix (his is skewed more toward sales).

## Process / steps
- Speed is the single most important factor: get sellers on the phone (not just text/email) immediately, and dispatch a buyer/picker as fast as possible — a deal that good "is gone" within minutes if you're slow. Getting on the phone (or better, in person) builds more commitment and closes better than text.
- To lock in a deal remotely before a driver arrives: send a Venmo/PayPal deposit immediately to secure it, explicitly framing it as confirming "your word is good that we have a deal."
- Build a "call list" of people willing to drive/pick up items on short notice for a set rate (starting with family/friends for free or low fixed rates, then formalizing into paid 1099 contractors) — the deeper this bench, the less personal life sacrifice (missed dinners, late-night drives) is required to seize fast-moving deals.
- Negotiation framework taught in the episode: "he who speaks first loses" — get the seller to name their lowest acceptable price first rather than opening with your own number. Build rapport specifically by talking positively about the item itself (not criticizing it) since that shared interest is the main connection point available; this warms the seller up to a lower price. If a seller refuses to name a number, counter with a deliberately unrealistically low offer to force them to reveal their actual floor (they'll often blurt out a real number in reaction, even one they'd never have offered unprompted). Use silence after a counteroffer rather than filling the gap.
- Buying philosophy: avoid needing to fix items yourself; the profit is in buying low and reselling near true value quickly, not in refurbishing (refurbishing crushes effective hourly rate and limits scalability, per Matt's direct comparison to a competing flipper who fixes mowers himself and makes more total profit per item but works ~80 hrs/week and "can never scale").
- Sourcing beyond individual Marketplace listings: proactively cold-call dealerships (via phone book/Google, not just online listings) asking if they have used/trade-in equipment sitting unsold — many dealers don't want to deal with used inventory or don't bother listing it on Facebook, making them a major "under-arbitraged" wholesale source. Establishing an ongoing relationship with even one such dealer can produce dozens of units a month.
- Transparency as a sales tactic: openly tell sellers/trade-in customers that you resell equipment (rather than hiding it) — this builds trust and generates referrals/repeat trade-ins, and is described as "more profitable" than operating secretively.
- Growth path: personal flipping (5-10 units) → hiring a driver/mechanic on a 1099 basis → renting/buying larger space (moved from a residential garage to a 24-acre property with a barn) → buying a physical building (an old dance studio, bought opportunistically because it was poorly marketed) → building out a full licensed dealership carrying recognized brands over about 4 years.

## Tools / platforms named
- Facebook Marketplace — primary sourcing and selling venue.
- Craigslist — used in his earliest flips (pre-Marketplace-focused era) alongside Facebook.
- Venmo/PayPal — used for securing deposits on remote deals.
- His own YouTube channel (@matt_howe / "Matt Howe" / "Howe Equipment") for further tactical content.

## Caveats / risks
- Physical/logistics risk exists in fast, informal transactions: an anecdote describes a trailer detaching from a truck on the highway during an early pickup (no safety chains used) — no one was hurt, but it illustrates real operational risk in rushed, inexperienced execution.
- Requires genuine willingness to prioritize speed over convenience/family time in the early stages (calling sellers between class periods while still teaching, driving at night, straining his marriage's risk tolerance) before the business could support outsourcing that labor.
- Category/geography matters for annual volume: cold climates like Michigan have a shorter mowing season (seasonal slow periods in winter), whereas year-round markets (e.g., Texas, cited by the host) can sustain more consistent flipping volume.
- Scaling via partners ("Family of Flippers") required a high degree of personal trust since it involved shared capital and profit splits (50/50 in his structure) — described as something he built with people he already knew well (college teammates, family), not a formalized franchise-like system.
- Explicit warning against buying anything needing repair once you don't have a dedicated mechanic on staff — the "fix it up" approach only becomes sensible again once you already have paid technician labor you're trying to keep busy in the off-season (a dealership-stage consideration, not an early-stage one).
- The specific product category (mowers, trailers) is explicitly said not to be the key insight — Matt states the real skill is picking any category with sufficient margin and executing on speed/negotiation/sourcing, and committing to it long-term ("you do it for 10 years") rather than switching niches after a month.
