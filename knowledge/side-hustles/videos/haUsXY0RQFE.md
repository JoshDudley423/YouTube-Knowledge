# The Most Profitable One-Man Business You've Never Heard Of
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=haUsXY0RQFE | Published: 2026-02-02

Field interview with Alex (Brushworksco) about his forestry mulching / land clearing business in Cincinnati, OH.

## The business idea: forestry mulching / land clearing
- Service: clearing overgrown land/woods (e.g., invasive honeysuckle in Ohio) using a skid-steer (Bobcat) with a forestry mulching attachment, which grinds brush/trees into mulch on-site rather than hauling debris away.
- Origin: Alex owned 5 acres (3 wooded/overgrown) and needed it cleared himself; tree services quoted "tens of thousands of dollars" and would take ~2 weeks; he found forestry mulching but only commercial-focused providers existed, and the nearest was in Lexington, KY (far from Cincinnati) — spotted a market gap for a residential-focused forestry mulching service.
- Market validation method (replicable): did keyword research using an SEO/keyword tool plus ChatGPT to brainstorm both industry terms ("forestry mulching," "land clearing" — how commercial buyers search) and consumer long-tail terms regular homeowners actually use ("clear my land," "get rid of honeysuckle," "open up my land"). Compared resulting search volume to concrete companies (a comparable price-point local service) as a benchmark: land clearing demand was close to concrete-company demand in his market, but concrete had ~1,000 competing companies locally versus almost none for residential forestry mulching — a clear demand/supply gap ("arbitrage opportunity").

## Startup costs and financing
- Bought the machine before securing customers (Alex says the "smart" order would have been business/customers first, then equipment, but he was confident enough in demand to reverse it).
- First attachment (Fecon brand, used/ex-rental): $15,000 (new: ~$40,000).
- First machine (Bobcat T770, used): $74,000 (a newer/larger Bobcat T86 new runs ~$115,000).
- Financed the machine + attachment with $5,000 down; monthly payment ~$1,400.
- Also bought a trailer ($11,000, paid cash, though financing was an option) and a used truck able to pull it ($8,000).
- Total effective out-of-pocket startup cost cited as roughly $5,000 (down payment) if truck/trailer are also financed/leased instead of bought outright.
- With hindsight, Alex says he'd buy the machine NEW rather than used: forestry work is hard on equipment, it's difficult to assess wear on a used machine, and new machines come with warranty coverage; new equipment was also eligible for 0% financing at the time.
- Larger machines (~12,000 lb) require at least a 3500-series truck to tow, and a CDL license to legally pull that trailer weight.
- Renting: Bobcats themselves are easy to rent (e.g., via Facebook Marketplace, ~$350/day for a machine without the forestry kit), but the specialized forestry attachment/kit (reinforced cab fall-guard, 3/4" polycarbonate door) is much harder and more expensive to rent — most dealers won't rent a bare Bobcat with a forestry attachment unless it's a long-term rental.

## Pricing and unit economics
- Pricing evolved from a flat day-rate to a formula based on (1) brush density tier (3 tiers) x (2) terrain tier (3 tiers) x acreage, with a base "per quarter-acre" price and multipliers for harder tiers.
- Simple starting alternative: price by the day — customers are comfortable with this and it's easier for a new operator.
- Worked example: 3 acres, tier-2 density, ~3 days of work, priced at $6,000-$7,000.
- Per-job cost breakdown for a $6,000 job: ~$80-$100/day diesel fuel (~$300 for a 3-day job), ~$250-$300/day labor if hiring an operator (~$900-$1,000 for 3 days), ~8-10% set aside for equipment repairs/wear (~$600-$800), plus a machine loan payment (~$1,500/month amortized). Net: total costs run roughly 30-40% of revenue even when paying a hired operator, yielding about $4,000 profit on a $6,000 job (or ~$5,000 if the owner operates the machine themselves, since no labor cost).
- Add-on upsell: "grapple work" (using a grapple attachment to move/pile debris) billed separately at $750/job; most customers add this on.
- Average job size (steady-state): ~$3,300.
- Wear/consumable notes: forestry mulching is harder on equipment than typical excavation — hydraulic couplings break more often and should be stockpiled in bulk (cheaper than one-off replacement); very hardwood species (example given: Osage orange, "like concrete" to mulch) slow work and can break teeth; the crew clears the work area of metal, tires, and barbed wire before starting (customers are asked to do this themselves via email instructions, but if they don't, clearing debris becomes billable time).
- Insurance: about $150/month for one machine (classified as an excavating company rather than a tree service, which is more affordable); general liability/umbrella coverage runs about $225/month.

## Marketing: the "capture, educate, sale" framework
- Step 1 (Capture): vertical video-only Meta (Facebook/Instagram) ads showing dramatic mulching footage — slow-motion before/afters — designed purely to grab attention around a recognizable problem (overgrown land).
- Step 2 (Educate): ad links to a YouTube channel (and/or website that features the YouTube channel prominently) that explains what forestry mulching is, shows what to expect on a job, and shows the finished result over time (e.g., 12-18 months later) — most homeowners don't know this service category exists or what to search for, so this step does real buyer education and builds trust/legitimacy before any sales conversation.
- Step 3 (Sale): funnel from YouTube/ads to the company website for lead capture; because leads have already watched educational content, they arrive "basically already sold," making close rates high.
- Video production: no professional equipment needed — a phone strapped to your chest or mounted on a cheap tripod for time-lapse footage is enough; free apps (CapCut, Instagram's built-in editor) suffice; footage doesn't need to be polished, just visually demonstrate the transformation.
- Website: built initially with WordPress; the same idea today could be built with an AI website builder like Lovable in effectively one prompt ("you can one-shot the website"). Must-haves: lead capture form, photos/videos, logo, service description, before/after images. When starting out with no portfolio images, Alex suggests borrowing stock/generic images of the equipment (e.g., generic Bobcat photos) as placeholder "advertising," since manufacturers likely wouldn't mind.
- Geographic targeting: ads run within roughly a 50-mile radius of the business (Cincinnati metro).
- Ad spend: started at $20/day; scaled up to ~$80/day as the business grew. Suggested initial test budget: about $500 to get a directional read on demand.

## Conversion funnel numbers (concrete)
- Cost per lead: ~$20-25.
- Lead-to-quote rate: "almost all" leads convert to an actual in-person quote.
- Quote-to-close rate: ~49%.
- Effective customer acquisition cost: roughly $50.
- Average job value ~$3,300 → roughly a 66x return on ad spend (host's calculation, using conservative $25/lead assumption).

## Revenue/profit trajectory (concrete numbers, business started mid-May 2024)
- First 7 months (May-Dec 2024): ~$170,000-$180,000 revenue; ~70% gross margin (~"69-point-something" percent exactly) → roughly six figures of gross profit in year one's partial season.
- Best single month in year one: $45,000 revenue in October (with over $30,000 of that as profit).
- Business is seasonal in Ohio (unlike Texas, which can run mulching year-round); year 2 (2025) started slow due to record rainfall, delaying the start from planned early April to June.
- Once running, June-Dec 2025 (6 months): $565,000 revenue, aided by buying a second machine to run parallel jobs and adding a remote-controlled (RC) machine, which commands premium pricing for hillside/hard-to-access work.
- Combined 12-month trailing performance (partial year 1 + partial year 2, per host's math): roughly $750,000 revenue, ~$550,000 gross profit (using the ~70% gross margin figure), and net margin cited at about 40%, implying roughly $300,000 net profit over that stretch — though this includes reinvestment into growth (new trucks, trailers, equipment, ads).
- Forward projection for the next full year: expected to exceed $1,000,000 in revenue (target ~$1.2M) using only the equipment already owned, i.e., no further capital equipment purchases required to hit that number.
- Extending into winter: year 1 they stopped mulching entirely by late November due to weather/mud. Year 2, Alex began proactively asking large farm-property customers during summer quotes whether they'd accept having the work done in winter instead, offering a 10% discount to move flexible, weather-tolerant jobs (large farms that don't mind ruts/mud) into the off-season — effectively eliminating seasonality by pre-filling the winter schedule with the right kind of property.
- Deposits: generally none collected, except jobs over $10,000, which require a 20% deposit.

## Scalability discussion (host + Alex's estimates)
- A solo operator (one person, one machine, working full-time year-round) could reasonably do about $35,000/month in revenue and roughly $20,000/month in profit — about $240,000/year in profit running the business themselves with no employees.
- With scaling into commercial/government contracts (right-of-way clearing, solar-field clearing — cited large incumbent player: Asplundh, described as a multi-billion-dollar company in that space), Alex speculates a single metro market like Cincinnati (population ~300K city / ~2M metro) could theoretically support a business scaling to ~$30 million/year.

## Adjacent revenue: snow removal with the same equipment
- Using a snow-box/plow attachment (cited cost: a couple thousand dollars — the cheapest attachment available) on the same Bobcat, they also do commercial snow removal/plowing.
- Revenue: ~$10,000-$11,000 per significant snow event, achievable within about 20 hours of clearing work (spread across multiple machines).
- Customer acquisition model: approach general contractors directly — GCs typically win snow-removal contracts from commercial property owners/HOAs (with contractual "trigger" thresholds, e.g., must plow once 1 inch of snow accumulates) but routinely oversell their own capacity, so they sub out overflow work to operators like Alex, taking a cut of the contract value.
- Framed as a way to keep equipment monetized during the off-season/winter for anyone whose primary land-clearing work is seasonal, and noted a Bobcat platform generally supports "hundreds" of attachment types (street sweeping, tree spade/transplanting, asphalt grinding, etc.) as other adjacent revenue options.

## Caveats / risks raised
- Equipment durability: forestry mulching is harder on machines than typical use; used equipment carries more uncertainty (hidden wear) than new — Alex's stated biggest regret/change he'd make.
- Requires proper LLC/entity formation before taking real money, to shield personal liability (video is sponsored by Bizee, an LLC-formation service the host uses personally — this is a paid sponsorship, not an independent recommendation).
- Seasonality is a real constraint in colder climates (Ohio) unless proactively managed via the farm-property/winter-discount tactic described above; not an issue in warmer climates like Texas.
- Requires physical labor/equipment operation, insurance, and hands-on quoting — not a passive or remote business.
- The $30M market-scaling figure for a single metro is speculative commentary from Alex/the host, not a demonstrated result.
