# 23 Business Ideas for 2026 (Part 2/4)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mg11UqqjHhA | Published: 2024-05-17

Note: despite the video's title, the actual content is Chris Koerner narrating Part 2 of "every business I've ever started" — a rundown of businesses #23-38 from his own history, including numbers, what worked, and what failed. Framed as case studies rather than a curated idea list.

**#23 – Alabama Wholesale Homes (real estate wholesaling, early-mid 20s).**
- Wholesaling = getting property under contract (with earnest money at risk) and assigning the contract to a buyer for a spread, without ever owning the property (e.g., lock up a $100K house worth $170K, assign for $135K).
- States he disliked the business because it's "inherently shady" — you're incentivized not to disclose to either party that you're wholesaling, and gurus taught manipulative negotiation tactics (e.g., always claim the "layout" is the flaw you can't get past to talk sellers down).
- Got 2 houses under contract via a government auction site; put up $1,000 earnest money on each and **lost both deposits** (both deals fell through) — his only real wholesaling experience.
- Bought the domain "Alabama Wholesale Homes," drove SEO traffic to it for years afterward, generating leads for other wholesalers/investors even after he personally quit the business.

**#24 – Texas Smokes (spinoff of his Buc-ee's resale business, Texas Snacks).**
- Noticed ~$100 smokers sold well at Buc-ee's; built a standalone Shopify site (texassmokes.com) reselling them: bought at ~$100, sold at $160-$170.
- Ran paid Facebook ads, sold "a handful," but concluded it would never be as big as Texas Snacks and was a distracting side project, so it was abandoned to protect focus on the core (steadily growing) Texas Snacks business.

**#25 – Better Inbox (SaaS/Chrome extension idea, not launched to market).**
- Idea: track how long you/your sales team take to respond to emails (a known driver of lead conversion in sales), since the one existing competitor tool was "clunky and too expensive."
- Bought the domain, hired a VA to start building it, then got distracted and dropped it. Framed as a genuinely useful idea that nonetheless had too high an opportunity cost to be worth pursuing ("if the opportunity cost of chasing this idea is high, it's not a good idea at all").

**#26 – Halo Maids (drop-service home cleaning business) — explicitly called one of the worst businesses he's started.**
- Model: recruit cleaning crews, build a marketing site, drive traffic, charge customers (example figures implied ~$200/cleaning, pay crew ~$120, capture the spread) — same drop-service structure used in his tree-trimming business.
- Learned via course from "Rohan Gils" (course branded "Launch 28" at the time). Started with friend "Ed."
- Explicit warning: do not start a home cleaning business. Reasons given — low ticket price, many ways to fail (varying surfaces/rooms, fixed appointment windows, crew quality control), only ~20-30% of jobs become recurring despite the recurring-revenue pitch, race-to-the-bottom pricing. Contrasted unfavorably with pressure washing, which he calls a "binary outcome" business (show up, clean, leave) with far fewer failure modes.
- Working with realtors as a client niche made it "extra hard."
- Marketing test: printed double-sided flyers, drove to Nebraska Furniture Mart (Warren Buffett-affiliated, "biggest furniture store in the world") on a Saturday and left flyers on cars, targeting people mid-move (thesis: movers need cleaners, and move-ins could become recurring). Distributed ~500 flyers, got only 1-2 jobs. Lost about $1,000-$2,000 total on the business.
- General marketing-hack philosophy stated here: "the effectiveness of a growth hack is directly correlated to its lifespan" — the best hacks work great but stop working quickly, so exploit them fast when found; most guerrilla marketing tests (his estimate: 80-90%) don't work, but the 10-20% that do can "crush."

**#27 – Doomsday in a Box (30-minute landing-page test, never became a real business).**
- Triggered by a viral tweet wishing for a one-click way to buy a complete prepper/survival kit; bought the domain and threw up a landing page within ~20-30 minutes of the tweet, commented on it to test demand. Got ~3 signups, did nothing further. Included as an example of a fast, cheap demand test.

**#28 – Oven Love Bake Shop (his wife's e-commerce cookie-bar business).**
- Started shortly after COVID; sold gourmet cookie bars via Shopify and at a local farmers market; built a loyal following, ran for years, including a mini-fridge on their porch for Venmo-based self-serve sales.
- Unit economics: cost ~$0.50-$0.60 per bar, sold for $3-$4, but highly labor-intensive (hand-cut, individually wrapped and stickered, driven to market/post office) — concluded it's very hard to scale given the manual labor per unit.
- Kept for its value teaching their kids entrepreneurship (kids helped in the kitchen and at the market) rather than being the best use of time/money; notes an actual cookie-bar chain concept ("Crumbl"-like, started in Utah — referred to only as "bars or something hipsterish") validates there's real scalable potential in the category if done differently.

**#29 – Chunky Mailers / Jumbo Mail Marketing (direct mail novelty business).**
- Thesis: direct mail is normally ignored/unopened, but mailers containing a small physical object/widget (a "lump," a toothpick, etc.) inspire curiosity and get opened; cheap Chinese widgets (~$0.01 each) plus postage (~$0.40-$0.50) makes it a viable, high-margin, repeatable B2B mail product (marketed toward dentists as an example niche).
- Started developing in 2019 but pivoted away to pursue a bigger opportunity (mobile home park investing) before it took off.
- Retained the chunkymail.com domain; a couple years later, a Twitter follower ("Christian Ruff") saw a tweet about the idea and asked to build it — became "Jumbo Mail Marketing," with Koerner's Texas Snacks partner Kirk operating fulfillment (via an existing 3PL) and Christian doing sales/marketing. Eventually sold the business to other operators for "a little bit of money"; Koerner is unsure of his current ownership stake or how it's performing today.

**#30 – Crave (viral short-form food video content, modeled on "Tasty," 2017).**
- Thesis: Facebook was rewarding fast-cut, overhead-camera cooking videos (as pioneered by Tasty, which reportedly went on to sell kitchen gadgets at Walmart); rented a camera, filmed 12-24 recipe videos in a ~12-24 hour session in his home kitchen with a friend, branded it "Crave," started social pages.
- Abandoned when his other venture (an LED cycling/bike lighting business, referenced as "LCD cycle business") started taking off with the same business partner; unused footage still sits in a Dropbox folder.

**#31 – Haley (outsourced/offshore global talent agency, modeled on "Shepherd").**
- Grew out of a broader "Co-Founders" cohort program: he mentored small teams of college students (2-3x/week Zoom mentorship) through a 30-day launch, then made them profit-sharing partners, then let them become equity holders as they left/graduated school. Other cohort businesses from this same program are mentioned generally (a business-owner marketplace).
- Haley's model: replicate the Global Talent Agency model — post jobs on Upwork, build an internal database of vetted offshore talent until you no longer need Upwork, then place workers directly with inbound leads.
- Tried marketing on Reddit after noting a competitor's success came from Twitter; Reddit drove some leads/calls but no major traction. Conclusion drawn: "Twitter + Global Talent = magic" (strong product/platform fit) that could not be replicated on Reddit — introduces the concept of "product-audience fit" (the right offer matched to the right platform/audience).
- Believes the business/niche is still viable and under-saturated (his estimate: 80-90% of the US workforce doesn't even know what "VA" (virtual assistant) means) but has no personal bandwidth to revive it; the website/business still technically exists.

**#32 – Millennial Mormons (content/blog, non-monetization passion project, 2017).**
- Modeled on the "write viral content, distribute via Facebook, build an email list, monetize via ads/products" WordPress blog playbook of that era.
- A niche blog for millennial members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints; the original founders eventually left the faith and began publishing content critical of the church while still presenting the site as faith-affirming (a "bait and switch" Koerner found objectionable).
- Koerner bought the site for "a few thousand dollars" (low revenue) purely as a passion project, recruited a dozen volunteer writers/designers to clean it up and remove the anti-faith content, rebranded it — but it eventually fell inactive with no clear cause given. Included as an example of a low/no-revenue acquisition driven by personal values rather than profit.

**#33 – Fast Tree Care (still an active, growing business at time of filming).**
- Another "Co-Founders" cohort business; run day-to-day by a mentee named James, based on a drop-service "outsource 100% of the labor" playbook (similar structure to Halo Maids, but in tree trimming instead of cleaning).
- Gross margins: **40-50%**, with very low overhead (essentially just gas and time) since all physical labor is subcontracted out.
- Koerner gave James a truck, a salary from day one, and a "playbook," but has never personally been to a job site or quoted a job — pure mentorship/capital role, weekly check-ins.
- The business has since run a paid bootcamp teaching others to replicate the tree-trimming business model, and Koerner suggests they may eventually pivot toward selling that educational/content product over running physical tree jobs, since the content side is "working."

**#34 – Outfish.com (sports-fan meme blog, college years, ~2010).**
- Modeled on "People of Walmart," aiming to be a viral fan-photo/meme blog monetized via banner ads; posted a few things with 2 partners and it "just died" quickly with no traction.

**#35 – Door-to-door electronics buying (a business he still believes is underexploited today).**
- Model: go door to door (uniformed/professional appearance) offering to buy people's used electronics (phones, tablets, laptops) on the spot using a valuation app like **Gazelle.com**, paying instantly via Venmo/Zelle/PayPal, then reselling on eBay/Facebook Marketplace.
- Validated at scale through his prior iPhone repair chain ("iPhone Restore"): device buybacks were their **2nd-biggest revenue line**, generating **$40,000-$60,000/month** just from buying devices in-store and reselling on eBay; when in-store traffic slowed, they sent employees door-to-door in neighborhoods, which worked.
- Also describes a B2B variant: partnering with schools for e-recycling drives — parents send old devices to school with kids, the business collects in bulk and pays the school (schools are less price-sensitive than individual sellers, making this efficient at scale).
- His pitch for why this remains a good opportunity: door-to-door sales teams already exist at scale for pest control, solar, roofing, and alarm systems — no comparable widespread door-to-door team exists for buying electronics despite clear demand (he cites having a dozen unused old devices lying around his own house that he hasn't bothered to individually list and ship).

**#36 – CTO/crypto advising (paid consulting/advisory role, crypto-cycle era).**
- Built a large Twitter following (60,000-70,000+) during his own crypto/token launch ("McAfee days" reference); crypto projects then paid him via **1-3% token allocations** (crypto's equivalent of equity) to serve as an advisor — in exchange for occasional conference appearances and social media promotion.
- Lasted a couple of years and "paid some good bills"; ended when the broader crypto market crashed and all the client relationships quietly disappeared without any formal offboarding.

**#37 – Stripe account rental / revenue share arrangement (2018-2019).**
- Met an e-commerce operator in India running a profitable Shopify dropshipping business (sourcing cheap goods from China, running Facebook ads) who couldn't get a Stripe account because Stripe didn't support India at the time.
- Agreement: let him process payments through Koerner's US Stripe account in exchange for **10% of all revenue processed through it**; Koerner did zero ongoing work for this cut. Ended when the arrangement/ad performance stopped working (exact cause unclear); the two remained friends.
- Flagged implicitly as an unusual arrangement rather than a repeatable, generally-recommended model — presented as an opportunistic one-off deal rather than something to replicate broadly (no discussion of legality/compliance implications of routing another country's merchant volume through a personal Stripe account).

**#38 – $10 Unlock.com / $20 Unlock.com (automated iPhone-carrier-unlock referral business, ~2013).**
- Used a tool called "Cubix" (per transcript spelling) to scan Craigslist for newly listed iPhones, filtering listings that mentioned a carrier lock (Sprint/T-Mobile/Verizon/AT&T) — locked phones sell for less than unlocked ones, and most sellers didn't know unlocking was possible/cheap.
- Automatically emailed those sellers pitching that their phone was worth roughly **$50 more unlocked**, directing them to either $10unlock.com or $20unlock.com (deliberately split-testing pricing).
- Result: the $20 price point got ~20% less volume but ~100% more total revenue (price-insensitive customer base) — clear evidence buyers cared more about the outcome (unlocked phone) than the price difference.
- Fulfillment: customer submitted their IMEI on the site, request routed to an eBay-based unlocking contact who performed the actual unlock (cost him $3-5/unlock), automated email notified the customer when done — nearly zero manual work on Koerner's end.
- Eventually stopped working/was shut down (unclear whether due to the scanning software breaking or rising competition); Koerner says he "wishes he still had it," implicitly because unlocking is less relevant industry-wide today (carrier locking is far less common now than in 2013).

**Overall meta-lesson from the episode:** many of these were quick, cheap tests (landing pages, flyers, a few emails) rather than fully built businesses, and Koerner explicitly frames "opportunity cost" as the real kill criterion — several ideas he still considers fundamentally good (Better Inbox, chunky mailers, door-to-door electronics buying, offshore talent placement) were abandoned not because they failed but because something bigger came along or he lacked bandwidth, not because the underlying idea was bad.
