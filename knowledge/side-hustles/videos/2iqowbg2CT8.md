# He Makes $20k/Month on the Weekends
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2iqowbg2CT8 | Published: 2026-04-24

Two parts: (1) Chris Koerner's own breakdown of running/hosting a farmers market (via his wife's kids' entrepreneur market), and (2) an interview with Michael McCoy, co-founder of Prepa Pizza, a pizza-dough-kit business that started at farmers markets and scaled into a multi-million-dollar e-commerce brand.

## Part 1: Starting/operating a farmers market (host side, not vendor side)
- Idea: instead of selling at a farmers market, own/operate the market itself and charge vendor booth fees — described as a low-overhead, "unsexy but very profitable" business.
- **Key overhead-reduction tactic**: partner with an already-popular outdoor shopping center/mixed-use retail complex rather than paying for your own land/lease. These venues want foot traffic/occupancy and will often host a market for free, including free advertising (e.g., electronic billboards seen by "100,000 people a day," email blasts to their list) and perks (gift cards) in exchange for the market driving bustle to their existing tenants.
- **Numbers**: a mid-size shopping center could fit 200 booths; realistic vendor booth fee is $50-100 each → a 200-booth market at $100/booth = $20,000 in revenue for roughly 5-6 hours of Saturday operation (plus modest prep/marketing time). Estimated potential: doing this weekly could net ~$10,000 profit/market → ~$40,000/month.
- **Operational lesson learned**: do NOT provide tables/chairs/tents for vendors — just provide a marked ground space (a laminated numbered sign + sandbag) and require vendors to bring their own setup; providing furniture was described as a costly mistake not to repeat.
- Real example run by the host's wife (Dallas-Fort Worth, kids' entrepreneur market): first year had 50 booths (took months to sell out); second year sold 100 booths in about a week, was "a mega success" with the venue asking to repeat it again immediately, even before formal results were tallied.
- Vendor performance pattern observed: kids selling higher-ticket items ($20-50 range, e.g., $35 custom hats with an 80% margin, low equipment cost — a $70 hat press + ~$4 blank hats) made hundreds of dollars profit in a few hours; kids selling low-ticket, labor-intensive novelty items (3D-printed trinkets, crafts) generally did poorly.
- Comparable existing model cited: Tractor Supply runs Saturday markets at their stores specifically to drive foot traffic to an otherwise off-the-beaten-path retail location.
- Famous brands cited as having started at farmers markets: Justin's Nut Butter, Tate's Bake Shop, Burt's Bees.

## Part 2: Prepa Pizza (Michael McCoy) — pizza dough kit business
### The idea
- A DIY pizza dough-making kit (flour blend + yeast + salt + helper ingredients) sold direct to home cooks who want restaurant-quality pizza dough, bundled with training/support (a free instructional masterclass + live chat/phone customer support) — the product being sold is really "getting you to the finish line," not just raw ingredients.
- Deliberately chose to sell a packaged product/kit rather than open an actual pizzeria (his wife vetoed opening a restaurant given their prior restaurant-industry burnout experience) — avoids restaurant overhead, licensing, and long hours.

### Numbers
- Started completely bootstrapped, manufacturing kits by hand in their home basement with his wife (batches of ~100 at a time, using gram scales and a manual sealing machine).
- Retail price: $30 (well, $29.97) per kit, ~2 lb of product; estimated ~80% gross margin on ingredients (a comparable homemade batch of flour/water/yeast/salt costs roughly $6-8 in ingredients).
- First summer: sold ~$60,000 worth of product working under 20 hours/week (roughly $20,000/month for the first 3 months), attending 3-4 markets/week (Wednesday, Thursday, and two Saturday markets — about 12-16 markets/month, ~48 total that summer).
- Per-market revenue: averaged $1,200-$1,500 in sales per market appearance, ranging roughly $400 to $2,000 depending on the day; best single market ever was ~$3,000 (at an unusually large combined event). Markets typically ran ~4.5 hours total including setup.
- Effective profit rate while physically at the market: described as "a couple hundred bucks an hour of profit" for time spent at the table (excluding upstream prep/manufacturing time, which the founder says was actually the majority of total time invested).
- Beyond farmers markets, also sold at food shows, golf tournaments (selling 40-50 kits per golf tournament), and neighborhood events — anywhere with foot traffic and a captive, food-interested crowd.
- Transition online: first agency-run Facebook ad effort underperformed ("dismal" for about 4 months); after McCoy took over Facebook ads himself and learned the platform directly, sales in the next <30 days matched what the prior 4 months had produced combined.
- Scaled to a best month of over $160,000 in online sales; cumulative online sales over a couple of years reached "a little north of $3-4 million."
- At time of interview, running roughly 60 active Facebook ad creatives simultaneously — noted as a key insight: successful Facebook ad accounts need far more creative variety and far more frequent rotation than most people assume.
- High product margin (~80% gross) is cited as the specific enabler that let them out-spend competitors on paid ad acquisition compared to businesses with thinner (10-20%) margins.

### Process / steps described
1. Validate a food-adjacent product cheaply and fast at farmers markets before investing in e-commerce/advertising — described as the lowest-cost, fastest way to test demand for a food-related product.
2. Give out free samples made identically to the actual product (minimal toppings/sauce to highlight the core product itself, e.g. the dough) — considered close to essential for physical/food products, though the founder notes he tested selling without samples too and found demand from a pre-existing enthusiast subculture (home outdoor-pizza-oven hobbyists already at these markets) made it roughly "50/50" whether samples were strictly necessary.
3. Attend markets consistently and repeatedly (3-4x per week) to build a returning customer base and gather constant real-time product feedback from strangers.
4. Once validated at markets, shift to online/paid social ads to scale beyond the physical throughput limit of in-person markets — but expect an early unprofitable "learning" period even with an outsourced agency; direct, hands-on ownership of ad strategy is described as the actual unlock.
5. Support the paid kit product with a free companion educational offering (a step-by-step masterclass course and live customer support/chat/phone line) to reduce buyer drop-off/failure and increase repeat purchases — framed as revenue-supporting, not a separate revenue line itself.
6. For choosing what to sell at these markets: recommends reselling food-*adjacent* products or equipment over selling prepared food directly, since prepared food requires far more capital and licensing (cites a rough $25,000 threshold to start an actual food-selling operation, e.g. a food truck/pizza operation) versus a packaged product with lower barriers.

### Regulatory notes
- Legal treatment of samples vs. sales varies but is broadly consistent across most states checked (Texas and Georgia cited specifically as functionally identical): giving away free samples of a food item is generally not regulated the same way as *selling* prepared food; selling actual prepared food (e.g., pizza slices) at a market effectively classifies the seller as a food truck/restaurant, triggering food handler permits and other restaurant-level regulation. Pennsylvania was noted as an outlier with unusually strict rules.
- Non-refrigerated/non-perishable packaged goods (e.g. dry pizza kits) generally required less licensing than perishable prepared foods in the founder's experience.

### Other farmers-market side hustles observed/mentioned as successful in the same ecosystem
- Baked goods sellers: consistently cited as some of the highest-earning, fastest-selling vendor category, especially skilled home bakers who reliably sell out.
- A vendor selling homemade pickles.
- A vendor selling fresh (refrigerated, cooler-transported) pasta who built a loyal, repeat-customer following across multiple markets, eventually running "an army" of staff attending different markets simultaneously; some of her customers refused to buy the same product online even when offered, preferring only to buy in-person at the market ("I don't trust the internet").
- Host's own planned/announced side project (not yet launched at time of recording): a powder drink mix ("Busy Juice") to be sold at a local farmers market using the same kit/bag form factor and roughly the same $30 price point / ~70% margin, with the intent to livestream the process for content.

### Caveats / risks
- Not every farmers market is a good venue — quality varies significantly; markets held within city limits (with active municipal marketing support, aimed at driving townwide foot traffic) were found to generally outperform markets held outside city limits or poorly marketed roadside markets.
- Farmers markets have a hard physical throughput ceiling ("you can only scale farmers markets so much") — treated explicitly as a proof-of-concept/testing phase rather than the end-state scaling channel; e-commerce/paid ads are positioned as the necessary next phase for real scale.
- E-commerce/paid ad scaling is not "press play and get rich" — required real, sustained personal effort to learn Facebook ads properly; the founder's own outsourced-agency attempt underperformed for months before he took direct ownership of the marketing.
