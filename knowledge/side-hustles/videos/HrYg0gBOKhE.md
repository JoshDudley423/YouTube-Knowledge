# How To Increase Your Short Term Rental Occupancy
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HrYg0gBOKhE | Published: 2024-04-01

Live call-in Q&A episode (Chris Koerner coaching several different callers on their own businesses/ideas). Six distinct segments, each with its own business/idea and advice — notes broken out by caller/topic.

## Segment 1: Real estate investing newsletter vs. local "Joy List"-style newsletter (caller: Carter)
- Idea A: A real estate investing newsletter modeled on "Sean P's five tweets of the week" format — curate a few popular real estate tweets, add personal-story/humor commentary, with the end goal of eventually raising capital from readers to buy real estate (an LP-raising funnel, not an ad-revenue play).
- Idea B: A local "Joy List"-style newsletter (modeled on an actual NYC newsletter business, The Joy List, focused on curating fun local events to help people make friends) adapted to the caller's own city (Phoenix) — appealing because it addresses a real personal pain point (wanting to make friends locally), but the caller had already found the reference business only charges ~$100/ad despite ~7,500 subscribers, suggesting weak monetization.
- Chris's advice: favor the real estate investing newsletter. Reasoning: per-subscriber monetization value is vastly higher for an investor-focused audience (potentially "100 to 1,000x" the value of a generic local-interest subscriber, since local newsletter ad rates are worth maybe $0.50-$2/subscriber/month) — the real value isn't ad revenue but converting an engaged, trust-built audience into capital-raising relationships over months/years. A Twitter/newsletter presence built around real estate investing is described as one of the best possible uses of an audience specifically for raising money from investors, because readers build trust in you passively over a long period before ever being asked to invest.
- Tactical guidance for the real estate newsletter: keep humor present but secondary — leaning too hard into comedy (cited pattern seen from other Twitter accounts) undermines being taken seriously enough to raise money later. Add original commentary/analysis in every issue (not just curated tweets) to demonstrate real expertise. To avoid seeming like you're overclaiming expertise (the caller only owns 7 units, feels that's not "guru" level), position yourself not as "I own X units" but as "someone who looks at real estate investing in an interesting way" or has achieved unusually good returns via a specific strategy — sidestep an unwinnable comparison to mega-accounts (e.g., Grant Cardone) by finding a differentiated angle instead.

## Segment 2: Boosting short-term rental (STR) occupancy via a fly-fishing niche brand (caller: Jimmy)
- Context: caller owns 3 cabins (1 in Blue Ridge, GA — walking distance to the Toccoa River; 2 in Pigeon Forge/Gatlinburg, TN), fairly standard amenities (hot tub, pool table — self-rated "6/10" on amenities), occupancy has dropped post-COVID-oversaturation: Blue Ridge ~50-60%, Pigeon Forge ~70-80% (highly seasonal — near 0% in Jan/Feb, ~100% in December).
- Caller's own idea: build a direct-booking website and market the Blue Ridge cabin specifically around fly fishing (an outdoor-centric area with no competing franchises/attractions), partnering with local fishing guides for cross-promotion, with a long-term ambition to build a broader "fly fishing brand" across multiple properties (e.g., adding a location in Idaho) potentially sellable to an outdoor brand like Orvis or Sims.
- Chris's step-by-step recommendation:
  1. **Step one — direct booking site + SEO content engine**: build a dedicated direct-booking website targeting low-competition, high-intent long-tail keywords (2-6 words) related to fly fishing/fishing in the Blue Ridge area. Use AI-driven automated content generation to publish keyword-rich articles on a regular cadence to build organic search traffic over time — cited reference: "Unlimited Content" (a business by Steven Oman/Sam Thompson) that fully automates SEO content generation, explicitly marketed as "80-90% of the SEO benefit for 80-90% less cost," described favorably as a business model that can't be disrupted by further AI advancement since it already leverages AI. Referenced influencers/educators on this direct-booking strategy: Isaac French, Richard Fertig (Stomp Capital) — Fertig specifically is credited as an inspiration for a similar fly-fishing/niche-outdoor-sport STR strategy, and is noted to derive over 50% of his STR revenue from ancillary services (not just bookings) — he's done a comparable windsurfing-focused build in the Outer Banks.
  2. **Step two — build a local fly-fishing marketplace as a separate, disguised lead-gen play**: launch a standalone local marketplace site (using Sharetribe or a similar marketplace-software platform) listing local fly-fishing shops/guides within a 2-hour radius (found via Outscraper), initially with no mention of the caller's own cabins. Drive fly fishermen to it via paid ads. Once relationships with fishing shops/guides are established (starting free — "enjoy these free leads, we'll figure out pricing later"), monetize either by charging shops/guides a per-lead fee or percentage of revenue, OR (a more creative alternative Chris proposes) flip the incentive: pay guides/shops a referral commission (e.g., 10% of a resulting cabin booking, ~$50-$200) for recommending the caller's cabins to their own clients — this over-delivers value to the guides (free leads + being paid) and builds strong incentive alignment.
  3. Chris explicitly frames this as a scaling opportunity, not just an occupancy fix, given the caller has expressed interest in building new businesses — but validates starting with the lower-effort occupancy fix first, since it can be executed "next month."

## Segment 3: Growing a stagnant video production business (caller: unnamed, San Antonio, TX)
- Context: 7-year-old video production company, revenue has flattened; tried cold email, LinkedIn outreach, Facebook/Google ads without success. Currently doing some remote/Upwork work (lower-value projects — animated explainers, motion graphics, editing) alongside higher-value corporate on-site work (interviews, testimonials) ranging $1,500-$15,000/project; average Upwork project ~$500, typically 10 hours of editing at $50-60/hour to build up profile/reviews. Recently hired someone full-time just to submit Upwork proposals (~10/day, ignoring sub-$250 or under-$30/hr jobs), converting roughly 1 in 10-15 proposals.
- Niches the business has found success in: tech/cybersecurity companies (from a network built while working at Rackspace) and large healthcare clients (hundreds/thousands of clinics) — valued specifically because large clients don't haggle over the value of professional video work. Recently expanded into animated explainer videos (good margin, high demand) via newly hired motion-graphics staff. Explicitly avoiding weddings and TikTok/Shorts/creator-style recurring content work as not fitting the business's existing skillset/positioning.
- Chris's recommendations, in priority order:
  1. **Improve Upwork proposal quality with deep personalized research + AI-personalized video** (highest priority, immediately actionable): have the dedicated Upwork-proposal staffer spend 5-10 minutes per application researching the prospect beyond the job post itself — visiting the company's website/profile, and specifically checking the Wayback Machine (web.archive.org) for things the company used to offer but no longer does, then referencing that specific detail by name in the outreach ("Hey [name], why aren't you offering X anymore?"). Chris notes from his own extensive hiring experience on Upwork (1,000+ job posts since it was called oDesk) that vendors who do this kind of external research and reference it consistently stand out and get hired — explicit confirmation bias/flattery effect. Combine this with an AI tool that can swap out the opening few seconds of a templated video proposal (inserting the prospect's name/company) so each proposal appears personalized in the crucial first 5 seconds (when most viewers decide whether to keep watching), while the rest of the video proposal stays a reusable template. Goal: raise the proposal acceptance rate from ~10% to 20-30%.
  2. **Run a persistent outbound cold email campaign** (sending to a scraped, geographically/industry-targeted list) at volume — 50-200 cold emails/day to complete strangers, framed as "always be sending" (a spin on "always be closing"). Rationale: even with a much lower response rate than warmer channels, consistent outbound volume guarantees eventual hits, some of which convert into recurring clients — described as "always being at bat" rather than sitting on the bench waiting for inbound.
  3. **Behind-the-scenes/BTS content on TikTok/Instagram** (e.g., showing how specific shots are made) — explicitly ranked as the lowest-priority of the three growth levers, a "nice to have" rather than a core strategy.
  4. On the caller's own side idea — leasing office space in a large multi-tenant building to build a small podcast/video studio as a lead-gen tool (potentially partnering with the landlord as a building "amenity," similar to a fitness center): Chris endorsed this, suggesting a flexible, month-to-month "WeWork knockoff" style private studio rental (not shared/coworking) targeted at local entrepreneurship groups (people who either already have a podcast or are about to start one) as both a stand-alone revenue stream (rentable by the hour, flat monthly rate, or with bundled editing services) AND a natural lead-gen channel for the core video production business, plus usable as the caller's own office when not rented out.

## Segment 4: Raising capital to buy a local shipping/mailbox store (caller: unnamed)
- Deal specifics: a local shipping/mailing store (mailbox rentals, package shipping, passport photos; authorized shipper for UPS/USPS/DHL/FedEx) 2 minutes from the caller's home, owner wants to retire, asking $660,000 cash (later referenced as "$60,000" — likely a transcription/mishearing; based on the 2x-profit-multiple math later in the conversation, the actual price is $60,000, not $660,000), owner will not do seller financing (been burned before). Financials: ~$142,000/year topline revenue, ~25% net margin (~$35,000/year net profit) averaged over the last 3 years. 90 mailboxes at ~80% occupancy (room to add more); pricing is below competitor averages (room to raise prices). One weak nearby competitor (1.5-star rating, ~25 reviews) vs. the target's stronger position (4.5 stars, ~25 reviews, better shopping-center location with high foot traffic — Kroger, Planet Fitness nearby). Caller does not have a W-2 (works contract/commission sales), wants to buy the whole deal with financing rather than using savings, has no prior business-ownership track record (a concern for SBA loan qualification and financing timeline — seller has other buyers already circling and wants a faster close than an SBA loan process typically allows).
- Chris's core advice: **do not buy this business.** Reasoning:
  1. Applies a "10 out of 10 execution on a 1 out of 10 opportunity" framing (attributed to a "My First Million" podcast episode) — even flawless execution on this specific deal caps out at a relatively small, capital- and time-intensive outcome (a ~$35K/year profit ceiling business requiring being physically open and staffed Monday-Saturday) for a similar or greater amount of effort that could be redirected toward an opportunity with 2-20x the upside.
  2. Chris draws on his own past experience owning retail businesses (iPhone repair shops) as directly comparable and something he "hated" — cites the stress of low-ceiling, in-person retail ownership ("picking pennies up in front of a steamroller," dealing with angry customers face-to-face) as a cautionary parallel.
  3. Specific alternative advice: the caller explicitly enjoys and is skilled at sales (currently commission-based contract sales) — Chris recommends leaning fully into that skill rather than pivoting into unrelated business ownership, e.g., selling much higher-ticket items (contrasts selling a "$10-30/month widget" against something like life insurance, which can pay commissions in the hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars/year with enough grind) rather than diluting a strong, hard-to-acquire sales skill set into running a low-ceiling retail operation.
  4. Suggests building a track record through lower-risk, faster-cycle activities first — e.g., flipping undervalued items (cars via Facebook Marketplace, or general flipping) — to build capital and a demonstrable record BEFORE trying to raise money for a bigger venture, and to make sure that eventual bigger venture is itself sales-oriented (leveraging the caller's real strength) rather than operations-heavy retail.
  5. Broader point: home-service businesses were floated as an example of a bigger opportunity category that doesn't require raising outside capital at all, as an alternative direction worth exploring instead of a capital-intensive retail acquisition.
  6. Explicitly names the sunk-cost fallacy risk: the caller has already invested emotional energy (talked to the seller, built spreadsheets) which Chris flags as a reason to be extra skeptical of continuing down this path rather than a reason to commit.

## Segment 5: AI video-generation software for mom-and-pop businesses (caller: representing "Deep Cognition," a Dallas-based AI company)
- Context: company (originally built "Deep Learning Studio," an AI development platform) is building customizable AI video-generation software, initially targeting mom-and-pop businesses (restaurants, motels) doing a few million dollars/year in revenue, to auto-generate promotional/social-ready videos from a business's own images/content (e.g., pulling from their website, menu, specials) for posting to Instagram/Reels.
- Chris's specific growth-hack idea (a cold-outreach/demo-as-marketing mechanism): build a bot/scraper (in-house or via an outsourced VA) that scans a target market of restaurants/hotels, pulls businesses with a strong volume of Google Business Profile reviews and photos, and auto-generates a promotional video FOR them using their own reviews/photos as an unsolicited demo — then sends it directly to the business (e.g., via text to the phone number on their Google Business Profile, if it's a cell number) as a cold outreach/lead-gen tactic.
- The specific psychological hook proposed: deliberately include a negative element in the demo video near the beginning (e.g., feature the business's actual 1-star review prominently) rather than sending a flawlessly polished, purely positive video. Rationale: a perfectly pleasant, unremarkable video is unlikely to prompt any response at all, whereas an unexpected/provocative element (seeing their own negative review highlighted) triggers an emotional reaction and drives a reply — likened to the psychology behind what makes tweets/videos go viral (needing something that provokes comment, not just polish). Suggests being transparent about the tactic once contacted, and having a second, fully polished "real" demo video ready to send immediately once the business responds, to convert the attention into a genuine sales conversation.
- Framed by Chris as a potentially "super scalable growth hack" for customer acquisition if built to run at scale (bot-driven, personalized-at-scale outreach) rather than a one-off gimmick.

## Segment 6: Alaska side-hustle triage — Airbnb vs. Turo vs. Shopify skincare e-commerce (caller: Anchorage, AK)
- Context: caller works full-time at a credit union; runs 3 side businesses with his wife — Airbnb (renting part of a duplex they live in), Turo (car-sharing/rental — 1 vehicle), and a Shopify skincare e-commerce business — trying to identify which to scale into a full-time replacement income, since none currently feels like "the one." Self-ranked preference: Turo (most loved) > Shopify (medium) > Airbnb (least loved, largely because it means sharing their own living space with strangers).
- Turo specifics: 1 vehicle (a Chevy Traverse, since sold), ran at ~80-90% occupancy during peak summer season, pulled off the platform entirely during winter (weather/liability/windshield-damage risk from being street-parked). Planning to buy a new vehicle (Kia Telluride, ~$35,000) to replace it, anticipating ~$220-250/day rental rate (vs. $150-180/day for the older Traverse) at a 10% Turo platform fee. A conservative model (rented at $220/day net of fees ≈ $200/day, over a 4-month/~120-day peak season, at 80% occupancy) projects ~$19,000/season gross for a single $35,000 vehicle — roughly a 2-year payback period before accounting for gas, insurance, off-season storage.
- Chris's recommendation: **do the market-research homework before deciding to pivot to something new — lean into Turo, since it's already the caller's favorite and already profitable.** Concrete research method proposed (a technique Chris says he personally used before buying and renting out RVs on RV Share/Outdoorsy):
  1. Spend 2-3 hours manually running Turo searches in the local market (Anchorage) across multiple future dates (a weekend each in early May, June, July, August, September) with no filters, logging in a spreadsheet how many listings/results come back for each date — this reveals overall seasonal demand shape.
  2. Repeat the same searches with narrower filters (e.g., SUV only, 3 years old or newer, certain mileage cap, 4WD only) to isolate demand and pricing specifically for the vehicle type/segment being considered, and compare far-future dates (e.g., August) against near-term dates (e.g., "next weekend") to gauge how demand/pricing changes as availability tightens.
  3. Record average rental price for each filtered segment alongside result counts, to build a hard-data-based (not speculative) model of vehicle-type demand and achievable pricing in the specific local market — described as "spreadsheet forecasting based on hard data... that probably no one else in the market is doing."
  4. Personal case study Chris shares as precedent: he did this exact exercise for Class C RVs in the Northeast Dallas-Fort Worth market before buying his own RV to rent on RV Share, discovering specific nuances (e.g., an RV that's too long intimidates renters, one too short doesn't fit families) that led to a real 2008 RV purchase with a ~3-month payback period; he later shared his research directly with an RV Share executive, who reportedly confirmed the findings matched their internal backend data.
  5. Explicit principle stated twice in this segment: "if you're going to have one rental house, you might as well have 100; if you're going to have one Turo, you might as well have 100" — a single-unit rental/sharing-economy asset business is inherently hard to make meaningful money from, so the real decision is whether the local market can support scaling to multiple vehicles (5-10+), including the added costs of off-season storage and insurance, which should be modeled into the same spreadsheet framework across a range of occupancy scenarios (10%, 40%, 50%, 80%, 90%, 100%) before committing capital.
  6. General advice: don't jump to a totally different, unproven business idea ("the grass is always greener") when an existing side business is already profitable and personally preferred — validate its scalability with real market data first, then revisit other ideas only if the data doesn't support scaling it.

## Caveats / risks
- The real estate newsletter's "raise capital from readers" business model depends entirely on long-term trust-building (months to years) — it is explicitly not a fast-monetization play.
- The STR fly-fishing marketplace idea requires successfully building two-sided trust (guides/shops AND consumers) similarly to any cold-start marketplace — no specific mitigation beyond starting the guide/shop side for free is discussed.
- The video production business's Upwork/cold-email growth tactics are explicitly framed as incremental (Chris: "it's meaningful... but it's a lot of effort that might equal one of your bigger corporate clients") — not a full fix for the caller's stated goal of predictable large-scale growth; Chris separately flags that generic explainer-video-to-companies-worldwide is a market segment "AI is going to crush," implying the caller's higher-value corporate/local service niche is the safer long-term bet over broad remote freelance competition.
- The shipping-store acquisition segment is presented as a clear "don't do this" case study — flagged risks include a hard revenue/profit ceiling, the operational demands of a physically-staffed retail location, lack of seller financing flexibility, and the caller's absence of a business-ownership track record complicating financing options like SBA loans.
- The AI video-generation cold-outreach growth hack (using a business's own negative review as an attention hook) is explicitly a deliberately provocative/borderline tactic — Chris frames it as needing transparency once the business responds, to avoid it reading as purely a bait-and-switch.
- The Turo/RV rental scaling advice explicitly warns that a single-vehicle sharing-economy business has a structurally low ceiling and meaningful seasonal/storage/insurance costs that must be modeled before assuming linear scaling economics.
