# I Tested This "Genius" Side Hustle (Is It A Goldmine?)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FFYu4JQ7dqw | Published: 2026-01-01

Chris Koerner field-tests a "game of skill" hole-in-one-style challenge business, inspired by a floating golf green in New Zealand that reportedly pays out $10,000 for a hole-in-one and nets ~$500,000/year — recreated using disc golf.

## The business idea
- A pay-to-play skill challenge: charge a small entry fee for a low-probability chance at a large cash prize (same underlying model as hole-in-one golf contests, "guess the number of jellybeans," carnival games, etc.). Business only works if the payout structure is calibrated so expected value strongly favors the house across many attempts ("the house always wins over the long run," same principle as a casino).
- Concept is generalizable beyond disc golf: suggested variants include a pull-up/hang-time endurance challenge, throwing a football through a ring, or a half-court basketball shot challenge.

## Test 1 — Clyde Warren Park, Dallas (failed)
- Setup: $10 for 3 throws at a disc golf basket from 65 ft, $1,000 prize for a make.
- Result: park security shut it down almost immediately — the park does not allow charging money or giving away cash on-site (had to move to the surrounding public street, where foot traffic was too low to get participants). Net revenue: $0. One free trial throw was given (no charge) after security intervened.
- Key operational lesson: **verify local venue rules/permits before setup** — a location can look ideal (high foot traffic, public park) but still legally prohibit cash games/prizes.
- Lesson on difficulty calibration: 65 ft was judged too easy/risky for the payout — a skilled recreational disc golfer ("a hipster millennial who plays disc golf 20 hours a week") could realistically make that shot, which would blow the economics. Concluded the distance should be more like 75–90 ft for a $10-for-1,000 payout structure.
- Also found that most passersby in a general public park had never played disc golf and didn't understand the game, reducing willing participants regardless of price.

## Test 2 — DFW Disc Golf shop, Arlington, TX (successful trial)
- Pivoted to running the challenge at an actual disc golf pro shop/course to reach people who already play (higher participation rate, but also higher skill/make-rate — an explicit tradeoff).
- Setup: $10 for one throw, $20 for three throws, from 95 ft; owner (David) agreed to let customers try in exchange for donating 100% of proceeds to his nonprofit (HDNP International, homeless services).
- Ran roughly 9 full rounds / ~180 total throws across a mix of skill levels (complete beginners to sponsored/tournament players). Total money collected: over $180 (a specific tally of $100 was noted partway through, growing thereafter).
- One player, Matt Harris (self-described "moderate" skill, plays tournaments), made the 95 ft ace and won the $1,000 prize.
- After the prize was paid, the remaining pooled entry money ($780 net after the prize) went to the nonprofit; framed in-video as "70 sleeping bags" worth of donation impact.

## Numbers / economics discussed
- Approximate cost basis for testing: a handful of discs ($12–$25 each; different discs — putter, mid-range, driver — suit different distances/throw styles).
- At 95 ft with a real disc golf audience, the operator can plausibly run at a slight loss or breakeven on a single "viral hole-in-one" hit, but the format is designed to profit heavily over volume once distance/skill/price are correctly calibrated (this session wasn't optimized for profit — proceeds were donated).
- Recommended target pricing floated at the end: ~$5 for one throw / $10 for three throws, calibrated against "someone at least somewhat proficient" — noted that far more people know how to swing a golf club than know proper disc golf throwing technique, which affects difficulty calibration versus a real hole-in-one golf contest.

## How to actually run this as a business (Chris's takeaways)
- Treat viability as a pure math/statistics problem, not a subjective "is this a good idea" question: once you know (a) the make-probability at a given distance for a given skill level, and (b) achievable entry price, you can directly calculate expected margin — "much simpler than asking if almost any other business is viable."
- Three variables to solve for before charging real money: distance, target skill level of participants, and price point.
- Recommended launch method: **run it for free first** to gather real make/attempt data (distance vs. skill vs. hit rate) before committing to a payout structure — treat the free runs as a controlled experiment, not lost revenue.
- Go-to-market: partner with existing disc golf tournaments, disc golf shops/pro shops, disc golf equipment brands, and disc golf apps/communities to access a concentrated audience of already-skilled players, and position the challenge as a "side game"/carnival attraction rather than trying to attract cold general-public foot traffic.

## Caveats / risks
- Legal/permit risk: public parks and similar municipal spaces may flatly prohibit cash games or prize giveaways on-site, regardless of legitimate intent (charity framing didn't matter to Clyde Warren Park security).
- Distance/prize calibration is the single biggest risk factor — set the bar too easy relative to the payout and a single skilled player can wipe out the house edge; set it in front of the wrong audience (non-players) and no one participates at all.
- The one live money-collecting run shown in the video was explicitly not run for personal profit (proceeds donated to charity), so the video doesn't provide a real-world profit/loss track record — the "$500K/year" figure quoted for the New Zealand inspiration is secondhand and unverified in the video.
