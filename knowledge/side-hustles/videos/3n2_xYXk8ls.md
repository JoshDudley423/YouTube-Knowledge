# How to Find HUGE Customers
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3n2_xYXk8ls | Published: 2024-05-09

Keynote speech ("You Only Need One White Whale") given by Chris Koerner at SMBash (small/medium business acquisition conference). Framework for finding one outsized customer/business/deal that changes your financial trajectory, aimed mainly at people buying or already owning small businesses — but the tactics (finding contact info, landing huge customers, negotiating) generalize to any side hustle trying to land a big client.

## Core framework: "White Whale" strategy
- A "white whale" = one business acquisition, customer, or deal disproportionate to your current scale that could fully change your life/trajectory (e.g., buying a $1.5M EBITDA HVAC business instead of a $500K one; landing one customer worth 100x your average customer).
- Thesis: pursuing 20% more risk/time/money for a target with 200%+ more upside beats "safe," typical-sized deals — most people (95%/80%) default to conventional-sized targets due to social proof/comfort, not because it's optimal.
- 5-step process:
  1. **Identify** all your past and future white whales (reflect on big past risks you've already taken to build confidence, then define your next big target — e.g., "there are 36 HVAC businesses at $500K EBITDA in Omaha; the 7 above $1.5M are your white whales").
  2. **Locate** the target's real contact info — not the office/fax line but the owner's personal cell/home address.
  3. **Generate an offer ("kill" the whale)** — creative but simple, since a business at that scale won't sell to just anyone with a form buy-box.
  4. **Execute until failure or success** — persist, but pivot to the next target rather than obsessing (fatal error) once a deal is clearly dead.
  5. **Rinse and repeat.**
- Your target number should be uncomfortable/scary but not bankrupting — "not just a goal, needs to make you sweat."
- Work least-important to most-important target first on your list, to practice technique before your top pick.

## Tools/tactics for finding a seller/customer's real contact info
- **CPAs** — go to accountants who serve many business owners in your target category ("hire one to hire ten"); ask directly if they know owners nearing retirement who might sell.
- **Upwork freelancers with access to TLO (TransUnion's TLOxp)** — paid skip-tracing data source; hire a freelancer rather than getting your own account.
- **Search Bug** — mass phone number validation (landline/VoIP/cell), ~3 cents per number.
- **Outscraper** — scrapes business listings in bulk from Yelp, Google Maps, etc.
- **TruthFinder** — cheap/light background-check service.
- **Slybroadcast** — sends ringless voicemail drops to a list of numbers without the phone ringing; ~$20 for ~400 numbers cited.
- **EmailSherlock** — reverse email lookup (upload an email, get other identifying info).
- **PhoneValidator** — same as Search Bug but for single lookups.
- **"Skip tracing" services generally** — same idea as TLO but for bulk lists.
- **Social platforms for finding low-competition contact points**: Strava (comment on activity where they're not competing for attention), YouTube comments on other people's videos, Goodreads, TripAdvisor (author found a seller by his frequent review activity), paid LinkedIn ads targeted at a specific named individual (example: someone ran an ad with their own face directed at a specific hiring manager by name to get referred for a job).
- **PayPal / Cash App / Venmo micro-payment trick**: bulk-upload a list of target emails to PayPal and send each a tiny amount (2-5 cents); if they don't have a PayPal account, PayPal itself nags them repeatedly to sign up to claim the money, effectively outsourcing your follow-up. Works the same via Cash App/Venmo.
- **Reverse username search**: Google a person's known username in quotes to surface every other platform/forum/message board (including Reddit) they use it on.
- **Twitter/X Advanced Search**: bookmark the advanced search page, search for their contact info being posted publicly.
- **Cold-guessing email addresses**: example given — found John McAfee's personal email in 2018 by guessing common formats (e.g., "jmcafee@...") after every paid tool failed; used it to get a meeting and co-founded a crypto business with him that scaled to 60,000 customers before crashing with the crypto market.

## Negotiation/offer tactics ("killing" the whale)
- Never ask a seller for their "bottom dollar" directly — once stated, their pride locks them to it and they won't come down further. Instead ask "what's a ballpark?" — leaves room to negotiate above a number that isn't explicitly their anchored floor.
- Use **flattery** — compliment specific, researched details about their business (not generic praise) — check web.archive.org for the history of their website/business decisions to build genuine specific knowledge before the call.
- Build **friendship/rapport before the offer** — people give better deals to friends than strangers; play a long game.
- **Tell your personal story** (why you want this specific business) rather than reciting generic buy-box criteria — makes the seller relate to you as a person.
- The offer itself should be **creative but simple** — overly complex structures (JV + equity buyback + earnout) make sellers suspicious/confused; keep it to something they can understand in one sitting.
- **"False confidence is confidence"** — acting the part of a qualified buyer while being transparent about actual limitations (no track record, but committed capital and effort) can win deals against much larger/more experienced competing bidders (cited example: buyer beat out 28 other bidders for a Florida events business despite it being bigger than he could obviously afford, partly because the seller was also the landlord and wanted a workable long-term relationship).

## Case studies cited (numbers)
- **Medical billing business (business partner "Nick")**: bought in Boise, ID for $3M; sold ~2 years later for $7M after giving a hired operator 10% (Nick never operated it himself).
- **Bitcoin mining facility acquisition**: found two similarly-sized (2.5 MW) mining facilities for sale on BizBuySell after China banned Bitcoin mining (creating a wave of US buyers); one listed in Oregon at $3M, another near the speaker's home listed at $750K. Negotiated the $750K listing down to $700K with only $50K cash down — seller financed the remaining $650K, with loan repayments tied to the price of Bitcoin (payments dropped toward zero as Bitcoin price fell). Facility was later sold for a "healthy profit."
- **Buc-ee's reselling e-commerce business**: noticed Buc-ee's (a ~$2B company at the time, only ~40 locations) had no online store. Cold-emailed the CEO offering to build/manage e-commerce for them — no response. Pivoted to launching it independently: spent ~$2,000 buying "one of everything" at a physical Buc-ee's, built a website reselling the products, generated press coverage by emailing reporters, which got Buc-ee's legal counsel to reach out. Buc-ee's said they didn't want to run e-commerce themselves and gave a green light to resell at full retail markup under the legal "first sale doctrine" (can resell any company's product, just can't impersonate the company — required adding disclaimers). Business has run 4 years, does "mid seven figures" in revenue, profitable, still the only site dedicated to reselling Buc-ee's merchandise.
- **iPhone repair store (personal, 2010)**: signed a 5-year lease with personal guarantee to open a storefront while in college, against mentors' advice to start small in a dorm room; described as a successful (if risky) white whale bet that "made a lot of money for a 20-something."
- **Relocation for scale (2014)**: business based in Alabama had all customers within a 1-day shipping drive; realized few big metro areas were reachable from Alabama, moved the whole family/business to Dallas to access more metros within 1-day shipping — business grew substantially afterward.

## Caveats
- Explicitly a mindset/prospecting framework, not a specific side-hustle idea — no product/market is prescribed; it's meant to be applied to whatever acquisition or big-customer target the listener already has in mind.
- Speaker acknowledges failures too: a failed Kickstarter, a family road trip business idea that fell apart at the Serbian border — the framework doesn't guarantee success, and picking the wrong motivation (analogized to Captain Ahab's vengeance-driven pursuit in Moby Dick) or failing to pivot away from a dead deal is called out as the main failure mode.
