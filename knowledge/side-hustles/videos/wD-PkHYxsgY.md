# Make $20K/Month Selling to Clients Who Don't Flinch at Price
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wD-PkHYxsgY | Published: 2025-11-01

Chris Koerner interviews repeat guest Samuel Thompson (X: @ImSamThompson, unlimitedcontent.com, hummingbirdfood.co) about a live case study: a $29 ebook ("How to Win Your Divorce") used as a low-cost lead-gen funnel to sell high-value referrals to divorce lawyers. Core thesis: use a cheap info product to generate near-break-even leads, then monetize the leads on the backend to a professional (lawyer) who can afford to pay far more per lead than a consumer product ever could.

## The core idea / funnel
- Identify a painful, expensive, emotionally-charged market with existing high-value service providers (here: divorce lawyers, avg. $270/hr, total fees typically $7,000-$23,000 per case) who can absorb a high cost-per-lead.
- Wrap a solution/guide to that pain point in a cheap ebook ($29) with a bump offer, sold via a two-step landing page ("funnel").
- Run broad, untargeted Meta/Facebook ads (automatic placements, broad targeting, optimize purely for "purchases" event) — deliberately did no market research beyond asking ChatGPT "how many people a year in the US get divorced" (~2+ million) and "do lawyers pay referral fees, how much" before launching.
- Use the book buyer as a warm lead, then refer a percentage of buyers to a lawyer for a fee — structured as a "consulting fee" rather than a legal referral fee to route around state bar rules restricting non-lawyer referral fees (structuring yourself as a paid consultant to the lawyer, not a lead broker to the client).

## Numbers from the actual test
- Wrote the first ebook draft using ChatGPT in roughly one sitting (author states "the entire book" while watching TV); V1 quality was poor ("AI slop"/placeholder), later revised over ~5 hours to something more substantive (~200 pages).
- Built the initial funnel on Perspective (landing page tool), later migrated to ClickFunnels for better upsell/downsell/bump-offer tooling.
- Ad spend: ~$500-$700 in the first week (~$100/day), generated ~$400 in sales — a loss, but treated as a useful "signal" (~$40 cost to acquire a $29 purchase).
- Added a $17 "negotiation training toolkit" bump offer alongside the $29 book; ~70% of buyers took the bump, pushing average order value (AOV) to ~$42-$48.
- Running roughly at breakeven with CAC (customer acquisition cost) around $40-$45 against that AOV; total sales reached "five or six grand" at the time of the interview.
- Printed 300 physical copies of the book via Printivity (not Printify, which is apparent-only for print-on-demand apparel) at roughly $6/book printing cost, landing around $8.50/book delivered with shipping — done specifically to increase trust/conversion vs. a pure digital PDF (spent roughly $3,000 on this print run).
- Also soft-launched a $300 backend "Financial Protection Protocol" upsell/program (post-divorce financial planning) a few days before pausing ads; no purchase data yet at time of filming.
- Hit a real obstacle: Meta classified the ad account under its "personal hardship" policy, which blocks purchase-event attribution back to the pixel and disables purchase-based optimization — forcing a pause on ads while awaiting Facebook's review, right after spending on the print run.

## Backend monetization / referral math (the $20K/month blueprint)
- Two referral-fee structuring options discussed for monetizing leads to a lawyer, framed as a wholesale/retail arbitrage:
  - Pay-per-signed-client: lawyer pays a flat fee (example used: $1,000) only when a referred lead actually signs a contract — higher payout per conversion but requires trusting the lawyer to self-report closes.
  - Pay-per-lead: lawyer pays a smaller flat fee (example used: $100) per lead delivered regardless of outcome (e.g., "every lead from California") — lower risk/effort for the lead-gen operator (no dependency on lawyer's own closing skill or honesty), lower payout per lead.
  - A third variant: percentage-of-invoice revenue share (e.g., 25-30% of what the lawyer ultimately bills the referred client) — higher upside but higher trust/collection risk since "lawyers don't have the best reputation for being upfront."
- Recommended approach for a solo operator: start with the lower-touch, lower-payout-per-lead model (pay-per-lead) rather than the higher-touch/higher-payout model, specifically to avoid burnout before you know if the underlying idea works; you can always renegotiate to higher rates later once you have real close-rate data.
- Recommended tactic to de-risk lawyer relationships: build trusted data-sharing partnerships with just 2-3 lawyers who will report back what's actually closing, then use that real close-rate data to negotiate/set pricing with lawyers you don't know or trust as well (who won't share close data).
- Illustrative math: sending 100 leads to a lawyer — at $100/lead flat with an 8% close rate, operator nets $10,000 for those 100 leads regardless of case value; if close rate turns out to be 16% via better lawyer/lead quality, that's effectively "left $6,000 on the table" versus a %-of-close structure, prompting a renegotiation or shopping the leads to a different lawyer.
- Reverse-engineered target-profit math example: a 3% "take rate" on the backend referral, combined with breaking even on the top-of-funnel book/ad spend, converting ~300 book-buyers/day, nets roughly $900/day profit (~$27,000/month) — i.e., the entire target profit is a function of (a) getting enough top-of-funnel volume via ads and (b) the back-end conversion/take rate, not complex business mechanics.
- Cost-per-lead arbitrage framing: legal-niche leads on Google Ads can cost as much as ~$5,000 for something like a trucking-accident case; by contrast, funneling leads through a cheap book funded by Facebook ads can produce an effective "lead cost" of well under $100 even while losing money on the book sale itself (e.g., $50 CAC vs. $40 AOV nets a real cost per lead around $10, and even a wider loss like $80/lead net-of-AOV is far cheaper than paying $1,000+ per lead directly via search ads).
- Framed advantage of using books specifically as the lead-gen vehicle (vs. generic Google/Facebook lead-gen, which lawyers are often burned out on and skeptical of): a highly niche book title (e.g., "How does a mom of four win her divorce in New Hampshire") can convert at a much higher rate (~20% on a small niche audience) than a broad, generic book (~2%) — suggested extensions: books targeting DUIs ("Erase Your DUI"), slip-and-fall accidents, domestic abuse situations, car accidents — each paired with the corresponding specialist attorney vertical.

## General business philosophy discussed
- "You don't need employees or big upfront capital to hit $20K/month profit" — framed as a realistic, high-leverage target for someone currently earning a stressed-out W2 salary (~$120K/year cited as a reference point); described as "shrinking your goals" to double income / halve stress via the 80/20 (Pareto) principle, rather than chasing million-dollar-a-year businesses.
- Explicit caution against over-researching before testing: don't rely on generic published benchmarks (e.g., average e-commerce add-to-cart rate or CPC) because they vary wildly by price point and audience (a $6,000 sauna vs. a fashion impulse buy have completely different funnel metrics); the only meaningful comparison is a business's own performance over time (is my CPC improving vs. my own baseline), not comparison to outside "industry average" figures which can wrongly discourage someone from continuing a working funnel.
- Explicit warning against overbuilding complexity/scope out of the gate — start with the simplest, lowest-effort monetization path (e.g., flat pay-per-lead) to avoid burning out before discovering whether the core idea has legs; people fail more often from overcomplicating their MVP than from the idea itself being bad.
- Noted tradeoff/caution: scaling from a $20K/month solo operation to a ~$1M/year, multi-employee business is disproportionately harder than it sounds — roughly "4x more revenue for maybe 2x more profit and 6x more work" once you add headcount/organizational complexity; the hosts describe personally experiencing a ~12-month "slump" where revenue doubled but take-home profit didn't grow, due to reinvestment/overhead from scaling.
- General transferable skillset emphasized across all these plays: copywriting, building a landing page, and running/reading Facebook ads — described as broadly reusable across almost any future business idea once learned via one real launch.

## Caveats
- Legal/regulatory caveat: referral fees for legal leads are restricted in many US states (some require the referrer to be a licensed attorney to receive a referral fee, similar to real estate referral rules) — the workaround discussed is structuring the arrangement as a paid "consulting fee" for services rendered rather than a legal referral fee, though this is presented as a practical workaround, not formal legal advice.
- Platform risk: the Meta "personal hardship" ad classification is an active, unresolved obstacle at time of filming — it blocks purchase-event attribution and ad optimization, illustrating that sensitive-topic ad campaigns (divorce, legal, financial hardship, etc.) carry real platform-policy risk that can halt a funnel with no clear resolution timeline.
- Success depends on real backend execution: the referral-fee arrangement only works if a trustworthy, high-converting lawyer relationship is found and actively managed (chasing close-rate data, renegotiating pricing) — a purely "set and forget" pay-per-lead model is lower risk but leaves money on the table if the lawyer converts well.
