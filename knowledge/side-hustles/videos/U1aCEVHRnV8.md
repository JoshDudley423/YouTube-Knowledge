# Exactly How to Validate Your Business Idea for $15
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U1aCEVHRnV8 | Published: 2024-07-08

Live call-in Q&A episode, five separate caller segments, each a distinct business/idea.

## Segment 1: Validating a pack-and-ship store market (market research method — the "$15" referenced in the title)
- Caller wanted to open an independent pack-and-ship store (mailbox/shipping store), budget ~$50,000-$60,000, in Indianapolis, open to relocating.
- Step-by-step market validation process taught (applied by host to his own iPhone-repair store 15 years earlier, replicated across 4 locations, all successful, later sold):
  1. Ask ChatGPT for US cities demographically comparable to your target city (similar population/income) — e.g., Columbus, OH as a comp for Indianapolis.
  2. Go to **outscraper.com**, use its Google Maps scraping feature to scrape all pack-and-ship stores in the comp city *and* its outlying suburbs (search suburb-by-suburb, not just the core city). Cost: ~$10, takes about an hour.
  3. Clean the data — remove non-competitors (FedEx, Staples, UPS Store chains) and keep only independent stores.
  4. Get each store's ZIP code, cross-reference with ZIP-code population data (Google search) to compute stores-per-capita by ZIP.
  5. Layer in demographic data per ZIP (site mentioned: "citystatedata.com" or similar) — crime rate, average income, population density.
  6. Identify undersaturated ZIP codes (low stores-per-capita) as candidate target areas.
  7. Call the existing stores in those ZIPs pretending to be a customer; ask about mailbox availability, hours, pricing — use ChatGPT to generate probing questions. Be bold; you'll never talk to them again.
  8. Cross-reference collected call data against demographics to find patterns (e.g., "high-crime ZIPs are busier" or "high population density = more volume") and find gaps/holes in the market.
  9. Check each competitor's web presence: Wayback Machine (web archive) and WHOIS.com to see how old each store's website/business is; read online reviews and their dates to gauge store age/health.
  10. This ~half-day of research yields more market knowledge than anyone else has for that metro's pack-and-ship industry.
  11. Take the resulting "baseline" profile (e.g., "50,000 people within a 1-hour radius, avg income $40K, crime rate 4%") and apply it to a similar city a few hours from the original target to find where a new store is 70-80% likely to succeed.
  12. When leasing, negotiate a **short-term lease with an option to extend** so you can exit within a year if it underperforms, without being locked into 4+ years.
- Host's personal opinion/caveat: he personally dislikes the pack-and-ship/retail model due to high capital requirement ($50-60K) relative to opportunity cost, and dislikes customer-facing retail generally (introvert). Alternative suggestions for the same budget: home service businesses with low overhead (gutter cleaning, landscaping, tree trimming), or buying a low-cost, fast-growing franchise not yet present in your area (research via entrepreneur.com).

## Segment 2: Local newsletter business (Winnipeg Digest) — growth & monetization
- Caller: 23-year-old, runs "Winnipeg Digest," a local newsletter modeled after Ryan Sedlack-style local newsletters (e.g., Naptown, Capital Daily).
- **Numbers**: started ~3 months prior; grew from ~150 (organic/Reddit) subscribers to 500 in a month running paid ads at **~$0.25 CAD per subscriber** (called very good/below-average cost). Instagram grew to 8,000 followers in one month posting daily "reels" about local events. Newsletter sent 3x/week via **Beehiiv**. Open rate ~53-54%, click rate ~4-12% depending on focus, end-of-email poll response rate ~50-55 out of ~1,500 opens (called a strong poll rate).
- Monetization so far: 4-5 sponsors, using a graduated-discount approach (first 3 sponsors get 80% off, next 3 get 60% off, next get 40% off, then full price). Sponsor pricing benchmarked around **$100 CPM** but most local businesses don't understand CPM, so sell it as a flat package instead.
- Host's advice:
  - Building Instagram audience first gives "local authority" that makes cold outreach to businesses easier.
  - Reverse-engineer an established comparable newsletter (Capital Daily, founded by Andrew Wilkinson) — specifically look at their **first ~20 issues** to see which sponsor types they picked first (chronologically) as a signal of what actually converts/has good ROI in this format.
  - Growth-hack idea: approach local businesses with big underused email lists (e.g., an auto repair shop with 20,000-23,000 customer emails) and propose a mutually free/discounted sponsorship swap — you promote them in your newsletter, they let you email their list once (with an opt-out) to convert some into subscribers. Framing: their old, unused email list is worth far more to them as a subscriber-acquisition channel than as a sponsor fee.
  - Technical caveat: before mass-emailing an acquired/partner list, run every email through 3-4 bulk email verification tools (mentioned: "Bulk Email Checker") to filter dead/catch-all/DNS-broken addresses and avoid bounce/spam issues; don't just dump them into Beehiiv and blast — either send one respectful "opt-out" intro email, or negotiate a free/discounted sponsorship in exchange for a one-time send to their list.
  - On finding the right sponsor "mix": look for advertisers with a balance of **high average ticket + decent volume** (example: a dentist who can profit ~$8,000 from one new patient acquired via a $200 ad buy is a great fit) — avoid extremely high-ticket/low-volume (e.g., $100K sports car dealer, unlikely to close even one from a newsletter ad) and extremely low-ticket categories (e.g., restaurant appetizers, where $300 of ad spend only drives $100 of incremental sales). Recommended approach: test ~10 different advertiser verticals and track data to find the best-performing category.
  - No firm subscriber-count threshold given for "ready to charge full price" — host reframes it as building enough of a track record/ROI story for advertisers rather than hitting a specific number.

## Segment 3: Insurance underwriting AI co-pilot startup (B2B SaaS networking advice)
- Two co-founders (a software engineer + an insurance-industry insider) building an "underwriting AI co-pilot" for commercial/specialty insurers — automates due diligence, cross-references manuals/guidelines, helps underwriters process more submissions with the same headcount. Competing against well-funded ($15-30M) incumbents with elite technical teams; betting on niche focus + industry fragmentation (no dominant AI standard yet).
- Customer type clarified: insurance companies or MGAs (Managing General Agents) that write commercial insurance — not individual brokers/agents.
- Current customer-acquisition method: cold LinkedIn outreach to underwriters, targeting the US (to avoid the insider co-founder's employer/network finding out he's moonlighting, since he fears being fired).
- Host's core advice: since direct competitors are all long-established insiders with huge networks, the founders should specifically research **how those competitors found their very first customer(s)** before they had funding/reputation — not just study their current product. Suggested research methods: search for founder interviews/podcasts/blog posts describing their origin story; if unavailable, call competitors directly, be transparent ("I'm not competing with you, just want to know how you got started"), and ask.
- Also suggested identifying adjacent/non-competing SaaS tools that sell into the *same* customer/job title (e.g., Guidewire, policy management software) and researching those founders' early customer-acquisition tactics as a proxy, since the buyer persona is identical even if the product differs.
- Anecdote surfaced from the caller during the call: their competitor's actual growth lever was attending trade shows — one specific trade show (of ~10 attended) generated ~80% of their customers. Host's takeaway: find and prioritize the single highest-leverage channel/event like that rather than spreading thin.
- For general industry networking: host pointed to his own prior in-depth Twitter threads on cold email (findable via Google site-search for his username + "cold email").

## Segment 4: Pivoting a web design/automation business off Zapier onto a proprietary platform
- Caller: 10-year web dev agency owner, building his own Zapier-alternative automation platform to migrate clients off Zapier at roughly half their current Zapier spend, then billing for automation-building services at ~$40/hour (or less) via an in-house engineering team.
- Current book of business: ~30 active clients (6-month view), but ~70% of revenue comes from just 5 "solid" clients (all under 100 employees, one large corporate); the other 25 are solopreneurs/1-2 person shops (many politicians/campaigns), mostly WordPress sites, low-touch/email-only relationships, no real automation budget.
- Existing automation clients: only 4 (subset of the top 5).
- Host's core recommendation: **don't chase new/cold markets first — mine the existing 30 clients**, since trust is already established (hardest part of any sale). Concretely: proactively reach out to each of the 30 with a specific automation idea tailored to their business (e.g., for a political-campaign client collecting donations, propose an AI-personalized automated SMS/email donor check-in/update sequence to increase recurring-donation rates — host draws analogy to his own investor-update cadence improving reinvestment rate from ~40% to ~60%), get them on a call, and pitch a concrete use case rather than a generic "you should automate" pitch.
- Caveat: only after exhausting this existing-client conversion path (host frames it as the "lowest-hanging fruit," potentially doubling the business) should the caller invest in vertical-specific expansion (e.g., building a legal- or HVAC-specific automation playbook) or paid ads/Google ads to find net-new customers outside word-of-mouth.

## Segment 5: Niche tax/financial-planning practice for Americans in Japan/Singapore
- Caller: 20-year Navy veteran, licensed tax preparer and financial adviser (registered in Pennsylvania, can serve clients nationwide), already filed an LLC; pivoting from an undefined generalist practice to a specific niche: **Gen X and Millennial Americans living in or moving to Japan and Singapore**, leveraging his own Japan experience/family ties.
- Rationale: the US requires citizens to file US taxes for life regardless of residency (one of only ~1-2 countries with this rule) — creates a recurring, sticky compliance need. Estimated addressable population: ~60,000 Americans living in Japan, ~30,000 in Singapore (mostly high earners — bankers, employees of multinational/Japanese companies). Singapore additionally offers a **3-year visa for self-employed people showing ~$15,000/month in US-based income**, making it more attractive to self-employed American expats than Japan (where self-employment is described as complicated).
- Monetization structure already approved: flat financial-planning subscription fee (**$450/month**) as an alternative to AUM (assets-under-management) percentage fees; caller prefers flat fee since younger clients are comfortable self-directing trades and mainly want a holistic financial "coach," not a trade-executor.
- Host's marketing/growth suggestions:
  1. Post daily on Twitter/X with a message specifically targeted at this niche audience (past generic posting/YouTube attempts failed because they weren't audience-specific).
  2. Targeted Google/paid search ads on niche keywords ("moving to Japan taxes," etc.) — inexpensive due to low competition/volume.
  3. Find and engage (or acquire) Facebook groups for Japan/Singapore expats. Host's own experience: **they've bought "busted"/dormant Facebook groups with tens of thousands of members for $200-$700 each.** Playbook for taking over a group: join and contribute genuinely for a couple of weeks to earn a "top contributor" badge/reputation, compliment the admin/moderator periodically (low-key, spaced out over weeks), then propose a deal — offer to keep the original moderator on (paid a small amount) to avoid getting immediately kicked out after ownership transfer, since transfer itself doesn't guarantee they can't remove you.
  4. Long-form, faceless YouTube content as a channel precedent: host cites a friend's channel ("Two and Twenty") that grew to hundreds of thousands/millions of views making long-form geopolitical/economic explainer videos with zero paid promotion or Mr. Beast-style boosts — suggests the caller could do similarly niche content (e.g., "Japan's age crisis explained," "where did Singapore's wealth come from") to build an audience funnel with no special growth hacks, just consistent high-quality content.
- Caveat noted: business described as a "slow burn" — but with high retention once trust is established (clients can stay 30 years); host is skeptical paid advertising works well this early without an established niche narrative/following first.
