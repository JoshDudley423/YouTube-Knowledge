# Stop Buying Things. Start Buying Assets that Pay for Themselves
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAyZZc_DjSE | Published: 2026-03-12

Interview with David Stillson (Fort Wayne, IN multipreneur) covering three small rental businesses: moving tote rental, RV rental, and a podcast/music studio rental.

## Moving tote rental business
- Idea: rent reusable industrial plastic totes (like the yellow-top ones) to people moving, instead of them buying/using cardboard boxes.
- Origin: started while prepping his own house move; found "tote rental" is an established niche (established players in Chicago, Indianapolis; none in Fort Wayne).
- Startup cost/economics:
  - Consumer totes (Home Depot) ~$7 each; wholesale/industrial totes from a specialty supplier ~$12 each but must buy by the pallet (~120 totes) for that price, ~$1,500/pallet with shipping.
  - Rents totes for ~$2/piece per rental.
  - Found supplier via a YouTube video naming wholesale tote suppliers; one supplier's warehouse was an hour away with ~1,000 totes in stock, able to fulfill reorders in 24-48 hours.
  - Started with 120 totes, immediately oversold and bought another 120; now (at time of interview) up to almost 400 totes.
  - Rule: only buy more totes when they oversell (i.e., grow inventory reactively, not speculatively).
- Go-to-market: initial attempt was Facebook Marketplace posts with AI-generated (Nano Banana) product images before even owning totes ("sell before you have it") — this did not generate traction.
- What actually worked: local networking with realtors and moving companies.
  - Found realtors via a niche local magazine ("Real Producers," present in most metro areas) that profiles top-producing agents and lists their sales volume/average home price, and that runs monthly in-person networking events for vendors.
  - His wife attended these events in person, met top realtors, and pitched a "realtor gifting program": realtors pay a monthly subscription so they can gift moving totes to their clients at closing.
  - Realtor subscription tiers: 2 totes/month package (charged $99 retail equivalent, realtor pays $60), or 5/month package (~$300/month). One realtor prepaid for a full year.
  - Built custom software: realtor login/claim portal showing available "gift credits," lets them create gift orders, unused credits roll over.
  - Built-in upsell: when a customer redeems a realtor's gift (e.g., 20 totes), the value paid by the realtor ($60) is applied as a $100 credit the customer can put toward a bigger tote package if they need more.
  - Branding: totes include a pouch with a printed card noting "this move is powered by [Realtor Name] at [Brokerage]" — a co-branded gift.
  - Logistics: company delivers and picks up totes; does not handle any packing or moving labor.
- Awareness: biggest competitive obstacle isn't other tote-rental companies, it's that most people don't know the service exists. Best discovered search term: "moving boxes" (not "tote rental"). Google Trends shows "tote rental" search interest rising steadily from ~2011 to present (relative score ~100/100), suggesting rising category awareness/tailwind.
- Pricing rationale vs. alternatives: cardboard box kits from Home Depot run $300-400 for a 4-bedroom kit, still require taping/assembly and disposal.
- Unexpected use cases found by customers: wedding planners renting totes to transport centerpieces; someone moving a 180-gallon fish tank's coral/fish-safe gear. Consider that totes/rental gear can find non-obvious markets.
- General framing: rental businesses (vs. reselling) let you buy an asset once and rent it out repeatedly without constantly re-sourcing inventory — lower overhead than owning real estate rentals, more scalable.

## RV rental business
- Backstory: already owned a personal Ember pull-behind travel trailer (financed via bank + 401k loan, ~$400/month payment) for ~1.5 years for personal use.
- Advice from a private Facebook group for RV rental hosts: "don't rent your own [personal] RV" — buy a second unit dedicated purely to the rental business.
- Bought a second unit, a Jayco Jay Flight travel trailer (pull-behind, 26-ft bunkhouse model), for ~$20,000, financed; payment ~$400/month (insurance separately ~$60/month, total ~$460/month all-in, roughly $5,200-5,500/year in costs).
- Market research process before/while buying:
  - Searched Outdoorsy for existing travel trailer listings in his area to see going nightly rates (regional pricing varies hugely — Florida/Colorado mountain destinations can get $200/night; Midwest markets like Indiana are much lower since they lack major destination draws, relying instead on state parks/festivals/concerts).
  - Directly messaged other local hosts with multiple-camper listings asking about their revenue; got informal benchmark from a couple of owners: "reliably do about $5,000/season per camper" (in rental revenue).
  - Did not do deep occupancy-rate research (host himself suggests a better method: search platform availability for "this weekend" vs. "a year from now" for a comparable unit to gauge how booked-up the market already is).
- Unit choice rationale: chose a "bunkhouse" floorplan specifically (recommended by the Facebook rental-host community) because it appeals to the broadest renter base (families/couples) without costing much more than smaller layouts.
- Avoid features that break: deliberately avoided any unit with slide-outs — described as the #1 failing part on RVs (motor failure, water leak risk, can get stuck and block return/turnaround). Also kept the unit "feature light" (no TV, no Wi-Fi) to minimize things that can break and generate 3am support calls.
- Listing channels: listed on Outdoorsy, RVshare, RVeezy, plus Facebook Marketplace; got first booking within about 2 weeks (end of Sept, an off-season purchase).
- Season structure (Indiana climate): effective booking season runs mid-June to end of October (state parks winterize/shut off dump stations Nov 1).
- Results: first partial season (2024) as validation, first full season (2025) generated ~$7,000 in revenue after platform fees (so ~$10,000 gross before fees) — this covered costs and validated the concept for one unit. Already had 3 repeat-customer bookings lined up for the following July at time of taping.
- Platform economics: Outdoorsy/RVshare take ~25% of the rental fee and also tack on extra insurance/ancillary fees to the customer. Strategy: treat marketplaces purely as lead-gen for first-time bookings, then push repeat customers to book directly through his own website at the same nightly rate — customer saves money (no platform markup) and host keeps more margin, no platform cut.
- Delivery model: does deliveries himself rather than requiring renters to own a tow vehicle — this widens the addressable customer pool (not everyone has an F-150) and gives him control to inspect/level/verify the unit is fully operational before leaving, reducing liability/insurance risk versus the renter towing it themselves.
- Training/QA to reduce support calls: made an unlisted YouTube walkthrough video covering setup/operation of the specific unit; requires renters to watch before pickup. Host also uses a written supplemental rental agreement spelling out tank-dumping procedures etc. (tank/waste handling is where he sees the most "horror stories" in the RV-rental community).
- Scaling constraints: HOA rules (no driveway space, can't keep a camper on-property more than 48 hours) blocked easy expansion; considered a consignment/revenue-split model with friends' unused campers (split nightly rate, no capital outlay) as a way to scale without buying more units outright.
- RV category comparison (useful reference for anyone considering this):
  - Class A (touring-bus style, diesel): very expensive (hundreds of thousands to millions), hard to park; a real rental niche exists but "not for the faint of heart."
  - Class B (Sprinter-van style/"nomad" conversions): popular in western US (Moab, etc.), starts around $80,000, rents comparably to Class C.
  - Class C (motorhome, drivable): most familiar type; convenient (no separate tow vehicle needed, can tow a car behind it) but has more slide-outs/complexity and higher mechanical/safety risk — host (Chris, the interviewer) personally sold his after a bad mid-trip breakdown call.
  - Pull-behind travel trailer (what David uses): towed via hitch, needs a truck or large SUV (his 26-ft unit weighs ~4,800 lb with ~500 lb tongue weight, towable by a regular pickup/large SUV).
  - Fifth wheel: pull-behind requiring a gooseneck/fifth-wheel hitch truck.
  - Toy hauler: pull-behind with an open cargo bay for ATVs/side-by-sides/motocross.
  - Teardrop trailers: small, very light, towable by almost anything; good for couples.
  - Pop-ups: cheaper, but canvas/soft-side versions are a maintenance headache (mold/moisture if not dried after rain) — recommends hard-shell A-frame pop-ups instead.
  - Airstream: premium travel trailer (~$130,000), not a teardrop despite similar look.
- Caveat/recommendation: would recommend the RV rental business to others only if they have some capital and either a suitable tow vehicle or choose a drivable class (C or B); pull-behinds require truck/SUV ownership.

## Podcast/music studio rental
- Origin: built out a home recording studio (~$30,000 retail in gear) originally as a hobby/passion project tied to a YouTube channel ("My Dad's Radio") bringing together a "collective" of 30-40 musicians to record and post videos. Host had an employee discount via his day job at Sweetwater (a major musical instrument retailer) that made buying gear cheaper.
- Monetization: opened the studio to outside bands and clients needing mixing/mastering services once the build-out existed anyway.
- Revenue: highly variable — "a bad month" is about $200; a good month has hit around $2,000. One-off example: got paid for producing a full audiobook for a self-published author.
- Competitive landscape caveat: Fort Wayne is home to Sweetwater's ~500-person sales team, and "every sales engineer at Sweetwater has a recording studio," meaning roughly 400 direct competitor home studios in the same town — described explicitly as "the worst place you could do this."
- Differentiation strategy given that competition: position as a video-capable studio (live-recorded band videos/demos for social/booking use), not just audio recording, since most competitors only do audio.
- Considering: possibly renting to podcasters too, but would need to re-decorate the space to look less like a music studio and more visually neutral/appealing for that use.
- Explicitly framed as a low-stakes side hustle, not a primary income goal ("more of an inside side hustle" — built it for himself anyway, monetizing incidentally).

## General/cross-cutting advice from the video
- Small rental businesses let you start with low capital, avoid needing to constantly re-source new inventory (unlike reselling), and are lower-risk/lower-overhead than real estate rental investment.
- Before starting, research whether others are already doing something similar (Facebook Marketplace searches, direct outreach to existing operators) — people running similar rental businesses were consistently open to sharing their numbers/advice when asked directly.
- Join niche communities (private Facebook groups) for the specific rental vertical to learn about risks, insurance needs, and operational pitfalls before scaling.
- "You don't have to be fancy about this stuff" — start low-tech, validate demand cheaply before investing heavily.
