
# "I Bought a Business That's a Scam. Now What?"
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UIfXwvvF0a4 | Published: 2024-07-01

Live call-in Q&A episode, three caller segments.

## Segment 1: Corey — automated bag/locker rental pods for stadiums, concerts, airports
- Concept: deploy self-service, credit-card-operated storage pod kiosks (same type of pod used for apartment package delivery, sourced from a manufacturer his family's company already works with) at venues with bag-size/clear-bag restrictions (stadiums, concerts, festivals) so fans with oversized/non-compliant bags can rent a locker instead of hiding valuables in bushes or throwing them away (real anecdote: security confiscating/discarding expensive purses left outside gates at University of Kentucky football games).
- Existing but non-automated competitors observed in the wild: a third-party box truck at Fenway Park (Red Sox) renting bag storage; old-school staffed gym lockers at a Nationals game. The pitch is that this idea automates that (type a code, locker pops open) rather than needing staff.
- Revenue potential estimated by the guest: capturing even 1% of the combined NFL/NBA/MLB/college-sports annual attendance market at $10/use was estimated at **~$40 million** market opportunity; host floated "$50k per game" as a single-venue estimate at scale.
- Go-to-market suggestion: cold-call/LinkedIn venue facilities managers directly (don't oversell it as a hard sales pitch — frame it as "would this work for your venue?"); pitch venues on a revenue share rather than leading with an upfront fee, framed as improving fan experience/retention rather than an added cost to them. Kiosks can be custom-wrapped (a service the guest's company already offers) to look like official venue-branded equipment rather than a generic box truck, avoiding a "sketchy" appearance.
- Second variant of the same pod ("pull things out" model): stock pods at campgrounds/RV parks with rentable items (hoses, leveling blocks, stairs, yard games, firewood) — guest personally has experienced frequently forgetting such items while RV traveling. Deposit-based enforcement suggested (e.g., "return this water hose or we charge your card $100/$200").
- Host's stated preference: the storage-locker (put-things-in) model over the item-rental (take-things-out) model, because it requires **zero inventory** to manage/restock (no hoses/firewood to replenish), just kiosk placement.
- Recommended validation step: call a dozen venue decision-makers, log all responses, and use that to judge viability before building anything.
- Caveat/reality check the host raises implicitly: no confirmed existing player has locked up this specific niche despite it being "not automated in the industry" — guest hadn't yet pursued sourcing conversations with actual venues at time of the call, only internal exploratory outreach.

## Segment 2: Alec — LED-screen smart phone case with NFC/NFT/charging (host advises against pursuing as-is)
- Idea: a physical phone case combining (1) an LED display on the back showing custom images/GIFs/NFTs tied to a companion mobile app and blockchain/NFT verification across multiple chains, (2) an NFC chip or QR code (link-tree/"Tappy card" style) for tapping to share socials/payment info, (3) a built-in charging clip so the phone charges without removing the case. Guest is a software engineer (used AI/LLM tools to write the app code) with ~2 years and some third-party contractor prototyping already invested, but no hardware product experience and prior co-founders have all left.
- Host's core advice: **do not pursue this idea** — it stacks three independently hard domains (hardware manufacturing, software/app, e-commerce/retail) into one venture, which is unusually high risk for a solo, first-time hardware founder; "hardware is hard" and typically costs 2-6x initial estimates. Host cites his own decade (2010-2019) running a cell phone accessory business as informing this caution, and notes the market is saturated with established, well-capitalized case brands (OtterBox, Case-Mate) who could easily copy and out-resource a differentiated product like this if it gained traction.
- Host explicitly names **sunk cost fallacy** as the trap to avoid: time/money already invested (even a full year of full-time effort) should not be treated as a reason to continue; framed as "a cheap MBA," not wasted, and pointed out the idea would likely take years and require raising capital before any revenue, meaning a decade-later retrospective might show quitting early would have been the smart move.
- Alternative direction suggested: leverage the guest's actual rare/valuable skill (coding) to build low-cost/zero-COGS software products instead of an ambitious hardware line; recommended resource: **IndieHackers.com** — a community/forum of solo technical founders sharing real revenue case studies (example cited: a Chrome extension scraping Yelp for fake reviews reaching $10K MRR in 3 months) as a source of more tractable, faster-to-validate software business ideas, plus a way to find co-founders.
- Also flagged in the discussion: guest already runs a side car-detailing business — host notes this existing, already-running venture may itself be an underrated opportunity worth focusing on rather than a totally new, harder concept; discouraged the guest from over-identifying/"marrying" any single idea just because it's the one he happened to think of, and warned against survivorship-bias thinking based on rare outlier startup success stories (referenced the movie The Pursuit of Happyness as an example of survivorship-bias narrative).
- No specific numbers (pricing, COGS, revenue) given for the phone-case concept since it was never built/sold — the segment is entirely a "should I build this" gut-check, not a case study of results.

## Segment 3: Tiffany — bought a fraudulent small business via SBA loan; recovery options (the title topic)
- Situation: caller bought an existing **custom merch/promotional products business** (screen printing, embroidery, promotional items — schools, sports teams, landscaping/construction companies, small local businesses) in New Jersey in August [2023], intending it to replace her well-paying day job's income. Did full due diligence (lawyer, accountant, business broker, SBA loan process, ~6 months of diligence from February to August closing) — despite this, the seller committed extensive fraud:
  - Claimed 7 active employees with full payroll documentation; in reality all employees had quit months before closing and he never disclosed it (justified pre-close employee inaccessibility as "not wanting to spook them," which read as a normal, non-red-flag request at the time).
  - Falsified P&L statements: showed buyers **+$300,000** positive cash flow (Jan-June) after all expenses; the real numbers obtained later directly from his own accountant's emails (found because the seller's forwarded email account was handed over as part of the transition) showed **-$150,000** for the same period — i.e., a ~$450K swing, meaning "it wasn't even a sellable business."
  - Sold equipment that was supposed to be included in the sale.
  - Left unpaid vendor invoices for outsourced work performed for customers after his employees quit, resulting in vendors contacting the new owner (Tiffany) for payment.
  - Fabricated claims of a second location and franchising plans — both untrue (confirmed by the buyer following up on removing the fake second location from the website).
  - Buyer's forensic check: used the **Wayback Machine** and **WHOIS.com** to determine the business's website domain had actually been dormant/parked (originally used for an unrelated tattoo shop, re-registered late 2021, not indexed by Google until 2023) — evidence the seller built up a "legitimacy front" shortly before listing the business for sale.
  - Guest speculated the real story: the seller's father (well-known/established in the same industry, feeding him leads/customers) helped him start the business in 2018, it underperformed, and the seller falsified financials rather than admit failure — complicated further because the seller's father's business is literally next door, making public complaints to customers awkward/risky (many customers only patronize the business because they know the father).
- **Current financial state at time of call**: monthly revenue ~$28,000-$30,000 (seasonal, screen-printing business, summer is a slow season since schools aren't ordering); COGS ~$9,700/month (**~65% gross margin**); payroll ~$10,000/month minimum (half of gross profit); $2,000/month outsourced graphic design agency (~50 designs/month included) flagged by host as overpriced — suggested finding an Upwork freelancer instead (host's own comparison point: he pays $7/design for YouTube thumbnails), potentially saving "at least $1,000/month." Rent: $2,000/month on a 5-year lease (4+ years remaining). SBA loan payment: $4,000/month, currently paused by the lender pending litigation outcome (would resume, plus Tiffany already personally covering shortfalls out of pocket alongside her own mortgage).
- Buyer is pursuing a lawsuit against the seller (in progress at time of recording; details of legal claims not covered) and holding off on a bankruptcy decision pending its outcome. She still works her original full-time job and cannot yet take a salary from the acquired business.
- Host's core advice (framed as: you have two real options — go all-in or cut losses):
  1. **Go all-in on aggressively growing the business** while awaiting the lawsuit outcome, rather than "languishing" and shrinking further: take courses on the (common, well-documented) screen-printing/print-on-demand industry, learn Google/Facebook ads (even a cheap ~$50 course), and consider print-on-demand/3PL partnership models common in that industry (partnering with a larger printer to fulfill orders you can't handle, or vice versa) to control costs without headcount.
  2. Alternatively, **fully sever the business today** (walk away from the lease, accept the loss) rather than let it slowly bleed and worsen her overall financial position — host frames indefinitely limping along as the worst option.
  3. Concrete customer-acquisition tactic recommended: identify her best-performing customer types/industries (schools, HVAC, landscaping, construction, etc.) via 10-20 target verticals, then use **Outscraper** (same tool referenced elsewhere in the channel) to scrape hundreds/thousands of matching local businesses, scrub out landline-only numbers, and send a **mass cold text** to business owners. Suggested framing: qualify first ("do you still own [business]?"), then share a brief, honest version of her situation ("I'm in a tough spot due to something outside my control, trying to grow my way out of it") as a personal-story hook, since the product itself (custom merch) is a commodity and differentiation has to come from story/relationship, not the product. Response-rate framed as a numbers game (host: "maybe it's 1%, maybe it's 30%").
  4. Caution against public/transparent disclosure of the fraud to customers, per the caller's lawyer's advice — risk of countersuit and alienating customers loyal to the seller's father.
- No resolution/outcome given in this episode — it ends with advice and encouragement, not a follow-up result.
