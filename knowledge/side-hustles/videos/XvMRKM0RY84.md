# How to Sell Your Stuff Through Uber and Lyft Drivers (+4 more biz ideas)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XvMRKM0RY84 | Published: 2024-08-31

Conversational brainstorm episode (Chris + guest Nick) covering five distinct business ideas.

## Idea 1: Rideshare drivers as a referral/distribution channel for high-ticket services
- Core concept: rideshare (Uber/Lyft) drivers spend large amounts of one-on-one captive time with riders who are often in a "transitory" life phase (moving, buying/selling homes) and may be good prospects for high-ticket services like real estate, life insurance, or health insurance.
- Proposed model: recruit/train drivers as referral partners for a licensed agent (real estate agent, insurance agent) — driver mentions the service or hands over contact info during a ride, agent pays the driver a referral commission (a specific figure of ~25% commission was floated).
- Legal caveat raised: high-ticket regulated services (insurance, real estate) require licensing, and there are laws restricting referral fee arrangements in some of these industries — flagged but not resolved in the conversation.
- Refined version (host preferred this over the raw "get the driver to pitch riders" idea, which he felt creates unwanted forced small talk for passengers): don't sell riders anything — instead sell or give the drivers themselves training/education (e.g., how to become a licensed life insurance salesman) that they can consume passively via audio while driving, since many rideshare drivers' schedules otherwise prevent them from investing in self-improvement.
- Monetization logic discussed for the training itself: charging even a small amount (e.g., $20) for an audio training course, rather than giving it away free, increases the likelihood recipients actually engage with/act on it (behavioral point: paid content sees far higher completion/action rates than free content — example ratio floated: give something free to 100 people and maybe 10% engage, sell it to 100 people for $20 and maybe 40% engage, and you also make revenue).
- Alternative model discussed: offer completely free sales/communication skills training to drivers (formatted specifically for the rideshare context — teaching them to have engaging conversations with every rider as informal "reps"), with modules/quizzes for accountability, and possibly pay drivers a completion bonus baked into the economics.
- End-state business model envisioned: build a marketplace matching (a) people you've trained as sales/referral partners (rideshare drivers) with (b) real estate agents, insurance agents, or other high-ticket-service providers around the country who want referral distribution — you become the matchmaker/platform operator taking a cut of whatever gets sold, without needing to be a licensed agent yourself.
- No revenue, cost, or execution data given — this is a purely theoretical/unbuilt idea discussed live.

## Idea 2 (mentioned but explicitly NOT recommended): "Rent old cars to DoorDash drivers" ("Presto Cars")
- Background: DoorDash, unlike Uber, has no vehicle age limit for its drivers (because DoorDash customers never see/ride in the car), creating a niche for renting/selling older vehicles specifically to DoorDash drivers who have bad credit or no cash to buy a car outright.
- This niche is distinct from the decade-old, established practice of fleet operators renting newer cars to Uber/Lyft drivers.
- Host explicitly says: "people are doing it successfully, they're making money — I don't recommend it, I would never do it," citing high operational headache: needing car trackers, expensive insurance, and dealing with non-paying customers/high-risk clientele.
- No numbers given (revenue, costs, margins) — flagged purely as an existing niche opportunity with significant downside, sourced from an Instagram "guru" selling a course on the topic.

## Idea 3: Scrape and undercut TheKnot.com in the wedding-vendor lead-gen space
- Background/origin: host tried to list his own property as a wedding venue on TheKnot.com and found no self-serve listing option — had to book a sales call.
- On the sales call, pricing was revealed as three tiers based purely on search-result placement priority, not more leads or better service: ~$5,400/month (top tier, "832 leads"), ~$2,500/month ("456 leads"), ~$1,200/month ("258 leads") — exact per-tier lead-count figures given verbally by the salesperson, described by the host as suspiciously precise for a business that also claimed "very seasonal" demand.
- Host (who has lead-gen business experience) flags several "red flags": lead-gen companies can't actually guarantee precise lead volumes (ad spend efficiency degrades as you raise budget); a hard sales push including an on-the-spot "sign now for 10% off" discount; complaints found on subreddits and Better Business Bureau reviews describing hundreds of leads with no closings.
- Drew an analogy to the Ashley Madison data-breach investigation, which found ~90%+ of "female" messages on the platform were fake/bot-generated to keep paying (mostly male) users engaged despite the platform's severe gender-ratio imbalance — used to illustrate how a subscription-based lead-gen platform is financially incentivized to overstate lead quality/volume to keep high-paying advertisers subscribed.
- Proposed business idea: build a free, no-strings-attached alternative wedding vendor directory ("like the Knot, except free"), starting with one geography (e.g., Texas) or one vendor niche (e.g., wedding photographers). Scrape/identify TheKnot's existing paying vendors, pitch them a free listing with no upfront cost. Once vendors start getting real leads, monetize later via a small per-lead fee (e.g., $5/lead) or a percentage of booked jobs (e.g., 5%), rather than large flat monthly subscription fees.
- Context noted: a commenter (described as knowledgeable in the wedding industry) said COVID and the rise of Instagram/Pinterest as primary wedding-planning discovery channels have already eroded TheKnot's traffic/relevance, arguably increasing the platform's incentive to inflate lead value to retain existing paying vendors.
- Additional business-model observation: subscription-based "tech" companies like TheKnot often run at ~90% margins, meaning there's substantial room to underprice them while still being profitable — described as a general negotiating/business-model insight (host says he negotiates harder with any subscription-based tech vendor knowing this).
- No execution or revenue data — this is a proposed/unbuilt idea.

## Idea 4: Regional wholesale-distribution consulting for local "influencer"/tradesperson figures
- Background: wholesaling exists for two reasons per the hosts — (1) economies of scale (a wholesaler buying hundreds of thousands of units gets better unit pricing than a small local business buying tens of units at a time), and (2) asymmetric knowledge (most small operators simply don't know how to source directly from international manufacturers, e.g., via Alibaba, or negotiate/import at scale).
- Host's own related past ventures cited as pattern examples: LCD Cycle (wholesaling cell-phone repair parts sourced from China to local repair shops), a similar model applied to "Texas Snacks," "Mining Syndicate" (crypto mining hardware), and a nascent "perfume vending" venture — all following the same "become the import/wholesale intermediary" pattern.
- Trigger for this idea: a local flooring "flipper"/influencer (Mike Higgins, based near Madison, WI/Green Bay area) with strong local market knowledge and reputation but no idea how to become a wholesale distributor himself, asked the host directly for help.
- Two business models discussed for monetizing this opportunity:
  1. **Consulting/education model:** teach the local figure (e.g., for a large one-time fee, $20,000 was floated) how to source internationally (start with Alibaba, contact manufacturers directly, negotiate, handle customs/logistics/international wire transfers) — a pure knowledge-transfer/consulting engagement where you don't touch fulfillment.
  2. **Preferred model — build/operate the branded storefront for them as a "faceless" backend operator:** build and manage a templated, white-labeled e-commerce site under the local figure's own name/brand (e.g., "HigginsFlooring.com"), where you handle sourcing, fulfillment, payments, and marketing collateral behind the scenes, and pay them a percentage (e.g., 5% floated) of sales generated for lending their name/local trust and driving product through it — reduces friction for the local figure (they don't need to learn wholesaling at all).
  3. Extension: replicate the same templated storefront model across a network of local "faces" in different regions (examples floated: Denver, Iowa, Florida) sourcing from one central warehouse, potentially achieving further economies of scale by aggregating demand across the whole network — though hosts flag this could introduce real logistics complexity.
  4. Alternative buyer segment floated: approach regional/local home builders directly (rather than local social-media "influencers") and offer to negotiate better wholesale flooring pricing by cutting out the retail flooring middleman they currently use, taking a percentage of the resulting savings as the fee — flagged as speculative and untested since neither host claims deep expertise in that specific vertical.
- No revenue/cost data — described as an emergent, real (a specific named contact reached out) but unbuilt opportunity.

## Idea 5: AI/automation implementation agency for local businesses (with a specific diagnostic framework)
- Observed gap: most business owners/operators (outside the "social media bubble" of tech-savvy audiences) don't understand what tools like ChatGPT, and especially Zapier, actually do or how they could use them — cited as a much bigger blind spot for Zapier specifically than for ChatGPT.
- Proposed operating framework for diagnosing automation opportunities in any business, organized around 5 core business functions: finance (accounting, AP, payroll), operations (fulfillment/service delivery), marketing, product (design/UX/quality), and people management.
- Step-by-step audit process to sell/deliver:
  1. List everything currently done manually (anything requiring a person to physically key something in).
  2. Identify the business's "source of truth" system — the central backbone system of record (examples given: an EHR/electronic health record in healthcare, a CRM like Jobber in home-services businesses).
  3. List every other software the business uses individually (typical minimum cited: at least four separate tools — email, CRM, invoicing, accounting).
  4. Identify what Zapier-style API integrations already exist (or don't) connecting the source-of-truth system to those other tools — the manual work found in step 1 will almost always live in the gap between the source of truth and these other disconnected tools.
- Pricing model debate: could charge either a one-time project fee (e.g., $10,000 with a guarantee of eliminating a position or saving X dollars) or a recurring SaaS/retainer model; hosts lean toward the recurring retainer model because clients will have ongoing questions/support needs after initial setup (non-technical staff will inevitably break something and need help).
- Sales-pitch strategy proposed: lead with a completely free, no-strings-attached ~3-hour audit/consultation where you map out the client's entire potential automation stack in detail (deliberately overwhelming them with specificity — naming specific tools like GoHighLevel, Zapier, Jobber, Streak, Google Workspace) so they see the value/expertise but feel unable to implement it alone, then offer paid implementation afterward (illustrative pricing floated: ~$5,000 setup + $500/month ongoing) once trust/value has been demonstrated rather than pitched cold up front. Framed as a "give value first, gamble on time investment" approach that improves with repetition/rep count.
- Caveat raised by the host himself: this specific approach ("this is the next wave," analogized to businesses helping other businesses get on Google Business Profiles or Facebook/Instagram a decade ago) may currently be premature for hyper-local small businesses (example: an orthodontist) — it may be hard to get a small local business to pay a recurring $2,000/month fee for AI automation even if it generates $6,000-$10,000/month of value, simply because they don't yet understand or trust the category. Suggested that timing/target selection (age/tech-savviness of the buyer) matters a lot for viability right now.

## General framework mentioned (not itself a standalone idea): "follow the funded startups" heuristic
- Framework: whatever recently-VC-funded tech startups spend their "other people's money" on (agency services, expensive B2B SaaS, executive placement/recruiting services, automation agencies) tends to become mainstream spending for ordinary small/local businesses roughly 2-5 years later.
- Applied example: an automation-agency operator on Twitter reportedly charges ~$3,000/month primarily to newly-funded startups (found via Crunchbase's "just raised a round" filter) because those buyers already understand and value the technology; the thesis is that this same service will eventually be sold successfully to mainstream local businesses (e.g., restaurants) once the category matures and trust/awareness catches up — drawing a parallel to how "get your business on Google/Facebook/Instagram" agencies emerged roughly a decade ago as those platforms went from novel to essential for small business.
- Framed as a way to time market entry into an emerging service category: sell first to sophisticated/funded early adopters to build a track record, then "translate" the same offering downmarket to Main Street businesses once the market matures.
