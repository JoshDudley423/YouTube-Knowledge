# How to Legally Avoid Paying (most) Taxes
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sp2hJ1d-Zb8 | Published: 2025-02-28

Interview with Brian Bourgerie (Invest Puerto Rico) on relocating a business/yourself to Puerto Rico for its tax incentive program (Act 60). This is a tax-optimization/relocation strategy, not a specific product/service idea, but is highly actionable for anyone running a location-independent or remote-manageable business.

## Core mechanism: Puerto Rico Act 60 tax incentives
- Puerto Rico is a US territory: US citizens can move there with no passport/currency change, but for federal tax purposes it is treated as foreign — Puerto Rico has its own tax code and US citizens living there do not pay US federal income tax on PR-sourced income.
- Individual incentive: 0% tax on capital gains, dividends, and interest for bona fide PR residents.
- Corporate incentive (Act 60 "export services" company): baseline 4% corporate tax rate (previously described as 0-4%); if your company earns under $3M in its first year after moving/setting up, you can secure a 2% corporate tax rate for the next 5 years — a detail the guest says most people miss and only learn about after the fact.
- R&D incentive: get 50% of R&D expenditures back as sellable tax credits. Example given: guest's company spent ~$1M/year on R&D, generating ~$500K in tax credits. These credits are liquid — sellable on an open market for 92-96 cents on the dollar, or the PR government will buy them back directly for 90 cents on the dollar, letting a pre-revenue/loss-making startup convert R&D spend into near-immediate cash without diluting equity.

## Structure for an existing US business (worked example)
Scenario discussed: a business earning $1M topline / $200K net profit, ownable/manageable remotely.
1. Set up a PR Act 60 incentivized "management services company" that bills/services your existing stateside company (as much of the business function as possible — accounting, customer support, sales, billing/invoicing — should be routed through the PR entity to lower the blended effective tax rate).
2. As owner, pay yourself a modest W-2 salary (guest suggests something like $30-50K, taxed at PR's regular local rates, which combined can be roughly comparable to normal state+federal tax) — leave the exact number to a CPA.
3. Take the remaining profit as an owner distribution from the Act 60 company, which is tax-free.
4. Worked numbers: on $200K net profit, roughly $150K can end up effectively tax-free as a distribution, with only the ~$50K salary portion taxed (and at PR's local bracket). Net effect claimed: roughly $75K/year in tax savings on this scenario (avoiding ~30% federal income tax, i.e. ~$60K, plus additional savings from routing other business functions through PR).
- At the ~$200K profit level, you're below the threshold that triggers PR's local-employee headcount requirements (those requirements kick in above roughly $3M).

## Exit/capital-gains angle
- If you also apply for the individual investor incentive (0% tax on capital gains/dividends/interest) and later sell your company: the fraction of your holding period during which you were a bona fide PR resident is tax-free on that portion of the sale proceeds. Example: own the company 10 years total, live in PR for the last 5 of those 10 → roughly half the sale price is tax-free to you as the individual.
- Important caveat: you must actually establish residency and time-in-PR before selling — you cannot move 2 days before an exit and get the benefit; the incentive rewards being a resident during the appreciation period.
- Case cited: a business owner with a ~9-figure exit (rolled-up consulting/services businesses) moved to PR and structured in advance, ending up with roughly 70% of proceeds tax-free versus an estimated 40-50% he would have kept tax-free otherwise.
- Corporate-level exit tax quirk: guest's own company sold to a New Zealand buyer; because PR is "foreign" for federal tax purposes, the sale wasn't subject to the usual federal exit tax that would apply to a US-domestic sale — the sale was simply taxed at the company's low PR corporate rate (2-4%), not at capital gains rates. Guest notes he didn't fully anticipate this benefit when structuring the company; it worked out as a bonus.

## Types of people/businesses who relocate to PR for this
- Remote/location-independent service exporters: digital agencies, podcasters, "digital nomad" style businesses.
- Owners of stateside businesses that don't require their physical presence: e.g. a friend who owns a chain of gyms in the southern US, manages them remotely from PR, pays a 4% tax rate, flies back to the mainland as needed (no international travel/passport issue since PR is US territory).
- R&D-heavy tech/hardware/agriculture companies (benefit most from the 50% R&D tax credit).
- Investors/fund managers (cited: Pantera Capital) who relocate their management teams, partly for the tax benefit and partly because it clusters them near other funds/LPs.
- Some people move for lifestyle-only reasons (beach life, family proximity) without realizing the tax incentives exist.

## Cost of living / practical notes
- Housing example (7 years ago, prices have risen since): 2BR/2BA + 2BR/1BA duplex-style house with pool, ~1 acre, fruit trees, in the mountains ~6 min from Krispy Kreme and 8 min from Costco (near San Juan, not on the coast) rented for ~$1,600/month.
- Some goods cost more due to import taxes/island logistics: dairy/milk and yogurt are pricier than mainland US; cars cost roughly 15% more.
- Healthcare is described as dramatically cheaper (example: total out-of-pocket cost for delivering twins, after insurance, was $152) but with fewer amenities/frills than mainland hospitals.
- Neighborhoods mentioned for relocators: Camino del Mar (gated, mixed community, ~10-15% mainland transplants), Dorado (higher-end, notable residents cited), Condado/Miramar/Hato Rey in San Juan (startup/arts/business districts respectively), Palmas del Mar (large master-planned community with marina/schools), west side (surfing).

## Caveats
- You must actually relocate and live in PR (become a "bona fide resident") to qualify — this is a real relocation decision, not a paperwork-only address change, and involves genuine cultural adjustment (bilingual environment, different school system, different retail landscape).
- Not every neighborhood/lifestyle fit is the same — guest warns against picking a location based only on a friend's recommendation or a single vacation visit; recommends visiting/testing different areas (mountains vs. coast vs. suburbs vs. city) before committing, citing friends whose relocation failed because they picked a home before evaluating fit for their family.
- Tax specifics (exact salary-vs-distribution split, corporate structuring, credit sales) should be set up with a CPA/specialist — the guest repeatedly defers precise numbers to professional advice rather than giving one-size-fits-all figures.
- Resource named: investpr.org (Invest Puerto Rico, a public-private nonprofit) offers free resources/services to entrepreneurs considering the move.
