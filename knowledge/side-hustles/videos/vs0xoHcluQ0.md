# How to Piggyback Your Way to $1M+ Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vs0xoHcluQ0 | Published: 2025-03-18

Chris Koerner + Nik (HoldCo Bros) discuss "piggybacking" business models (attaching a new offering to an existing venue/customer base) and using AI agents for market research.

## Med Spa piggybacked on golf/country clubs
- Med spas = cosmetic/wellness clinics (Botox, injectables, semaglutide/GLP-1 treatments, hormone replacement/testosterone therapy).
- Market size: ~$4 billion in the US, growing >10%/year (vs. ~2-3%/year for the overall US economy) — described as "5x the average rate of growth."
- Margins cited: 15-20%, sometimes higher.
- Real example cited: a friend is in talks to open a med spa on a golf/country club campus that's being renovated with new condos/amenities; framed as giving club members (mostly male golfers) a benefit to bring spouses.
- Deal structure discussed: club owner in this case wanted no equity, just wanted the added member amenity — the operator would fund buildout, provide physicians/clinical oversight, run the business, and give members a discount rate.
- Koerner argues the more standard/expected structure is a revenue-share or percentage-of-sales lease (like a mall/Walmart in-line tenant model) rather than free amenity — cites his own iPhone repair store in a mall paying $2,200/month plus 10% of revenue as the norm.
- Generalizable pattern: identify membership-based businesses (golf, country club, tennis club, gyms) whose members have money and an unmet adjacent need, then place a complementary business inside/next to it. Example given: Walmart in-line tenants (eye clinics, Great Clips, Subway) — optometrists inside Walmart are required by regulation to have a separate exterior entrance door.
- Caveat/risk raised: behavioral mismatch — if a venue's customer base skews heavily one demographic (e.g., 90% male golfers) and the new offering skews the opposite (e.g., med spa skews female), it may not convert; countered that this specific club is a broader "country club" with family amenities, and that men are increasingly using Botox too.

## Sauna / cold plunge business inside gyms, country clubs, pools
- Real example: a podcast guest opened a sauna/cold plunge business inside a gym; buildout was cheap and it "is going great."
- Startup costs cited: cold plunges ~$5,000 each, saunas ~$10,000 each; bought 2 of each, placed them in unused square footage near the gym entrance, and rents the space from the gym.
- General framework: find any facility that already has locker rooms/showers/bathrooms (gym, country club, public pool) and piggyback a cold plunge + sauna offering on top rather than building standalone infrastructure.
- Market-timing argument: cold plunges/saunas are not yet "mainstream" (still early/growth phase of adoption curve) despite feeling like an oversaturated trend — compared to smoothie shops (genuinely saturated) vs. this category (still room to grow). Example: in the hosts' social circle, ~80% of families have $130k pools, but almost none of those same families have a cold plunge or sauna yet — implying latent future demand.
- Suggested demand-testing methods before buying equipment: (1) rent 1-2 cold plunges + 2 saunas (~$20,000 total) and test them for a month at different venues (gym, mall) to gauge usage; (2) cheaper growth-hack test — put up a sign at a gym saying "sauna/cold plunge coming in 2 months, sign up here for a free first month," collecting emails via QR code, to gauge demand before any capital outlay.
- General "distribution-first" framework: identify a trend, then ask "where are the people who'd use this already going, and how do I get placed in front of them" rather than building a standalone location.

## Using AI agents (ChatGPT Deep Research & Operator) for market research
- Suggested research pipeline for finding a location to "piggyback" a product (e.g., cold plunge):
  1. Pull an IBISWorld industry report on the category to see which states show the most growth/searches.
  2. Cross-reference with Google Trends (state-level search volume normalized by population).
  3. Use the Wisconsin Franchise Search database — Wisconsin is one of the only states requiring franchisors to publicly publish their FDD (Franchise Disclosure Document) — to find franchise performance data.
  4. Have the AI read FDDs, identify best-performing locations (example: found the best cold-plunge franchise locations were all next to LA Fitness), then reason about where else to place a competing unit (e.g., a Planet Fitness or mom-and-pop gym instead, since LA Fitness likely has an exclusive contract).
  5. Can extend to cross-referencing crime data, unemployment rate, economic growth rate, and retail square footage by zip code around top-performing locations.
- Tip: ask Deep Research first "what are some non-obvious correlated data points I should look at?" before diving in — it will suggest things like income, disposable spend at Whole Foods, etc.
- Tip: Deep Research can run actual regression/correlation analysis (R-squared, causal effect estimates) between data points on request.
- "Just the tip" tactic: find a great business interview (podcast/YouTube) that goes deep on numbers (pricing, churn, first-year sales, addresses), copy the video URL into Descript (free or ~$5) to get an accurate speaker-attributed transcript (YouTube's own transcripts lack attribution/have bad formatting), then paste that transcript into ChatGPT/Gemini and ask it to extract business model, distribution, startup costs, margins, and hiring needs. Claimed to be an underused technique that surfaces info most people (including PE/VC analysts) don't bother digging for.
- Cost: full Deep Research + Operator access requires OpenAI's $200/month tier; free/$20 tier can approximate long-form output by first asking for an outline/table of contents, then requesting detail section-by-section.
- Prompting tip for getting a "PhD-level" research partner experience: explicitly prompt "you have PhD-level experience in [industry], help me think through how to find the best locations" so the AI asks clarifying follow-up questions before proceeding (Deep Research proactively asks scoping questions, e.g., "public records, interviews, or social media?" "which revenue range?" "how should data be formatted?").

## ChatGPT Operator — pros/cons (browser-automation agent)
- Cons: buggy/unreliable across different websites (a Facebook Marketplace outreach test worked for the host but failed for many viewers with no clear explanation); slow, sometimes slower than doing it manually; makes mistakes; can't run unattended on certain major sites (Facebook requires the tab to stay open/can't navigate away; couldn't get it to work on YouTube at all); requires "babysitting" similar to Tesla Full Self-Driving requiring hands on the wheel; long-running Deep Research tasks can silently stall for over a week with no output.
- Pros: works well for low-complexity, well-defined tasks — e.g., "find a hotel within 5 miles of this address, 4+ stars, under $150/night" (compared favorably to an executive assistant), or "find a 2015 Toyota Tundra within 100 miles, under 100k miles, under $30,000." Can fill web forms, apply filters, and message people directly, though reliability degrades the more steps requested in one task.
- Notably solved a CAPTCHA during testing but then had a guardrail preventing it from submitting the solved form — highlighting current safety-guardrail limitations distinct from raw capability.
- Framing/thesis: this is "version one" of agentic AI — the advice is to start experimenting now (even at real dollar cost) to build a skills edge before agents become mainstream/easy, drawing an analogy to Tesla FSD's multi-year improvement curve but expecting AI agents to improve on a much faster (~18 month) timeline than self-driving did.

## Deep Research — pros/cons
- Pro: functions like a free-out-of-college research analyst (vs. paying $50k-$60k/year for a junior analyst), producing a digestible, citeable report you can follow up on.
- Example: asked for a "deep dive" on a public figure; took 5 minutes, cited 29 sources, returned ~3,700 words.
- Con: can take very long on complex, multi-source tasks (one request for cross-referenced healthcare-provider revenue data by state was still not delivered after more than a week).
- Con: without knowing how to structure a proper research methodology (as in a PhD-level research process — correlated vs. non-correlated data, causation vs. correlation), it's hard to prompt Deep Research to produce maximally useful output; hosts want it to ask more clarifying questions than it currently does.
- Predicted market impact: likely to commoditize/threaten paid industry-report business models (e.g., IBISWorld's ~$3,000 PDF reports) — hosts expect providers to either drop prices, dramatically increase report depth/length, or both, as AI reduces the cost of producing this research.

## General framework reiterated
- "My favorite boat is a boat that has a tailwind" — prioritize riding an already-growing trend/demographic shift over pure execution skill.
- Distribution-first thinking: rather than asking "what should I build," ask "where do the target customers already congregate, and how do I get access/placement there."
