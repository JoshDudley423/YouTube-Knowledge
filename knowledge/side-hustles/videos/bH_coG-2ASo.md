# "Stupid Simple" Apps Are Making Millions While You Overthink
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bH_coG-2ASo | Published: 2025-12-19

Guest: Will Cannon, serial bootstrapped SaaS founder (UpLead — a B2B sales lead/data platform, and Signaturely — an e-signature tool), self-taught, no outside funding, built a large-scale B2B data business before pivoting to SaaS.

Core business idea/pattern demonstrated across the guest's ventures: identify an established, fragmented market with real demand (validated via public review sites like G2), clone/simplify an existing successful business model rather than inventing something new, and prioritize proving you can acquire customers profitably before fully building the product.

## Background/origin (context for credibility)
- Grew up low-income; earliest hustle was burning custom mixtape CDs for classmates in high school (charged $10-20/CD, made about $100/week).
- Early career: cold-calling mortgage sales during the pre-2008 subprime boom; made his employer $100K in his first month but was paid only $10K, prompting him and his then-girlfriend (now wife) to start their own mortgage company from their apartment, later sleeping on an air mattress and showering via a jerry-rigged handheld shower in their office bathroom to afford overhead while scaling. Lost nearly everything in the 2008 financial crisis (4 house foreclosures, car repossessed).

## Business #1: Data/lead-list brokering (pre-UpLead)
- Origin: while running the mortgage company, they had been buying realtor/loan-officer contact lists to recruit staff; noticed coworkers wanted to buy his leftover lists for ~$100 each.
- Found a similar existing business for sale on a marketplace site (BizBen, akin to BizBuySell) called ListGuy.com — doing about $10,000/month revenue, listed for sale around $200,000. Rather than buy it, researched/"window-shopped" its model (explicitly recommends browsing BizBuySell-style marketplaces and reading due-diligence materials on listed businesses purely as free market research/idea generation, without breaching any NDA or stealing proprietary info) and built a copycat: The Data Supplier, selling packaged contact lists broken out by industry (realtors, dentists, chiropractors, etc.).
- Reached roughly $30,000-50,000/month within 2-3 months of launch, driven by SEO (ranking #1 for various "[industry] email list" search terms — describes this as "gray hat" SEO at the time) plus cold emailing prospective business buyers.
- Unit economics: could acquire large, broad-coverage US consumer/business datasets (name, DOB, address, interests, phone) for as little as roughly $1,000 for 200-300 million records via industry relationships, then resell the same underlying list repeatedly to different customers (a digital file, effectively free marginal cost to resell) — ran two parallel businesses (he and his wife each ran one) selling the same categories of data at two different price models (his: bulk list pricing, e.g., $500 for every US realtor, $200 for a state-level list; hers: per-lead pricing, roughly $0.50+ per lead depending on volume/specificity) to capture both types of buyers.
- List accuracy/validation: emails were cheap/easy to re-verify; phone numbers were harder — used a call center in the Philippines to hand-dial a randomized subset (e.g., 500 out of a 10,000-record list) and produce a disposition report (disconnects, wrong numbers) to estimate overall list accuracy.
- This grew into a broader agency serving small/medium businesses and some Fortune 500 clients with custom cleaned prospect lists, sustaining roughly high-six to low-seven-figure annual profit for about a decade before transitioning to SaaS.
- Also mentions (as a cautionary tale, not endorsed for replication) a related side venture buying distressed out-of-state houses sight-unseen based on cold-texted lowball offers derived from scraped Zillow "for sale by owner" listings and Zestimate data (offering ~40% under estimated value) — resulted in losing money on properties they never physically inspected; specifically warns that automated/remote real estate investing (his framing: "this is why I'm short OpenDoor") systematically misses hyperlocal knowledge (school district issues, rezoning, etc.) that AI/data alone can't capture, which is why large-scale automated home-flipping platforms have historically struggled to be profitable.

## Business #2: UpLead (B2B sales lead/data SaaS)
- Attempted to build a "Sales Leads" SaaS platform via an outsourced dev team found on Elance back around 2011; the contracted team took payment and disappeared without delivering or refunding — this bad experience delayed his SaaS pivot by roughly 5 years.
- Eventually launched UpLead in 2017, explicitly narrowing scope to B2B-only data (vs. mixing B2B and B2C, which a major incumbent, Sales Genie/InfoUSA, did) — deliberate positioning against a specific competitor gap.
- Revenue ramp: roughly $2K → $5K → $8K → $10K MRR within the first several months; kept running the legacy list-brokering agency in parallel for about the first 6 months for cash flow, then fully pivoted once SaaS MRR hit $10-20K. First-year revenue was roughly $1M ARR, driven substantially by cold email (with a documented multi-step follow-up sequence, not a single email).
- Ran the business with no employees initially (himself and his wife, doing customer support from the car while driving their child to preschool); first hire was a customer support person (still with the company at time of filming). Notes that hiring/team-building was the hardest part of scaling, requiring trial and error since he had no prior experience managing a large organization.

## Business #3: Signaturely (e-signature SaaS, launched 2020)
- Method for choosing this market: went to G2 (formerly G2 Crowd, a B2B software review/comparison site) specifically to find a large, fragmented market with multiple billion-dollar-plus incumbents (a signal of a big total addressable market with room for multiple winners) — explicitly avoided WYSIWYG "pick a random idea" approach.
- Selected e-signatures partly on market-growth logic: at the time (2020), reportedly only about 1% of all documents signed globally were signed electronically, implying a long runway of category growth (and COVID-driven demand for remote/electronic signing accelerated the case).
- Before building anything, validated an "ability to acquire customers profitably" — specifically identified that top competitors (DocuSign, Adobe Sign) drove significant traffic via free tools/templates (signature generators, contract/agreement templates) optimized for SEO, a channel he already had deep expertise in from his list-brokering SEO experience (tens of millions of cumulative site visits driven across his site portfolio) — treated this SEO advantage as the specific edge justifying entry into an already-dominated market.
- Differentiation angle: read G2 reviews of competitors and found a recurring complaint that DocuSign/Adobe Sign were too complex (built for enterprise clients) — built Signaturely to be radically simpler ("stupid simple signatures" — no API, no contract lifecycle management, just upload a document and route it for signature), aiming for a tool "your grandmother could use."
- Built the MVP using the same outsourced dev team from UpLead (sourced via Upwork). Domain name ("Signaturely") deliberately chosen to include "signature" for direct SEO benefit.
- Monetization model: freemium (3 free document signatures/month, paid tiers for more volume/teams/branding), later shifted to a free-trial-with-credit-card model (reduced revenue somewhat since most users only wanted the free tier, but kept a "backdoor" free plan available).
- Current scale (at filming): 1.9 million total users (approaching 2 million), but 99%+ on the free plan; roughly 800,000-900,000 monthly site visits, heavily driven by the free tools/templates funnel. Revenue is comparatively modest relative to UpLead, but the guest treats the large free user base as a strategic asset/pivot option (a base he could redirect focus and monetization toward later, e.g., if he eventually sold UpLead).

## Broader untapped business ideas the guest flagged for others to pursue
1. Increasing automation of outbound sales/lead-gen workflows: predicts that within a few years, tools like his own will integrate directly with a company's CRM (Salesforce/HubSpot), auto-identify ideal customer profiles from existing closed-won data, and autonomously send outreach and book meetings with minimal manual setup — flagged as an evolving opportunity space, though not yet fully solved by anyone.
2. "Give the core software away free, monetize the ancillary services" model: build a free, hyper-specific vertical CRM/software tool (e.g., "a CRM only for dentists," built cheaply via a white-labeled platform like GoHighLevel rather than custom development) and make money instead through bundled ancillary services with better margins than SaaS subscription fees — specifically credit card payment processing (via white-labeled "interchange plus" pricing, netting roughly 0.2%-2.5% on non-Amex transaction volume), plus potentially payroll and group health insurance. Worked example: a dentist doing $100K/month in card volume currently paying $300/month for HubSpot could get an equivalent CRM for free in exchange for switching card processing to the operator's white-labeled processor, netting the operator roughly $1,000/month at a 1% average take — several times more than a $300/month software subscription fee would have generated, while costing the client nothing extra. Cites Squire (a CRM/all-in-one platform specifically for barber shops, reportedly raised $100M+, valued around $1B) as a real-world proof point for extreme vertical specialization paired with ancillary revenue streams, though Squire itself charges for its software directly rather than giving it away.
3. General framework for cloning any existing SaaS: browse G2/competitor sites and reviews to find a common complaint (e.g., "too complex"), pick one clear differentiating angle (not price, since underlying software costs are trending toward zero and can't be a durable moat), and — critically — validate customer acquisition and willingness to pay before building anything: run cold email campaigns or paid ad tests and build a landing page/funnel to gauge real demand pre-launch ("sell it before you have it"), only building the product once you've proven you can get "yeses."

## Caveats/pushback on hype
- Explicitly cautions against the current narrative that AI/no-code tools (Cursor, Lovable, Replit, etc.) make it trivial to "build DocuSign in 48 hours" and scale to $10-20M ARR — states that going from $0 to $100K ARR is fundamentally different from scaling to $10M+ ARR; building is easier than ever, but acquiring customers profitably, building a durable brand, and meeting enterprise requirements (e.g., SOC 2 compliance) remain hard, unsolved-by-AI problems.
- Reassures that most people don't need to reach massive scale — building a business generating ~$10,000/month is "life-changing for 99% of people in the world" and should be treated as a realistic, sufficient short-term goal rather than assuming you must out-build a category leader.
- Notes SaaS is "probably on a downturn" compared to its 2021 peak hype cycle, but argues incumbents retain real, non-trivial advantages (brand trust, compliance credentials, existing sales-team familiarity) that a cloned/no-code competitor can't simply out-execute on features or price alone — brand-building remains essential to being genuinely considered by buyers.

Tools/platforms named: G2 (market/competitor research via reviews), BizBuySell/BizBen (browsing businesses for sale as free market research), Upwork/Elance (outsourced dev talent — caution: Elance experience was a scam/non-delivery), GoHighLevel (white-label CRM platform for building vertical SaaS cheaply, with unlimited free sub-accounts on a $300-500/month agency plan), Twilio (custom bulk texting tool, built via a $1-2K Upwork contractor), Zillow (scraped for FSBO seller phone numbers in the cautionary real-estate example), ChatGPT/AI tools (for competitor analysis), Beehiiv (sponsor mention, newsletter platform).
