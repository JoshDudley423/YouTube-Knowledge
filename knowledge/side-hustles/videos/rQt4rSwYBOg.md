# The Ultimate Guide to Starting Your First Service Business (Part 1/2)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rQt4rSwYBOg | Published: 2025-02-24

Interview with Daniel Cronauer, who built a ~7-figure power washing/home-service business over 12 years in northeast Pennsylvania, starting from roof soft-washing and expanding into commercial coil cleaning, water tower cleaning, gas stations, and Christmas lights.

- Origin: started a roof soft-wash cleaning business at 22 after a DIY roof-cleaning experiment for a family event impressed him; noticed many well-maintained houses in his area had visibly dirty roofs (unmet local demand) and researched the process via online forums before building a homemade rig.
- Early sales technique: reused the Cutco knives door-to-door sales training structure/framework (establish rapport, show the problem, then the solution) directly for pitching roof cleaning door-to-door; also used postcards, mailers, flyers, and a basic website. Cites having no bank of local competitors doing the service yet as a first-mover advantage, though he expected competitors would follow.
- First-year numbers: ~$100,000 revenue, ~50 roofs cleaned, roughly a $2,000-ish average job value range (varied). Paid himself last, reinvested profit into equipment (e.g., bought a snowplow for off-season winter income, better rigs each year).
- Key principle repeated: "sell it before you have it" -- sold a commercial power-washing job to a restaurant needing hot water pressure washing before he owned the $7,000 hot-water power washer, then bought the equipment after landing the sale. (This first hot-water job also had an equipment mishap -- wrong-rated hose line caused diesel fuel to spray on him -- framed as a normal part of the learning curve when doing new-to-you work.)
- Landing Walmart contracts: met two people at a networking event who already served as Walmart vendors; befriended them and had them walk him through Walmart's vendor-onboarding paperwork (described as harder than working with the federal government, "so much red tape" -- but framed as a moat that filters out less committed competitors). Ended up servicing 7 Walmart stores: storefront power washing (concrete outside) plus front/back restroom cleaning, at ~$1,800/stop/month = ~$12,000/month recurring from one client relationship. Walmart doesn't allow chemical use (policy), so job costs are just water, gas, and labor -- margin on Walmart jobs cited at least 50%.
- Niche discovery -- HVAC/refrigeration condenser coil cleaning: broke in via a referral doing an emergency winter coil-cleaning job for a Wawa convenience store chain (freezing conditions, had to torch-thaw frozen equipment on site). Found this a highly profitable, low-competition niche ("I know of maybe two other companies doing only this in the Northeast").
  - Unit economics named: ~$350 per store for an Aldi-sized location, ~25 minutes of labor; ~$1,800 for a Sam's Club's refrigeration coils plus ~$1,000 for its AC units; rack-house/compressor setups similarly ~$1,000-1,800. Chemical cost negligible (~$50-60/job); revenue is mostly travel + labor, water/gas only -- no chemicals needed for most coil jobs.
  - Sales channel: sell to HVAC/refrigeration companies (who are chronically understaffed and dislike coil-cleaning grunt work), not directly to the end retailer (e.g., not directly to Aldi) -- pitch is "you don't have enough techs, subcontract the cleaning to us," which aligns incentives (saves the HVAC company time/money) versus being seen as "just another sales guy" when pitching the retailer directly.
  - Seasonality/limits: coil cleaning has a tight demand window (roughly March-June, ahead of peak summer heat and after pollen season hits) which complicates scaling/staffing; residential coil cleaning exists as a want but wasn't pursued at scale by this business due to logistics -- suggested go-to-market for someone starting fresh in residential coil cleaning would still be through HVAC companies with preventive-maintenance contracts, since homeowners rarely initiate the service themselves and residential units are simpler (easier "just unplug it" work) than commercial.
  - Revenue scale: coil cleaning alone does ~$500-600K/year for this business and is a reliably recurring/predictable annual revenue stream (unlike one-off roof jobs), which the owner explicitly values over lumpier revenue.
- Business expansion via saying yes to opportunities: agreed to travel to service another company's expanding footprint (drove 6 hours to do work in Ohio) rather than that company hiring a new $80K/year employee -- got paid $25,000 for taking on that added workload themselves. General advice given: "say yes to opportunity... you can always back out later."
- Water tower cleaning: found via networking with a water-utility contact; commands premium pricing because of high barriers to entry (must be a "legitimate" vetted vendor, not a "Joe's power washing" outfit) -- water tower jobs quoted at $6,000-$12,000+ per tower, plus lift rental at $800-$2,000 depending on lift size; typically completed in a single day; costs are roughly $200 chemical (bleach) + $100 gas + labor. Philosophy stated: "don't compete, dominate" -- deliberately targeting the high end of the market (few competitors, better rates) rather than competing on price at the bottom.
- Gas station chain contracts (cautionary, lower-margin example): won a large multi-state bid (~700 gas stations across Pennsylvania, NY, Massachusetts, Connecticut for a "Turkey Hill"-type chain) but described gas station corporate contracts as low-margin/commoditized -- example: was pressured down from an $800/store quote to $450/store after a competitor undercut, because the regional decision-maker awarding the contract has no incentive to pay more (won't get fired over "a dirty gas station" but will get fired for going over budget). At $450/store, jobs could be completed by 2 techs in as little as 15 minutes each; owner instructed crews to stop and leave once they'd logged one hour of work on unprofitable contracts. Notes that some other gas station chains/owners have more pride/investment and pay meaningfully better rates for the same service.
- Pay structure for technicians: hourly base pay ($17-22/hour) plus a percentage commission of each job's revenue (lead tech ~5%, assistant tech ~3%) -- intended to give techs a stake in high-value jobs (e.g., a lucrative water tower job) rather than flat hourly pay, which increases motivation/buy-in.
- Christmas lights business: started ~8 years before this recording as a residential add-on; briefly sold it to an employee who mismanaged the money and had to take the business back; after regaining it, grew customer count from ~12 to ~250; projected to do $450,000 in revenue the following year. Valued specifically for its recurring/reoccurring annual revenue.
- Current business structure (~12 years old, seven figures overall): a residential division ("Suds" branch, ~3-5 person crew) doing $300-350K/year in residential washing; a commercial division (~4 trucks/2 techs each) doing gas stations, water towers, and coil cleaning.

Caveats/risks:
- Gas station corporate contracts can be a low-margin trap -- decision-makers are incentivized to minimize cost, not quality, and will happily switch vendors for a lower bid; profitability requires very fast, tightly standardized execution or should be avoided/renegotiated.
- Walmart-style enterprise vendor relationships require navigating heavy administrative/compliance red tape (vendor packets, system access) that can be a genuine barrier for less persistent operators -- framed as a moat, but a real cost to entering.
- Coil cleaning's narrow seasonal window (roughly March-June) makes year-round staffing and scaling harder than a business with steady demand.
- Not all Home Service niches translate cleanly to residential -- the interviewee explicitly says coil cleaning doesn't make sense residentially at his company's current scale/logistics, even though the maintenance need is real, because it doesn't fit his existing operating model (large multi-store commercial routes).
