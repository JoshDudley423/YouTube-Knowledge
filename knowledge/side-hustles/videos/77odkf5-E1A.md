# How to Make $300k/Year from a Laundromat
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=77odkf5-E1A | Published: 2024-08-28

Guest: Tyler Purcell (Cincinnati, OH) — owns 4 laundromats ("Laundry Spot") alongside running a wealth management firm (4 advisors, ~$250M AUM) as his primary full-time job.

## The idea
- Buy/build laundromats (self-service coin/card laundry) in markets with existing but poorly-run, outdated competitors ("zombie mats"), and out-execute them with modern equipment, cleanliness, and customer service. Also layer in commercial laundry accounts and residential pickup/delivery.
- Started because his father-in-law already owned a laundromat and pitched him and his brother-in-law to partner and expand; Tyler initially dismissed the idea ("not sexy") until seeing the books — a single existing location was netting ~$250,000/year.

## Numbers
- Store economics: average store nets a bit over $300,000/year. Company-wide: ~$3M revenue / ~$1.1M net last year; on pace for ~$3.2M revenue / ~$1.4M net this year (4 locations). Target/typical margin: 30-40%.
- Startup cost: a new 4,000-5,000 sq ft store costs ~$1.5M all-in (equipment + buildout) at current prices; they put 20% down (~$300K) and financed the rest as a combined business+equipment loan through a local bank relationship (equipment manufacturers and specialty lenders for laundromats/car washes are also financing options).
- Depreciation benefit: ~$800,000 of laundry machines in a store can be depreciated (or bonus-depreciated), a major tax advantage similar to car washes.
- Lease example: one location has a 30-year lease with five-year renewal options, at under $14/sq ft even 25 years in — described as effectively an appreciating asset since market rent will likely double or triple over the lease term.
- Pricing: only modestly more expensive than nearby competitors — roughly $0.50 more per load, about a 15% premium (e.g., $3.50-$4 vs. competitors on a small load).
- Average spend per visit: ~$20. An 80 lb large machine costs $11.25-$25 to wash, plus a few dollars to dry — a single big load can already run ~$15.
- Payment mix: ~35% of customers still pay with quarters/coins; the rest use loyalty cards, credit/debit. They intentionally don't push customers away from cash/coins — their target demographic often avoids leaving personal information or using cards.
- Grand opening ramp: after grand-opening weekends, they're already running at roughly a $500K annual revenue run rate in month one.
- Residential pickup & delivery (newer line of business): ~35 recurring weekly residential customers as of filming, up from 0 six months earlier. Priced at $2/pound; bags hold up to 25 lbs (so a full bag ≈ $50, fully inclusive of folding and delivery). Driver pay: $20-22/hour — route density (driver already in the area) is critical to profitability since a 20-25 lb pickup is a small ticket relative to driving time.
- Track record: claims zero location failures to date; has put an estimated 11 competing "zombie mat" businesses out of business.

## Why laundromats over other "sweaty" businesses (comparison to car washes)
- Similarities to car washes: heavy equipment depreciation benefits; both are "boring," historically under-invested categories now attracting more capital/innovation (Tyler frames laundromats as roughly 5-10 years behind where car washes are now in terms of modernization/consolidation).
- Key difference favoring laundromats: recession/downturn resilience. Car wash subscriptions are a discretionary expense people cancel first when money is tight; laundry is a necessity people keep paying for even in hard times (cites his business growing, not shrinking, through COVID). Analogized to mobile home/RV park rent by the host — necessities get paid before discretionary subscriptions.
- Perception gap that creates the opportunity: most people picture laundromats as the "25-year-old dirty, crime-ridden" type, and don't realize modern washers exist at home too — but even upper-middle-class customers show up when their home washer breaks, valuing cleanliness, safety, and staffing over price.

## Site selection / how they find and win locations
1. Look for geographic pockets already containing 3-4 laundromats ("zombie mats") that are outdated (haven't been updated in ~20 years) but still have real customer traffic — the traffic proves demand exists despite terrible execution (visible cockroaches, no modern equipment cited as a real example that motivated their first location).
2. Do hands-on due diligence on those competitors: personally do laundry there, record equipment brands/pricing, and even take water-usage readings to back-calculate their approximate revenue and validate the size of the local market. Then run standard demographic reports to cross-check that data.
3. Explicitly choose NOT to buy and renovate existing old laundromats — infrastructure (utilities, layout, space for modern high-capacity washers) is usually inadequate, making a fresh build ("white box" or new construction) more cost-effective than retrofitting, even though it means starting without an existing customer base.
4. Prioritize visibility, signage, and — critically — ample parking (20-30 spaces) over the prestige of the surrounding retail center; one of their best-performing stores is in an otherwise struggling strip mall (vape shops, tattoo parlors) that the landlord was initially reluctant to lease to, but has since filled up entirely, driven partly by the laundromat's traffic ("elevated the whole shopping center," compared to how an Apple Store or Tesla lifts a mall's profile).
5. Lock in long-term leases (aim for 20-30+ years with renewal options) since rent will likely rise substantially over decades while their locked rate stays flat.

## Grand opening playbook (explicit, repeatable tactics)
- Physical mail works well for this demographic: send 2 rounds of "coming soon" postcards, then postcards for a grand-opening promotion.
- Run back-to-back FREE laundry weekends (started with one, expanded to two after seeing results) — the marginal cost is only utilities and staff time, but it drives massive word-of-mouth: people who came the first weekend tell friends/family, roughly doubling attendance the second weekend.
- Combine with PR/community outreach: contact local papers for interviews, run Google/social ads, and — for their most recent opening — hosted a "VIP" red-carpet event with catering, a bartender, and a live band, inviting city officials and local business owners; generated local news coverage, new commercial accounts, and goodwill with city officials (who otherwise still hold the outdated "laundromat = bad" stereotype).
- All owners and ~12 rotating staff work the free weekends hands-on, aiming to convert the free-traffic spike into hundreds of 5-star reviews immediately.
- Saturday/Sunday (especially Sunday) are by far the busiest days — more laundry gets done on the weekend than Monday-Friday combined — so grand-opening promotions are scheduled on weekends when people are actually free, not weekdays.
- Ongoing seasonal promos to maintain novelty without permanently discounting: e.g., "Free Dry July" (free drying the whole month) — deliberately kept as an occasional/limited-time event (compared explicitly to McDonald's McRib/Shamrock Shake scarcity marketing) rather than a permanent price cut, to preserve both specialty and margin.

## Pricing/discounting experiments (what didn't work)
- Tried moving weekend-only capacity-constrained stores' overflow to weekdays via discounts (up to 20% off, free dry) — did not move demand at all; weekend timing is driven by customers' work schedules (many are hourly/shift workers with no weekday laundry time), not price.
- Explicitly rejected weekend surge pricing (raising prices when busiest) on ethical grounds — described as feeling like "surge pricing for the poor," inconsistent with their goal of providing a fair, non-exploitative service even where they have pricing power.
- Considered but didn't pursue idea of non-laundry incentives (e.g., a Planet Fitness-style "free pizza" weekday draw) — untested but the operator was skeptical it would shift behavior.

## Business lines beyond self-service retail
- **Commercial laundry accounts** (their current growth focus): picking up large volumes (~2,000 lbs at a time) from businesses and processing centrally — much better margins/efficiency than self-service walk-ins; large national players (Cintas, UniFirst) dominate this but Tyler sees it as underexploited by typical laundromat owners.
- **Residential pickup & delivery** (early stage, growing): built on top of existing commercial delivery routes/driver efficiency rather than as a standalone business — Tyler explicitly warns this does NOT work as a standalone startup (a solo entrepreneur without an existing profitable laundromat trying to be "the Uber of laundry"): margins are too thin, and several VC-funded pickup/delivery-only laundry startups (some raising ~$20M+) have failed because their washers sit idle most of the time without a public-facing retail location subsidizing utilization. The economics only work when residential delivery is a value-added layer on an already-profitable physical laundromat with commercial route density.
- Outsources dry cleaning (positions the business as a "one-stop shop" for all laundry/dry-cleaning needs without operating dry cleaning in-house).
- Target underserved segments for delivery: young single professionals (time-poor, low laundry volume) and elderly customers (physically can't do their own laundry); large families with multiple kids in sports are seen as an untapped, high-need segment, but cost is currently prohibitive at scale for that use case.

## Operational/security details
- Coin handling isn't treated as risky/burdensome: security cameras are good enough to zoom in and read bill serial numbers, deterring scams (e.g., customers falsely claiming they inserted a $20 rather than a $5) — this reputation for tight tracking reportedly reduces crime/drug activity at their locations compared to typical "zombie mats," even among the same customer population.
- Bookkeeping in the industry is notoriously poor: Tyler notes that when evaluating laundromats for potential purchase, most existing owners have no real P&L, just an informal spreadsheet — making revenue/income very hard to verify when buying an existing laundromat.
- Customer data capture is limited by design/demographic preference — most customers don't want to give out personal information (part of why 35% still use coins); the only real data capture is via loyalty cards, and via targeted postcard campaigns offering a $30 loyalty-card credit to new customers, which lets them estimate rough acquisition cost vs. long-term lifetime value (framed as very high since a retained customer may visit weekly for years).

## Caveats / risks
- Committing capital requires patience for lease negotiation and location vetting — a wrong location choice (e.g., insufficient parking, so lots fill up before the store is at capacity) caps growth permanently regardless of demand.
- Residential pickup-and-delivery-only laundry startups are explicitly called out as a bad standalone business for someone without an existing profitable laundromat — despite the apparent market size, changing consumer habits around laundry (much stickier/more personal than other convenience services) plus thin margins have sunk well-funded competitors.
- Customers are highly habit-attached to how their laundry is done (specific detergents, folding preferences) — hard to convert loyalty from self-service to paid delivery service at scale.
- Ethical tension exists between profit-maximizing pricing (e.g., weekend surge pricing) and community-oriented positioning; the founders have consciously chosen not to exploit pricing power even where it would likely increase margin.
- The equipment/decor side of the industry (unattractive industrial-looking machines) is acknowledged as an unmet market need, but Tyler doesn't see enough addressable market size in "prettifying" laundry equipment (e.g., vinyl wraps) to be its own standalone business, since forward-thinking operators like Tyler are a small fraction of the market.
