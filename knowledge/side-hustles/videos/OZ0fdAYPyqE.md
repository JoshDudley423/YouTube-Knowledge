# How to Start Reselling (Even With $0 in the Bank)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OZ0fdAYPyqE | Published: 2025-07-23

Interview with Shannon Jean (businessbrainshow), a professional liquidation reseller, walking through live auction listings and reselling strategy. Extremely dense — Shannon runs a paid Reseller Mastermind (shannonjean.com/mastermind) and resale-permit resource (shannonjean.com/resale).

## Zero-cost entry point
- You can start reselling with no capital: pick up free items posted on Facebook Marketplace (e.g., free couches with minor flaws), clean/restage/re-photograph them, and resell for profit purely on sweat equity. Example cited: a member ("Mike," a weatherman) sources free couches this way as a side project.
- College towns are a strong niche for used furniture/couch flipping because turnover is constant (students/frats/sororities cycling furniture every year); target move-in/move-out windows (July for back-to-school) and Facebook groups like "Flown and Grown."

## Core sourcing method: liquidation auctions
- Primary marketplaces named: B-Stock, GovDeals, Costco liquidation channels, and manufacturer/retailer-run auction sites (e.g., Target, Advance Auto Parts liquidations after being acquired by Pep Boys).
- **Rule #1**: Never buy without seeing the manifest (itemized list/spreadsheet of exact contents and condition) — without it, you have no idea what you're actually getting and no recourse.
- Ignore listed "MSRP/retail" or "extended retail" values — these are inflated; sellers often list liquidation lots at claimed retail values many multiples above what they'll actually sell for. Estimate real value independently (e.g., mentally halve or more the stated retail).
- Buy close to home to minimize freight/shipping cost — factor freight into your maximum bid.
- "Buy with math, sell with emotion": don't buy based on hype/excitement (math must work on paper first), but do sell using storytelling/personal narrative (e.g., "helping my kids pay for college," "adding an in-law suite") — people buy from people, and a personal story lets you avoid competing purely on price, especially for commodity goods.
- Bid at the *end* of the auction using a preset auto-bid/max amount and then walk away — don't get emotionally invested or babysit results; there's always another similar lot the following week. Costco-brand liquidation auctions close on weekends and tend to sell for less because fewer people are watching then — bid there deliberately.
- Reverse-engineer your bid from your expected resale price: decide your target sale price, subtract shipping/freight/fees, and that's your maximum bid — walk away if the seller won't hit that number.
- Before buying, **list the item for sale on Facebook Marketplace using stock/manifest photos before you own it**, just to test click/message demand — a research technique almost nobody uses, and it can save you from a bad purchase.
- Avoid "sexy"/hyped items with heavy existing competition and thin margins (e.g., iPhones, sneakers, Stanley tumblers) — even at a steep nominal discount, popular high-demand-supply-matched items get bid up close to their real resale value, leaving no margin ("upside down" risk). Prefer boring/overlooked categories (batteries, auto parts, rugs, tumblers in unpopular colors, generic furniture) where competition is thin.
- Buying in bulk/quantity reduces per-unit risk — e.g., 9 units of an item means you only need modest individual sell-through, not one perfect sale.
- Expect some inventory in any given liquidation lot will never sell — plan to donate or recycle the unsellable tail rather than force sales.

## Specific example deals discussed (illustrating math, not to be taken as still-live prices)
- Target liquidation pallets (sporting goods, new/unsold, not returns): a $250-retail floating dock available at ~$3.78/unit landed cost (~99% off); a $450-retail inflatable pool similarly deep-discounted. Advice: identify the one or two highest-value items in a lot that can be resold quickly to recoup the whole investment, then treat the rest as gravy.
- Walker Edison flat-pack furniture (via B-Stock, direct from manufacturer, no middleman markup): 16-pallet lots claimed at $76,000 retail bid up to only ~$2,825-5,000 (~95% off); strong fit for Facebook Marketplace because furniture buyers default to FB Marketplace over eBay/Amazon. Suggested marketing tactic: film a time-lapse assembly video as your listing's lead media (mirrors "stump grinding" style attention-grabbing content) and reference IKEA-style branding language even though it's a different company. A specific rattan king bed example: landed cost ~$30/unit vs. comparable new listings selling $400-1,000+ on Facebook Marketplace in that metro.
- Stanley 40oz tumblers (bad example, cited as a deal to AVOID): despite brand popularity, bid price was already ~50% of realistic resale value ($14.35/unit bid vs. ~$25 realistic resale, when true market value elsewhere was checked) — a trap because emotional/hype buying inflates the auction bid past the real margin. Lesson: high perceived demand for a well-known brand doesn't guarantee auction discount reflects real arbitrage room; check actual sold comps first.
- GP Gold Peak Ultra Plus AA batteries (GovDeals, 8,000 units, 10-year shelf life per date code): each battery resells around $1/unit (vs ~$2/unit in Amazon retail packs) — an appealing deal because it's small/cheap to ship (fits in a padded envelope, no special packaging), sells reliably as a commodity everyone eventually needs, and you can ask sellers clarifying questions directly on GovDeals before bidding (used here to confirm consistent 2031 expiration dates across all cases).
- Wholesaling tactic: rather than retail-selling every unit yourself, approach local retailers (hobby shops, discount/close-out stores like Ollie's) empty-handed, ask if they buy from individual resellers, and pitch a bulk price for e.g. 500 units once you have a relationship — framed explicitly as a relationship business (build rapport, bring donuts, follow up).
- Costco liquidation as a geographic-arbitrage play: Costco is trusted not to inflate its "retail" figures (already sells at a discount), so its liquidation manifests are more reliable. Many mid-size US metros (100,000s of people) have no Costco — e.g., Waco TX (~280,000), Jackson MS, Amarillo TX, Lubbock TX, Laredo TX, Topeka KS — creating a market for reselling authentic Costco liquidation goods (or organizing group-buy runs) to underserved communities. Same logic applied to IKEA: the host personally ran a business ("My Modern South" in Huntsville, AL, pop. ~500K, no IKEA) buying customer IKEA orders in bulk at the nearest IKEA store, adding a ~30% markup, collecting payment up front, and delivering via a rented U-Haul to a local parking-lot pickup window.
- Advance Auto Parts liquidation (post-acquisition by Pep Boys): large lots of ignition/emission parts, brake components, etc., available deeply discounted (a 3,911-piece lot claimed ~$126K retail, bid only ~$4,000-7,000). Advised against buying without category expertise, since niche parts (e.g., "speed calibrator," "leaf spring," "clutch pack") vary wildly in real resale demand — recommended using AI (e.g., feeding an exported manifest to ChatGPT/Perplexity/Manus) to identify "gold" (high-demand, low-supply SKUs) by cross-referencing eBay sold listings, Facebook Marketplace, forums, and subreddits, then manually verifying AI's picks. Alternative: option to buy the whole lot cheap and resell it wholesale in bulk to an industry buyer at a markup without ever retailing individual pieces (e.g., buy for $6-7K, resell the full manifest to an in-industry buyer for $25K).
- M&A tracking as a sourcing signal: watch news for company acquisitions/mergers (e.g., Dick's Sporting Goods acquiring Foot Locker) — these frequently trigger warehouse consolidations and liquidation events in the following months; get ahead of competitors by tracking this and pre-positioning for resulting auctions (example: sneaker liquidation auctions followed the Foot Locker acquisition).
- Seasonal arbitrage — a major recurring theme: buy off-season liquidated seasonal goods (Christmas trees, Halloween decor, pool/summer items) at steep discounts (often <$5-10/unit) right after or mid-way through the following buying season, then resell near-full retail once the actual season arrives. Named driver: large volumes of post-holiday returns (e.g., people returning Christmas trees to Costco on Dec 26, no-questions-asked) get relisted as liquidation lots. Example: 158 new artificial Christmas trees, retail-claimed ~$11,710, bid at $526 (a handful of interested bidders) — resold near full retail ($65-89/unit) once the season arrives. A mastermind member is opening a full seasonal artificial-Christmas-tree lot business built from year-round liquidation buys; suggested enhancement: also partner with a real tree farm to sell both real and fake trees from the same lot and capture the whole local market.
- Lego/collectible sets (e.g., a Costco-exclusive Lego Star Wars Grogu set, 140 units): treated as a specialized, higher-knowledge "investment grade" category — Costco listings are more trustworthy on stated value since Costco rarely inflates prices. Popular/scarce sets can resell well above retail once officially sold out (e.g., $80 retail sets fetching $199 on eBay) but require category expertise; recommended networking with dedicated Lego resellers on X (Twitter) for guidance, and potentially partnering/splitting a large lot purchase with a knowledgeable partner.
- Blackstone griddles (Costco return/liquidation, "AB grade" condition — i.e., appearance-graded, may be dirty/greasy or pristine): 3 pallets available for ~$625 total; sold openly as used/ex-Costco stock, landed cost under $1,000 for multiple units that can resell around $300-400+ each — cited example of $3,000+ profit potential in a single weekend reselling several units on Facebook Marketplace (the brand name itself, "Blackstone," drives high search interest).
- Similar high-velocity examples cited: Costco ice-maker liquidation flipper who buys 1-2 pallets on Thursday, lists by Friday, sells out and recoups investment by Sunday, netting ~$1,900/week; liquidated designer/name-brand rugs (e.g., Ruggable) bought at ~$7.58/unit and resold for $100-120/unit on Facebook Marketplace, since buyers want large affordable rugs without paying full retail.
- Beyond pure reselling: liquidation/auction sites are also useful for personal savings/business infrastructure — examples given include a 32x12 modular building sold for $6,000, dual shipping-container modular office units for $2,500, and 2,200 sq ft of reclaimed European oak flooring (removed from a Dartmouth College renovation) sold for $360 — useful to remodelers, house flippers, or anyone needing cheap structures/materials, not just resale inventory.
- Google Glass Explorer Edition example (1,000 brand-new units, GovDeals): illustrates a niche where you don't need to sell every unit at top price — sell enough (e.g., 500 units at $150 wholesale) to recover the full ~$45,000 investment, then hold and slowly sell the remainder at a higher price ($199+) with no risk, treating some as potential future collectibles.
- Negotiating tip for oversized/unsold municipal lots: if a private seller has too large a lot to move (e.g., 1,000 units with zero bids), suggest they break it into smaller sub-lots (e.g., lots of 100) — smaller price points are easier for buyers to commit to and increase the chance of a sale.

## Caveats
- Manifests and condition grading vary — "like new" store-liquidation stock and "AB grade" store-returns are different risk profiles (returns may be used, missing parts, dirty, etc.); this is inherently a hands-on business ("you get your hands dirty").
- Category expertise matters a lot; buying outside your knowledge (auto parts, Legos) without research or an industry contact risks paying more than real resale value.
- Consumer electronics generally recommended against — margins have been squeezed to near nothing across the reselling community.
- Popular, well-known brands (Stanley tumblers) are often NOT good deals because bidding competition already prices in the brand premium — real opportunity tends to be in "boring" overlooked categories.
- Selling with genuine personal narrative works, but the reseller still needs enough volume/quantity in a lot to make solving logistics (freight, storage, per-unit fulfillment/shipping) worthwhile.
