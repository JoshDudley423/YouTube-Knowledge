# A New AI Gold Rush Just Started. Don't Miss This One. (+4 Ideas)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E1mdDql_2yI | Published: 2025-08-25

Interview with Cody Schneider (repeat guest, builder of Graphed.com) covering multiple concrete, automatable AI-powered business ideas — mostly one-person, agency-style services built on top of AI tools.

## Idea 1: AI-avatar UGC ad agency for e-commerce/supplement brands
- Enabling tech: newer AI-avatar platforms (example named: makeugc.ai) can now generate avatars that physically **hold a product** in video, using only uploaded product photos — previously avatars could only talk, not interact with physical goods. Search volume data cited: interest in this roughly tripled year-over-year (from ~1,600/month to ~4,300/month, per the tool shown on screen).
- Old process (pre-AI): hire real UGC creators (~$100/video), physically ship them the product, get exactly one script read per creator — 10 ad variations meant $1,000 and weeks of coordination, with a high failure/rejection rate (often only 1 of 10 usable).
- New process: generate ~1,000 creative variations (different hooks/scripts, same or different AI avatars) without shipping any physical product; the human's job shifts entirely to curation — reviewing and selecting the top performers rather than producing content directly.
- Target niche recommended: **supplement e-commerce brands** specifically — reasoning: supplements are ~15% (~1 in 10) of e-commerce sales, margins are high, it's a recurring/subscription-friendly category, and these brands already spend a large percentage of revenue on paid ads (targeting businesses "addicted to ads" rather than trying to convince ad-skeptical businesses to start).
- Service offer/deliverable: e.g., 25 pieces of ad creative per week, done-for-you (hook research, script writing, video generation/editing), delivered to the brand's own ad account — the agency doesn't run the ads themselves, just supplies creative based on a shared performance dashboard, iterating each week's batch off the prior week's top performers.
- Pricing/economics: suggested $5,000/month per client is easily justified (cites existing agencies charging ~$8,000/month for just 10 videos); to hit ~$1M/year as a solo operator, need ~16 clients at $5K/month, each requiring ~2 hours/week of curation work (~32 hours/week total) — framed as "a one person business that can easily make like $80K a month."
- Client acquisition approach: identify e-commerce supplement brands (many doing ~$10M/year with only ~5 employees), cold email the founders directly with 5 sample ads already made based on researched pain points, delivered via a shared Google Drive folder as proof of capability.
- Best-performing winners can optionally be "leveled up" by handing them to a real human creator to remake in higher production quality — this is additive to, not competing with, the brand's existing agency/creative team.
- Research method for niching down within a category (e.g., "creatine gummies for female long-distance runners"): scrape Reddit for real customer quotes/pain points about the specific sub-niche, then build ad hooks/scripts directly from those quotes.

## Idea 2: Mining Reddit/forums for underserved local-business niches (e.g., med spa lead gen)
- Method: use Perplexity (or ChatGPT's deep research mode) with a simple prompt like "what are the main pain points [industry] have in growing the business, Reddit" to surface real, aggregated complaints from public forums (Reddit, Instagram comments, Facebook groups).
- Worked example — med spas: surfaced pain points included client acquisition/retention, high operating/startup costs (avoided — financing is a different business), **staffing/turnover** (a possible staffing-pipeline business), and **over-reliance on discount platforms like Groupon** (flagged as the most promising angle) — Groupon customers are characterized (per scraped forum sentiment) as rarely rebooking and quick to leave negative reviews, which becomes a specific, resonant cold-outreach angle ("tired of getting Groupon's worst customers?").
- Economics reasoning: estimated med spa customer lifetime value ~$5,000 (repeat visits 4-8x/year), which supports charging ~$1,000 per acquired paying customer as a lead-gen fee (a ~5x ROAS for the client) — used to justify a high per-lead price point.
- Layered service ideas that fall out of the same pain-point research: paid lead gen via Facebook ads targeted at women 30-50 in the top income bracket (matches typical med spa clientele demographics), local SEO / Google Maps ranking optimization (consistent NAP — name/address/phone — across directory listings like MapQuest, Foursquare, Yelp; keyword-optimized business profile with photos), social media management, and even adjacent unrelated services (e.g., a cleaning business for "facility standards/ambiance" complaints, or a staffing pipeline).
- General workflow praised: iterative "deep research" prompting — start broad, pick the most promising bullet point, open a fresh deep-research prompt on just that angle, and keep drilling down until you have "a perfect playbook of exactly what to copy," including studying successful existing operators in the niche (their pricing history, landing page evolution, fulfillment method) via targeted deep-research prompts on them by name.
- General principle stated: online/internet businesses should be thought of the same way as saturated-but-still-profitable local service businesses (e.g., a city having 80+ window-washing companies that each still turn a profit) — there's room for many competitors of varying quality in a given online niche, not just one winner.

## Idea 3: Webinar-to-ebook lead magnet service (B2B)
- Premise: over 60% of B2B companies run a webinar monthly (nearly all run at least one per year) as a marketing tactic — a very large, easily identifiable target market (any company with a marketing team).
- Offer: convert a company's existing webinar recordings into a written ebook/PDF lead magnet, which the company then promotes on LinkedIn ("comment 'guide' for the free PDF") to grow their email list and re-market future webinars — described as a self-reinforcing flywheel (more list growth → bigger next webinar → more webinar content → more lead magnets).
- Production process: take the webinar transcript, feed it to Claude with a prompt like "write an ebook based on this transcript with key insights and takeaways," then format the output in Canva — described as low-effort ("transcript, Claude, Canva").
- Lead-sourcing/outreach method: search YouTube for "webinar," filter by recency (posted that week) and length (20+ minutes) to find companies currently running webinars; find company/marketer emails via Apollo; cold email and/or LinkedIn-DM offering a completed sample ebook made from their most recent webinar for free, then pitch the ongoing paid service.
- Pricing: suggested ~$1,000 per webinar processed (a company running 4 webinars/month = $4,000/month), or an unlimited-volume flat retainer (~$8,000/month, including back-catalog webinars).
- Optional viral/network-effect variant: offer a discounted retainer (e.g., half price) in exchange for permission to market your own webinar-to-ebook service to the attendee email list generated by that webinar — since webinar attendees often work at other companies that also run webinars, creating a referral loop.
- Rationale for targeting an "unsexy," non-technical niche: low competition (few people are hunting a random low-subscriber YouTube channel's webinar to cold-pitch it), decent budgets (webinar marketing already has proven ROI so companies know it works and are willing to pay to scale it), and companies buying this service already know how to do it themselves but simply lack the bandwidth — objection risk is treated as near-zero ("they know how to scrub their floors but still hire a cleaning company"). Personal anecdote: guest grew a company's (Rupa Health) live weekly webinar series from nonexistent to ~4,000 attendees/episode using this general content+list-building strategy.
- Suggested positioning refinement: niche the agency to a single vertical (e.g., only biotech, only cybersecurity webinars) to build referenceable social proof/name recognition within that space, same logic as niching an SEO agency to one category (e.g., "SEO for e-commerce beauty brands").
- Tool mentioned for semi-automating LinkedIn lead capture from webinar comments: Phantom Buster (~$50/month); alternative is a human VA manually handling replies/nurturing since it can perform better for high-ticket handoffs.

## Idea 4 (bonus, non-AI): Niche flight-deal newsletter, city-specific
- Premise/critique of existing model: services like Scott's Cheap Flights (now rebranded "Going," reportedly ~$35M/year revenue, up from ~$3.8M in 2020) find generically "cheap" flights, but travelers actually want flights cheap **from their home airport** specifically — an underserved niche-down opportunity.
- Build process (demoed live): use Google Flights' native price-tracking/alerts feature (set "track prices" for a specific route, e.g., Dallas→London) for every major destination out of a target home city; route the resulting price-drop alert emails through an automation that reformats them into a branded newsletter (e.g., via a listener/parser feeding into Beehiiv), and send when a deal drops.
- Business/branding approach: register a simple city-specific domain (e.g., dallasflights.com, cited as available for $11 on Namecheap) for each target city and treat each as its own funnel; can be replicated across every major US city once one market's playbook is proven; a directory could aggregate all the city sites.
- Monetization: (1) paid annual subscription (Scott's Cheap Flights historically charged ~$72/year — annual pricing preferred over monthly to avoid churn when no deals appear in a given month; suggests adding a "if you don't book a flight in a year, get a full refund" guarantee to reduce buying friction, betting few people will actually claim it), (2) possible affiliate/referral kickbacks from airlines/booking sites for driven bookings, (3) sponsored ad slots sold across the full network of city-specific newsletters once scaled (e.g., a hotel chain buying a promotion across all newsletters).
- Framed as buildable in a weekend, requiring only "some capital and time" to scale to the tens-of-millions-per-year range demonstrated by the Going.com comparable.

## Bonus tangent: Nextdoor-based lawn care marketing (non-AI, cited as a real example)
- An 18-year-old's lawn care business acquires 100% of its customers organically through Nextdoor: posts before/after photos of jobs done in a specific neighborhood ("I just did [address]'s lawn — message me if interested"), relying on hyper-local social proof (neighbors recognizing the specific house/street) to generate inbound requests, no paid ads.
- Suggested cold-start variant for quickly generating income (e.g., "make $50K this summer"): door-knock offering one free lawn job specifically to get a real before/after post and the resulting Nextdoor social proof snowball — hosts note this only works ethically if the "did your neighbor's lawn" claim is literally true (contrasted with contractors who falsely claim to have serviced a neighbor to build trust).
