# Don't Sleep on This Specific $0-to-Millions Reselling Strategy
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QYRz3WgE7eY | Published: 2025-04-16

Interview with Shannon Jean (serial reseller/liquidator, founder of ~10 companies, cohost of Business Brain Show, $5/month mastermind at shannonjean.com/mastermind). Part 1 of 2. Core theme: liquidation-lot reselling — buying pallets/truckloads of overstock/returned goods at 2-5 cents on the dollar and reselling at 20-30 cents on the dollar.

## The idea
- Buy liquidation lots/pallets/truckloads (via B2B liquidation marketplaces) at deep discounts and resell individually or in bulk to consumers, other resellers, or businesses (contractors, screen printers, sporting goods shops, etc.).
- Shannon's own path: iPhone/electronics repair and resale → luxury handbags (spent ~1 year deliberately learning the category) → now moving toward industrial/restaurant equipment liquidation, driven by tariff-related supply shifts pushing more US companies to buy refurbished/used industrial goods.
- Core thesis: category choice is secondary; a repeatable system/process is what actually produces results and transfers across categories (electronics, handbags, patio furniture, industrial hardware, apparel, etc.).

## Numbers
- Sold ~$4 million of handbags over 3 years (solo), netting $557,000 net profit.
- Real deal example (NFL/NBA/MLB apparel lot): paid $28,000 for ~20,000 pieces (accidentally bought mostly plus-size due to a spreadsheet reading error — cells weren't expanded); recovered the full $28,000 back after selling just ~5,000 pieces, then sold the remaining ~13,000 pieces for a total of $103,000 from the $28,000 buy-in — done out of his garage, shipped by his wife.
- Signature Hardware/Ferguson industrial liquidation example: listed store price $1.8M (rule of thumb: cut store price in half to get real value, or discount further to 25% for a "cynical" estimate — ~$435K here); lot sold at auction for $13,000; anyone with a resale certificate could bid. Adding ~$10,000 freight = ~$23,000 all-in. Selling just 4 of the most expensive items back (one at $10K, three at $8K) returned $34,000 — already profitable — while leaving ~3,000 more items unsold in inventory.
- Grainger industrial hinge lot: 1,000 specialty security door hinges (retail ~$220 each = $220,000 retail value; scrap/aluminum value estimated ~$3,000) bought at auction for $985. Buyer called the hinge manufacturer directly, got referred to a sales rep/client, and sold 200 hinges at $40 each ($8,000) while retaining 800 in stock.
- Kohler liquidation auction examples cited: a lot listed at $23,988 sold for $500; another listed at $36,533 sold for $2,300 — both all brand-new bathroom fixtures (faucets, sinks, lighting).
- Alphabroder (imprintable apparel supplier, ~$2B/year in sales) liquidation auction: a lot of brand-new jackets/polos/clothing listed at $116,929 sold for only ~$230 (5 pallets).
- Costco liquidation buys (via B-Stock): ~$12,000 worth of patio furniture bought for ~$800 (during a free-shipping promotional period); ~12 pallets of Blackstone grills bought so cheaply that "every buddy" got one for ~$100 each. Costco liquidation is considered comparatively safer/more reliable than other retailers' liquidation listings because Costco doesn't typically discount heavily up front (so its stated "store price" is closer to real, unlike other retailers where the listed value is called "a lie").
- eBay selling limit example: Shannon's own selling limit on eBay reportedly reached $5 billion (built up over ~15 years via reputation, 99%+ feedback score, same/next-day shipping, and volume history). Selling limits are based on total dollar volume of listed inventory, not number of listings (e.g., 1,000 items at $10 each = $10,000 toward the limit).
- Shannon's rough buying formula ("the product equation"): take the stated store/retail price, cut it in half (assume the listed price is inflated), then aim to pay no more than ~20% of that halved figure — i.e., roughly 10% of stated retail, landed/shipped, as a "safe" buy price.
- His stated split on what drives resale success: ~70% is having a good system/process (math-driven bidding, research, logistics), ~30% is category selection/market awareness (e.g., knowing regional demand patterns, or macro trends like tariffs driving industrial liquidation demand).

## Process / how to do it
1. **Pick a category deliberately** — look for one with more demand than supply, ideally higher-ticket (Shannon avoided consumer electronics specifically because margins have been squeezed to nothing over the past decade as failure-prone components — floppy/optical drives, etc. — disappeared from modern devices; recommends against buying phones/electronics for resale now unless parting them out for components).
2. **Budget real time to learn a category before committing** — Shannon took 8-12 months of small, low-stakes test sales (a couple hundred dollars at a time) to learn photography, "credibility statements," self-issued authenticity certificates, and platform-specific mechanics before scaling into handbags. "Never buy anything unless you know what you can already sell it for" (his "first law of auctions") — use free tools like eBay's product research (formerly paid, called Terapeak) to check 3 years of actual sold prices/volume on eBay before bidding on anything.
3. **Source liquidation lots** via B2B liquidation marketplaces — named example: B-Stock (bstock.com), the marketplace many big retailers (including Costco) use to liquidate returned/overstock inventory. Requires registering and obtaining a resale permit/certificate.
4. **Build platform relationships proactively, before you need them** (an "insurance policy" against platform risk/account bans): find and engage the actual category managers/product managers at eBay, Poshmark, etc. via LinkedIn — comment supportively on their posts, congratulate their wins, mention (briefly, once) how much volume you do on their platform, then stop and let them respond. Volunteer to speak at platform events once invited. Also connect on LinkedIn with suppliers/manufacturers up the chain in your chosen category (e.g., handbag sellers should follow luxury brand/conglomerate news like Prada acquiring Versace, and engage those companies' people directly).
5. **Differentiate via "unfair advantage."** Identify what you specifically have that competitors don't (money, warehouse space, ability to handle bulky/heavy goods, same-day local delivery capability, video/on-camera charisma for live selling, customer service skill, etc.) and lean into it rather than competing on price alone. Example cited: a mastermind member selling pressure washers on Facebook Marketplace differentiates purely by including a walkaround video (most competitors post photos only), which the Marketplace algorithm rewards with top placement.
6. **Customer service as a moat in higher-margin categories**: offer free returns unconditionally; invest in real packaging/presentation (a thank-you card, nice box, small coupon) since customers spending real money (e.g., $1,000 on a handbag) already expect to be disappointed/scammed — exceeding that expectation avoids negative feedback. On eBay specifically, refusing returns causes the platform to algorithmically bury your listings; offering free returns gets you boosted visibility.
7. **Customer complaint-handling technique ("the two tokens")**: in any dispute, you can only choose "it's no big deal" or "this is the end of the world" — always take the "end of the world" empathetic token yourself (apologize profusely, immediately offer a prepaid return label plus a discount coupon for a future purchase) rather than defending yourself/blaming a carrier, because if you minimize it, the customer escalates to fill that gap; if you catastrophize on their behalf, they frequently back off and don't even want to return the item. Don't offer a choice — decide and act immediately.
8. **In low-margin categories, don't over-invest in service** — accept it as a cost of doing business, but if repeat purchases aren't possible, extending unlimited generous service doesn't pay off the way it does with a repeat-purchase premium category. When a category's margins get squeezed to nothing (his electronics example over ~10 years, moving from desktops → printers/monitors → laptops → phones/iPads), the recommended move is to proactively exit ("work your way out of business, or someone else will") rather than keep squeezing.
9. **Selling bulk/liquidated lots — call people, don't just list/email.** Cold-call small niche businesses that would want the specific product wholesale (e.g., for an NFL jersey lot: called small sporting goods shops, sports card shops, comic shops, team-supply stores to find buyers at $30-40 wholesale prices they'd resell at $120+). For B2B/industrial or bulk apparel lots: use a data-scraping tool (Outscraper mentioned) to build contact lists of relevant local businesses (e.g., all ~30,000 local screen printers in the US) and text/call them directly with the actual manifest/inventory list and a true story about how you sourced it. Most calls go nowhere, but "one person can change your entire life."
10. **Price discovery via reverse negotiation**: instead of quoting your price first, ask the prospective bulk buyer what they'd pay; if they name a price lower than your target, you can walk away, source cheaper, or simply come back later ("I actually found more of it") and requote closer to what they said they'd pay — using manufactured scarcity/urgency ("this batch is already gone, but I might get another next week") to close at a better number.
11. **Take the first opportunity to recover your capital fast** on any large purchase — Shannon's explicit mental framework for every auction: "what do I have to do just to get my money back?" first, before thinking about total profit; once principal is recouped, remaining inventory is pure upside runway (sometimes sold off over a year or two).
12. **Study auctions continuously as market research**, even ones you don't buy — watching what obscure/niche B2B liquidators (e.g., Alphabroder, Grainger, Signature Hardware/Ferguson, Kohler) sell weekly builds pattern recognition for future deals, and crowdsourcing via social media posts/comments ("who's in the imprintable clothing business?") surfaces expert contacts and buyer leads.

## Tools/platforms/resources named
- B-Stock (bstock.com) — liquidation marketplace where major retailers (including Costco) sell overstock/returns; requires a resale permit.
- eBay, Poshmark, Mercari — resale marketplaces mentioned; eBay's free product-research tool (formerly the paid tool "Terapeak") for checking historical sold prices/volumes.
- LinkedIn — used to find and build relationships with platform category managers and category suppliers.
- Outscraper — data scraping tool suggested for building bulk-contact lists of niche local businesses (e.g., screen printers) to cold-text/call for wholesale sales.
- Named liquidation sources referenced as real examples: Costco (via B-Stock), Signature Hardware/Ferguson, Grainger, Kohler, Alphabroder.
- Shannon Jean's $5/month mastermind (shannonjean.com/mastermind) and his podcast, Business Brain Show (businessbrain.show).

## Caveats / risks
- Not passive income — Shannon explicitly warns "it's hard... it's difficult but very rewarding," requiring consistent, ongoing work (sourcing, calling buyers, learning categories); no guaranteed/automatic profit.
- Manifest/spreadsheet due diligence matters — Shannon's own $28,000 mistake (buying mostly plus-size apparel because collapsed spreadsheet columns hid size breakdown) shows real risk of misreading lot manifests before bidding; he got lucky that plus-size sports apparel demand bailed him out.
- Big/obscure industrial liquidation lots (e.g., the $13,000 industrial equipment lot) are explicitly NOT recommended as a first deal — finding buyers for large, obscure industrial items is a lot of manual outreach/calling work, and most people won't want to engage; better attempted only after building experience and momentum with smaller flips.
- Consumer electronics resale is described as largely played out/low-margin now compared to a decade ago — a caution against choosing that category today.
- Retail "store price" / MSRP on liquidation manifests is called "a lie" (except Costco, which is comparatively more reliable) — buyers must independently discount and verify real resale value rather than trusting the listed value.
- The category itself is described as secondary to system/process discipline (a 70/30 split); jumping categories without a repeatable process (math-based bidding, research, fulfillment/logistics) is implied to be the more common failure mode.
