# Oops! I Started 15 More Businesses For You To Copy (Part 4/4)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vUGbZMP9Gk0 | Published: 2024-11-07

Chris Koerner rapid-fires through businesses #58-72 of the ~75 he's started/co-founded. Part 4 of a 4-part series ("Every Business I've Ever Started").

## Co-founders (holding co / incubator hybrid, #58)
- Holding company Koerner co-owns with best friend/business partner Nick.
- Model: fund the idea + take majority equity, coach a talented entrepreneur to run it (not a pure incubator like Y Combinator, not a pure hold-co).
- Ran a cohort of 3 college students, trained via Zoom 2-3x/week for 60 days.
- Spent $65,000 just on the "co-founders.com" domain name.
- Caveat: extremely time-consuming to run well; currently paused in favor of other ventures (vending machines, pet cremation, etc.).

## Copy Paste Site (web design agency, #59)
- Idea: web design agency whose pitch is "show us any website you like, we'll copy it" — check the site's tech stack via BuiltWith (Shopify/Webflow/WordPress) and rebuild it exactly.
- Rationale: most small business owners aren't design-oriented and just want something that already looks good.
- Execution: outsource the actual build to a virtual assistant (sourced via Upwork), keep a cut/margin as the middleman.
- Lead gen: post as a web design job on Upwork to find VA talent; hundreds of web design jobs get posted daily by business owners — you can be the middleman between requesters and fulfillers.
- Result: got some leads, never chased hard, business still live as a domain but unmonetized. Koerner explicitly says "someone could still run with this."

## Fulfillment Score (shipping-rate lead magnet / 3PL play, #60)
- A calculator/landing page (fulfillmentscore.com) asking e-commerce brands: how many orders/month, avg shipping cost/order, avg weight, avg days to arrive, carrier used, email.
- Pitch: "are your shipping carriers (USPS/UPS/DHL) ripping you off?" — most e-commerce brands overpay for shipping.
- Two monetization angles: (1) loan out your own negotiated FedEx/UPS account rates to smaller e-commerce brands at a markup, capturing margin per package shipped; (2) use it as a lead magnet to sell 3PL fulfillment outsourcing to Shopify stores.
- Built in 1-2 hours; never heavily pursued but still considered a good idea.

## Josh Prout Scam site (not a business — cautionary tale, #61)
- A hosting/data-center scammer took a $50,000 deposit for crypto mining hosting in San Antonio (during Mining Syndicate days) that didn't exist (facility was a fabricated pitch deck copied from LoopNet).
- Koerner tracked him down personally (bought a motorcycle tracker), got the FBI involved after the scammer hit another victim for $400k, recovered $35,000 of the $50,000.
- Bought "joshprout.com" and "joshproutscam.com" to own the SEO on the scammer's name as retaliation/warning to others — not intended to be monetized.
- Lesson/caveat: sizable "surface area for getting scammed" comes from moving fast and hiring/dealing with many unknown people; do diligence on hosting/facility deals before wiring deposits.

## Nomo Snore (anti-snoring device, #62)
- Physical product: a neck-brace-style contraption to reduce snoring by keeping the airway open, invented/tested (n=1) by his friend Grant.
- Planned channel: Facebook ads, direct-to-consumer.
- Margin described as "stupidly high."
- Never launched fully (some Shopify setup started with college student partners); idea still liked by Koerner despite never getting off the ground.

## Off Marketers (off-market asset sourcing agency, #63)
- Agency model: charge clients (real estate investors/brokerages) $1,000-$5,000/month to cold-call and find off-market deals (single-family homes, mobile home parks, RV parks, businesses for sale).
- Value prop: a $3,000 investment in sourcing could save $300,000 vs. buying on-market.
- Found real customers and had early success, but lost momentum after ~month 2 (team got distracted, service quality dropped) and refunded customers rather than fix it.
- Caveat: needs sustained, dedicated cold-calling effort — folds quickly if under-resourced/distracted.

## AI Guy (AI newsletter, #64)
- Newsletter started under the co-founders program by a partner named Zach; covered AI content/news.
- Hosted on Beehiiv; got cost-per-acquisition down to ~$0.80 per email subscriber via Facebook ads.
- ~6,000 subscribers, dormant (not emailed in over a year) at time of recording; Koerner offered to sell it (open to any price) — email chris@cofounders.com.
- Monetization estimate for a reactivated 5x/week newsletter: ~$2,000-$3,000/month ad revenue (~$0.50/subscriber/month), i.e., roughly $15,000-$25,000/year profit potential.
- Ongoing cost: Beehiiv hosting bill (~$600-$800/month mentioned, unclear exact figure) paid while dormant.

## Startup Gems (AI-voice-cloned podcast experiment, #65)
- Experiment (not intended as a real business) after ElevenLabs launched AI voice cloning: cloned Koerner's own voice, used AI to write scripts about entrepreneurs, produced 5-15 minute daily episodes.
- Hosted on Buzzsprout; posted to Reddit/Twitter (200-300k views each), got 1,000-3,000 downloads on day one.
- Ran ~80+ daily episodes, then declined; explicit test of "can a podcast grow organically with zero promotion" — answer: no, it shrank without active promotion.
- Later relaunched as a real human podcast, renamed "Kerner's Corner" then "The Koerner Office" after episode ~8-9.

## The Nobs Crypto Podcast (#66)
- His first-ever podcast, from his "McAfee days" (Twitter/crypto persona era); tied to a crypto community with ~60,000 members (~1/3 from Vietnam/Nigeria) that ran an airdrop and whitepaper.
- Co-hosted with friends Grant and Max; flamed out after ~3 episodes.
- First episode got 3,000 downloads — noted in hindsight as actually a strong number for a new podcast.

## Twice Used (recycled iPhone glass products, #67)
- From his LCD Cycle business (broken iPhone screen repair/recycling): irrecoverable broken screens (with dead pixels) were unusable for repair, so they tried upcycling the broken glass into clocks, coasters, and a coffee table.
- Ran a Kickstarter; got press from AOL and CNET via cold-email outreach/PR, but raised under $8,000.
- Lesson stated explicitly: strong PR/attention without meaningful Kickstarter dollars raised = no real product-market fit, don't keep chasing it.

## Fire and Embers (restaurant acquisition attempt, #68)
- LLC formed with friend Nathan to buy "Big Ray BBQ" in Allen, TX — a $650,000 business.
- Deal died because the landlord was viewed as difficult/untrustworthy; feared getting "shafted" post-lease-signing.
- Framed as a passion project ("no one buys a restaurant because they're psychologically sound").

## Pro In Your Pocket (services marketplace, advisory role, #69)
- A Thumbtack/Angie's List competitor Koerner advised on (not founder); his brother-in-law Justin was the developer.
- Got some early traction but never reached a stage where marketing (Koerner's intended role) mattered — product development was "too hard."

## Lawyers in China (lead-gen for international lawyers, #70)
- After being scammed by a Chinese vendor for ~$200,000 of hardware, hired a China-based lawyer (~$450/hour) via a translator friend.
- Structured a rev-share deal: lawyer agreed to pay 30% of everything billed to referred clients, forever.
- Built a Wix site ("Chinese lawsuit"-type domain) running Google Ads targeting Americans searching "international lawyer," "Chinese lawyer," "lawyer in China" — a lead-gen-only play (US-China business disputes are common).
- Was scaling ad spend successfully until the lawyer relocated his family to Canada and stopped taking the business, ending the arrangement.
- Explicitly generalizable: this lead-gen model "doesn't have to be China — can be between any two countries."

## My Modern South (regional IKEA delivery/markup service, #71)
- Modeled on a real business he read about: person in a small city far from any IKEA (Huntsville, AL — 3 hrs from IKEA Memphis or Atlanta) takes customer furniture orders, places them at IKEA, drives a U-Haul/box truck to pick up, delivers/meets buyers in a parking lot, and charges the order cost plus a ~30% markup.
- Koerner set up an ugly website and posted in Facebook groups/Craigslist, got interested customers, but never fulfilled an actual order before pivoting away.
- Applies the general resell/logistics-arbitrage pattern (compared to his "Texas snacks" business).

## Animal Aftercare (programmatic SEO lead-gen, #72)
- Bought the domain for $10 ("animal-...cremation"-type site).
- Programmatic SEO stack: Tag Parrot, Webflow, Zapier, Whalesync, Airtable, Google Sheets tied together to auto-generate landing pages combining keyword x geography.
- Formula: 5 keywords (dog cremation, pet cremation, horse euthanasia, dog euthanasia, pet euthanasia) x top 1,000 US cities + top 100 Canadian cities (Canada sized at ~10% of US) = 5,500 landing pages, each with a lead-capture form.
- Rationale: in small/niche local markets there's no real competitor (no one opens a dedicated horse cremation facility for a small town) but people still search — programmatic pages capture that long-tail organic traffic.
- Monetization: sell the leads to vet clinics, especially large chains (noted that Mars/Mars Candy owns a large share of US vet clinics).
- Status at recording: generating multiple leads/day organically with zero ad spend; also building similar programmatic SEO sites for friends/family as a side service.

## General caveats/patterns across this episode
- Many of these were low-effort tests (a landing page, a form, a domain) meant to gauge demand before committing — several got real leads/interest but were abandoned due to lack of follow-through/attention, not lack of viability.
- Recurring theme: middleman/arbitrage models (Upwork job arbitrage, shipping rate arbitrage, off-market deal sourcing, IKEA delivery markup, lead-gen-to-professional referral) work well when someone actually stays focused on execution.
