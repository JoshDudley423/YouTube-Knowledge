# They Banned My $35K/Month Page So I Started a Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qv-dw57pFNA | Published: 2026-03-23

Live, real-time screen-recorded launch by Chris Koerner of an info-product business ("Plans of Grandeur") built entirely from his existing Google Drive archive of unpublished content/spreadsheets/docs, sold via his newsletter after his ~$35K/month Facebook page was banned. Demonstrates a repeatable playbook: repackage existing knowledge/assets into a paid product and sell it to an owned email list using rapid A/B price testing.

## The idea
- Sell a "vault"/bundle of previously-unpublished content (in this case: training videos, guides, docs, and tracking spreadsheets from years of $10,000/month 1-on-1 creator consulting) as a one-time-purchase digital product to an existing email list — a fast way to generate revenue from an "unfair advantage" you already have (an audience, expertise, or unused content archive) rather than building something new from scratch.
- General principle demonstrated (transferable beyond this specific product): launch minimum-viable using only a payment link and an email, and use live A/B testing of price and email format/length on small newsletter segments to find the winning offer before rolling it out to the full list.

## Numbers
- Time from idea (a dog walk) to first paying customer: under 2 hours (business "launched," per Koerner's framing, in under an hour — from 11:38 AM to first Stripe payment at 12:41 PM).
- Initial newsletter list size used for testing: ~9,600-9,961 active subscribers (some emails no longer valid).
- Price test 1 (small samples, <10% of list each): $47 vs. $97, identical email/subject/copy otherwise.
  - Early click-through: $47 email ~3.7% CTR (76 clicks of ~2,056 opens... roughly), $97 email lower CTR but higher conversion-per-click.
  - Landing-page conversion (post-click): $47 → 16/76 ≈ 21%; $97 → 6/62 ≈ 10%.
  - True email-to-purchase conversion (based on unique opens): $47 → ~8% conversion; $97 → ~29% conversion (note: video's numbers show some internal inconsistency/rounding, but the takeaway stated is that the higher price converted much better per opener).
  - Same-day revenue after both test emails: $1,334 in ~1 hour 20 minutes (~$1,000/hour from one email) — 6 sales at $97 + 16 sales at $47 shortly after send.
  - After a few more hours: 35 total customers (8 at $97, 27 at $47) — worked out to ~$0.27/subscriber on the $47 offer vs. ~$0.08/subscriber on the $97 offer (~3.5x more revenue per subscriber from the cheaper price in that window).
  - After ~1 week: 43 $47 sales ($2,21x total) vs. 22 $97 sales ($2,134 total) — roughly comparable gross revenue between the two prices, but ~5% more revenue per buyer on the $97 tier; higher-priced sales skewed later in the week (people took longer — a day to 5 days — to commit to the pricier, more considered purchase, while the cheaper price triggered more impulse buys).
  - Decision: Koerner ultimately chose to sell at $97 going forward, reasoning that a pricier product yields a smaller but more serious buyer base with fewer support/refund issues, even though it converts slightly less impulsively.
- A/B test 2: showing the price in the email vs. hiding it (must click through to see it) — showing the price won decisively: 23 sales (from 375 views, ~3% CVR... actually stated as the higher performer) vs. 11 sales (from 287 views) when price was hidden — visible price roughly doubled the conversion rate. Lesson stated explicitly: always show price in the email.
- A/B test 3: linking to a Stripe payment page vs. a Skool community page for checkout — Stripe won: 13 sales vs. 10 sales, attributed to Skool's extra signup friction (must create a Skool account before paying) outweighing its cleaner/more "legitimate" presentation.
- Cumulative revenue from all A/B test sends (before the full-list announcement): ~$11,000 (Skool $970 + Stripe ~$10,22x on 126 payments, mostly at $97, some at $47) — called "basically pure profit" since it was pre-existing content.
- Full-list launch email (sent to remaining ~3/4 of the list not already covered by tests): 125 sales in the first 1 hour 20 minutes at $97 each = $12,125; using Claude to forecast based on sales velocity, projected 200+ sales by end of day and 250-300 total over 5 days (~$24,000-29,000 gross from that one email).
- Total revenue disclosed by end of video: $30,000-$40,000 in net profit generated over the course of the video/launch, with ongoing "a few thousand per month" expected passively afterward.
- Cost structure: essentially zero marginal cost (content already existed); only cost cited was Stripe's ~3% transaction fee.
- Committed to donate 10% of all revenue (not profit) publicly to the National Kidney Foundation — documented via a Loom video sent to purchasers as proof.
- Domain name registered for the product on Namecheap: $11.28/year.

## Process / how to do it
1. **Identify your "unfair advantage."** Everyone has one (a job history, a niche insight, an audience, an unused content archive) — leverage whatever you already have rather than starting from zero. Koerner's specific asset: years of unpublished 1-on-1 creator-consulting materials (previously sold at $10,000/month for weekly calls) sitting unused in Google Drive.
2. **Build the buyer list from an existing newsletter/audience** (Beehiiv used here). Export/segment the full active-subscriber list, then randomize it (Google Sheets `=RAND()` + sort) before splitting into small test samples (roughly <10% of the list per price variant) so the sample isn't biased toward older/most-loyal subscribers.
3. **Set up products/payment links in Stripe** for each price variant being tested (e.g., $47 one-time vs. $97 one-time). Mark items as "lifetime"/one-time explicitly in the copy so buyers know it isn't a recurring subscription. Minimize checkout friction deliberately — don't collect name/phone/extra fields unless the data is worth the conversion hit; every additional required field or step lowers conversion.
4. **Get a domain name and product name fast, without overthinking branding.** Speed/momentum matters more than optimizing the name — "friction, time, and lack of momentum kill deals." (Product named "Plans of Grandeur," from a personal story: a psychiatrist once told him he had "delusions of grandeur," which became his branding hook — an example of turning a personal narrative into product storytelling.)
5. **Tag and segment subscribers in the email platform** (Beehiiv tags + dynamic segments) so different price-tier emails only go to their intended sub-list, and excluded lists prevent duplicate sends.
6. **Write short, low-friction emails**: no header/banner images at the top (extra scroll = friction), put the first-name personalization token without a leading comma (so it still reads fine for subscribers missing a first name), and — critically — always disclose the price directly in the email body rather than hiding it behind a click (tested and shown to roughly double conversion).
7. **Run the price test simultaneously** (send both price-variant emails within minutes of each other) to avoid time-based confounds; keep everything else in the email (subject line, wording, structure) identical so price is the only variable.
8. **Iterate serially**: after price, test email length (shorter, price near the top perform better broadly, though a full narrative email that builds trust/backstory also converted well here) and checkout destination (direct Stripe link vs. a community platform landing page).
9. **Deliver via the platform that's actually being sold** — after testing, content was migrated from a raw Google Drive folder into a Skool community (run by a business partner) for a cleaner, more "justifiable" $97 purchase experience, with a Claude-generated logo and a Loom welcome video; buyers were auto-invited to Skool after paying via Stripe (automation already built), which let Stripe remain the primary checkout link even after the delivery platform changed.
10. **Use an LLM (Claude) as a forecasting/decision aid**: fed it screenshots of sales velocity and email performance to project total expected sales (Claude's early-hour extrapolation model: ~40-50% of eventual total sales occur in the first hour) and to sanity-check pricing/channel decisions (e.g., whether to declare Skool or Stripe the "winning" checkout link).
11. **Price fairness commitment**: promised to make things fair to earlier/higher-paying buyers if the price later changed (e.g., partial refunds to $97 buyers if the price dropped to $47) — an explicit tactic to preserve trust in the newsletter relationship.
12. **Plan for post-launch monetization**: raise price after the pre-sale window closes, send a short follow-up "stragglers" email in the days after launch, and fold the offer into a standing automated email flow/welcome sequence for ongoing (smaller, "a few thousand per month") passive revenue.

## Tools/platforms/resources named
- Beehiiv — newsletter platform; used for subscriber export/import, tagging, dynamic segments, and sending all campaign emails.
- Stripe — payment processing; used for product creation, payment links, and per-link analytics/A-B tracking.
- Google Sheets — used to randomize and split the subscriber list into test segments (via `=RAND()` and sort).
- Namecheap — domain registrar used to buy the product's domain name (~$11.28/year); explicitly recommended over GoDaddy.
- Skool — community/course-hosting platform used to deliver the final paid content in a more polished, justifiable format than a raw Drive folder link.
- Claude — used both to generate a placeholder logo and as an analytical tool to forecast total expected sales from partial-day sales velocity data.
- Google Drive — original home of the raw content sold (videos, docs, spreadsheets from past consulting work).
- National Kidney Foundation — charity receiving a public 10%-of-revenue donation, used as both a genuine cause and a trust/marketing signal in the offer emails.

## Caveats / risks
- This launch leaned entirely on an existing, warm, large-ish email list (~9,600-10,000 active subscribers) built over years — Koerner explicitly pre-empts the "easy for him, he has a big list" objection by citing a Beehiiv founder example making ~$500,000/year off a smaller list with just one email/week, and claims even 2,000 subscribers can generate tens of thousands of dollars a year, but this is asserted, not demonstrated in this video.
- A/B test sample sizes were small (dozens of transactions per test) and results showed some contradictory signals (e.g., early data suggested $47 was the better price by revenue-per-subscriber, but a full week's data showed $97 modestly outperforming per-buyer and was ultimately chosen) — the video itself flags that these percentages "might not hold" over longer time horizons.
- Skool vs. Stripe test result (Stripe won by 30%) is presented as somewhat counterintuitive even to Koerner, and he explicitly ran it by Claude for a sanity check before committing to a decision, cautioning against declaring a clear "winner" from a small sample.
- The originating trigger for this business (a sudden Facebook page ban costing "hundreds of thousands of dollars a year" in income, alleged copyright infringement he says was false) is presented as unresolved — he never got the banned account reinstated, illustrating real platform-dependency risk for anyone building an audience/business primarily on a single social platform.
- High-ticket info-product/course selling is acknowledged to have "a bad rap" in general due to sellers overpromising relative to delivered value — Koerner frames his approach as ethical only because the content itself is pre-existing, previously sold at high value ($10K/month), and priced far below what it previously commanded, not because high-ticket courses are risk-free.
