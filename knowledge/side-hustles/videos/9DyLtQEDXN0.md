# How a Regular Guy Started a 1-Person Business with AI
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9DyLtQEDXN0 | Published: 2026-06-01

Interview with Phil, a retiree (retired from other work June 2025) who built a one-person AI consulting/automation "agency" business from scratch with no coding or marketing background, using tools like Claude, Go High Level, and vibe-coding, landing his first paying clients entirely through his existing personal network.

## How he started
- Began building simple **voice agents in Go High Level** (a CRM/automation platform) because it was easy to build with no coding background. Pitched them first to friends, then cold-called roofing companies — described voice agents as "not really my use case" but useful as a low-stakes way to build credibility/proof of capability.
- First real deal came from a pitch to a personal friend who runs an investment/wealth management firm (manages ~$1.4 billion, 9 offices in the Nashville area). That friend didn't trust AI enough to know where to start, but after months of conversation, signed Phil to a **$50,000 six-month part-time consulting contract** (~$8,333/month, ~20-25 hours/week), initially to build a content-creation system and automate parts of his marketing/social media, with no prior background in marketing himself — Phil relied on asking AI (Claude) the right questions and leaning on the firm's own marketing staff for domain context.
- Between his first voice-agent experiments (~October) and closing that $50k deal (March), Phil methodically worked through his personal phone contacts (1,000+) and had 7-10 in-person sit-downs with business owners. Early meetings mostly failed to convert — he attributes this to talking too much about what AI could do rather than listening to the prospect's actual pain points (a "sell by listening" lesson).

## Client acquisition approach and sales technique
- Core principle repeated throughout: nearly all his business came from direct friends, family, or friends-of-friends — not cold outreach. States a "floodgate principle": once you solve one problem well for someone, they come back with more requests from adjacent parts of their life/business (rental properties, other ventures), turning one relationship into multiple engagements.
- Recommends explicitly asking for referrals after a successful build — something he admits he hadn't been doing himself until reflecting on it during the interview. Host reinforces: cold calling < friend-of-a-friend < a direct friend, in terms of conversion likelihood.
- Meeting technique: does not walk in with a rigid question list. Occasionally has Claude help generate starter questions if he doesn't know the industry, but his main goal in every meeting is simply to get the business owner talking about their pain points, since most owners "know they need AI" but don't know practically what to start with or how.
- Records every client conversation (previously via a Limitless pendant, similar to a Plaud device — noted as possibly being discontinued/absorbed by Meta at time of recording; suggests a smartphone voice-memo app as a free substitute), then feeds the transcript into Claude to help draft proposals, pricing, contracts, and technical solutions after the fact.
- Qualifying/pricing approach: for the investment-firm client, he asked the prospect how many hours/week they wanted him working, then quoted a price based on that; the client actually countered by proposing the $50,000 figure himself. For the chiropractor client, Phil built the entire pricing proposal (with Claude's help) around a fixed project fee and let the value proposition (taking a fully proven product launch off the client's plate) do the selling — described the resulting close as "not a hard sale at all."

## Client engagements built (concrete numbers)
1. **Investment firm** — $50,000 for a 6-month part-time (20-25 hrs/week) retainer covering content creation and marketing automation; built a system using a 22,000-chunk knowledge base drawn from the client's ~570 existing radio commercials/content to auto-generate on-brand content in the client's own voice.
2. **Nonprofit / ministry friend** — built "Grant Craft" for free (as a proof of concept): an app that finds relevant grants, researches each grant's specific application requirements, and drafts the application from information fed in about the ministry (a human still reviews before submission), using a Claude Code agent to automate the grant research/filling process. Pricing model set up for future use: $1,500 per completed grant application + $2,000/month retainer, but with a client-friendly structure — no billing until a grant is actually awarded, and total billing capped at 8% of the awarded grant amount (a commission-based, low-risk-to-client structure).
3. **Chiropractor** (same nonprofit friend, expanded relationship) — launched and managed marketing for a new FDA-approved body-toning laser treatment. Pricing: **$3,500 upfront setup + $1,500/month for 6 months** (renegotiate after), plus **$75 per new client beyond the first 10/month**, with the client separately covering CRM costs (via Go High Level) and contributing $500/month toward paid ad spend. Deliverables: a content-creation app for the specific laser device, an automated Go High Level chatbot deployed to the chiropractor's existing 7,500-contact client base to field questions, a signup/launch form, and ongoing monthly social media management. Actual time cost after setup: ~1.5 hours/month of ongoing work for $1,500/month. Setup time for the $3,500 build: ~12 hours, despite Phil having zero prior coding background and having "never built" several of the specific pieces before — built almost entirely through conversing with Claude (didn't know what an "MD file"/CLAUDE.md was until the month before recording).
- This chiropractor relationship subsequently expanded into additional requests: promoting other in-practice laser services, and building a functioning CRM/client-reactivation system (most small businesses, Phil notes, similarly lack a functioning CRM).

## Framing / mental models discussed
- Host's "sign in a growing town" analogy: as more local businesses adopt basic AI-driven content presence, having *some* consistent content output becomes table stakes ("you need a sign") rather than a competitive edge or a promise of virality — the actual bar for local business content in 2026+ is just "publish something regularly," not achieving high production value or going viral.
- Both host and guest explicitly reject the idea that this approach can scale indefinitely on friends/family alone — Phil acknowledges he hasn't yet settled on a specific productized niche/service tier (considered building tiered offers, e.g., free/$250/$1,000, as a lower-friction lead-in) but the host notes that having actual revenue already is more valuable than having a clean niche this early.
- Discussed the general escalation pattern for solo AI-agency operators: solve one business owner's specific problem once, then realize the same solution is reusable/sellable to many similar businesses in the same vertical (e.g., building a tool for one dairy farmer, then selling access to every dairy farmer in a region) — a path from bespoke consulting/agency work toward a scalable software product.
- Host's own related anecdote: two 18-year-olds making ~$7,000/month buying used VCRs on Facebook Marketplace for ~$10 and reselling on eBay for ~$100 (no one manufactures VCRs anymore, but nostalgia demand from millennials persists) — cited as an example of a supply-gone/demand-persists arbitrage opportunity spotted via casual conversation. Also mentions personally testing DoorDash driving and earning ~$30/hour with zero experience/reviews, framed as evidence of abundant low-barrier income opportunity right now, while acknowledging this can feel dismissive to people who are "grinding" without results yet.
- On self-doubt/imposter syndrome: both agree the core obstacle isn't technical skill but discipline and willingness to have the conversations — Phil notes that even without deep AI expertise, he still knows "significantly more" than 99% of the small business owners he meets, which is enough to be valuable; recommends being as encouraging to yourself as you would be to a struggling child rather than self-critical.
- Explicit anti-pattern named by the host from his own past: avoiding friends/family as a client base out of pride/embarrassment about looking unstable while trying many ventures — framed as a self-imposed, unnecessary barrier; recommends reframing outreach to friends/family as "helping them make or save money," not "selling," which removes the social awkwardness.

## Tools/platforms named
Claude (core tool for coding/vibe-coding, proposal and contract drafting, pricing strategy, and content strategy conversations), Claude Code (used to build and run an agent that automated grant research/filling), Go High Level (CRM/automation platform used for voice agents, chatbots, and client CRM work), Limitless pendant and Plaud (personal audio-recording devices for capturing client conversations verbatim), Replit (used for an unrelated vibe-coded livestock-management app for a family member, mentioned as an example of quick personalized app-building), Beehiiv with its new MCP integration to Claude/ChatGPT/Gemini (lets an AI tool query real newsletter analytics — open rates, subject line performance — to plan content strategy; mentioned as a sponsor segment, not part of Phil's own build).

## Caveats
- Phil explicitly states you cannot scale a business indefinitely on friends and family alone — eventually you must branch out to a repeatable acquisition channel or productized offer.
- The chiropractor and nonprofit deals both originated from one single existing personal friendship, illustrating how concentrated/lucky early client acquisition can be rather than a proven repeatable funnel.
- No independent verification of these revenue figures beyond the guest's own account in the interview.
