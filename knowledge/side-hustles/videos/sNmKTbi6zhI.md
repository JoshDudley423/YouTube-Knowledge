# what I wish I knew at age 20
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sNmKTbi6zhI | Published: 2025-05-06

Solo mindset/principles episode (no specific side-hustle idea or product) using stories/analogies to teach general business lessons. Notes below capture the actionable principles, since this video's substance is mindset guidance rather than a business model.

- Foundation-over-appearance principle (the Vasa warship analogy): the Vasa, a 1600s Swedish warship, sank on its maiden voyage (half a mile out, calm conditions) because it was built top-heavy with insufficient ballast, prioritizing an impressive appearance (64 cannons, open gun ports to show them off) over structural stability. Business parallel: things that look impressive but lack real substance underneath will eventually fail -- cited examples: Nikola Motors' faked "driving" truck demo video (truck was actually just rolling downhill) that led to fraud charges; resumes full of buzzwords with no evidence of actual value delivered; startups with many employees or large raised funding but no revenue. Core prescription: sales is the foundation -- "you start with sales and you end with sales," build the substance (revenue-generating activity) before/alongside the appearance.
- "The harder way is usually the better way" (the two-basketball story): a basketball won at a carnival game for $45 (effortful, costly) was treated with care (kept indoors, cleaned) by one son; a basketball fished for free out of a pond was neglected/lost by the other son within weeks. Business application: growth hacks and shortcuts that feel "too easy"/like a cheat code tend to be short-lived -- "the efficacy of a growth hack is directly correlated to its lifespan." Cites that manually hand-typed cold emails reportedly outperform automated/templated cold email blasts (attributed partly to deliverability/spam-filtering effects), reinforcing that unscalable, high-effort tactics (cold calls, door-knocking, individually written outreach) tend to have more durable payoff than fully automated shortcuts.
- Finding hidden drag / testing small things (the treadmill analogy): a treadmill had a manual incline lever accidentally left on for 6-8 months, meaning all training on it was harder than it appeared, until discovered via checking the manual. Business application: look for small, undetected "hidden drag" hurting performance -- e.g., a slow-loading website (a few hundred milliseconds of load time can measurably affect e-commerce conversion rates), or a toxic team member setting a company's cultural floor ("culture is often the temperament of your most toxic employee, everything falls to that lowest common denominator"). Counterbalancing principle: don't over-optimize either -- example given of a Facebook ad campaign stable at $1/conversion that gets progressively "improved" (new markets, translated copy, changed pixel/button colors) until it degrades to $130/conversion and can't be restored to the original baseline; and the opposite failure mode of finding something that works (his newsletter subject line format, unchanged and untested for over a year despite subscriber base growing ~100x) and never revisiting it to see if further gains are available. Recommends deliberately sitting between "analysis paralysis / over-optimizing to death" and "done is better than perfect."
- Power-law / "find your whale" principle: cites that a single ~150-location enterprise customer would take roughly the same sales effort as (or less than) their existing single-location customers, but would 10x the business (Voltera, their medical billing software business for skilled nursing facilities) with little added overhead -- illustrates chasing outsized, disproportionate wins rather than only optimizing average-case effort. Also cites: 600 short-form videos produced in a year, with ~90% of new followers coming from just ~2% of those videos -- restates the 80/20 rule as closer to a "96/4 rule" (apply 80/20 to itself) and advises focusing effort on identifying and doubling down on that top few-percent outlier content/channel rather than treating all output as equally valuable.
- Focus/attention principle ("you ride where you look," from a mountain biking trail analogy): looking directly at an obstacle (a tree) while riding increases the odds of hitting it; keeping eyes on the clear trail/path keeps you on course. Applied to mindset: sustained focus on negative outcomes tends to manifest more of them (confirmation bias / self-fulfilling attention), while deliberately noticing and "overemphasizing" small positive coincidences/blessings tends to surface more of those -- framed pragmatically as useful regardless of whether it's "real" causation or just attention/confirmation bias.
- Friction and subscriber quality tradeoff: reducing friction to collect email addresses increases opt-in/conversion rate but also increases unsubscribe rate and lowers average subscriber loyalty/engagement -- there's a real tradeoff spectrum between ease-of-acquisition and quality/durability of that relationship; recommends finding a "happy medium" rather than either extreme (zero friction, or excessive gatekeeping).
- Closing anecdote/ethic (temple carpet story, more life-philosophy than tactical): witnessing volunteers quietly and anonymously clean up a large mess (muddy footprints on temple carpet) without ever telling the person responsible, taking hours of unrecognized effort. Lesson offered: "be the person who scrubs, not the person who complains," and recognize that most kindness/support you receive in business and life happens without your ever finding out about it -- gratitude is presented as directly correlated with happiness.

Caveats/risks:
- This episode is explicitly a mindset/analogy-driven solo episode, not a specific business model, niche, or step-by-step tactic -- there is no concrete side-hustle idea, pricing, or numbers to extract here, unlike most other episodes in this batch.
- The claim that manual cold emails outperform automated ones is asserted without data/citation ("I don't know why, maybe it's deliverability or MX records") -- treat as anecdotal.
- The newsletter subject-line A/B test finding (boring/generic subject line beating a "clever" one) was last tested over a year prior to this recording at a much smaller subscriber base (~1,500 vs. ~150,000 at time of recording) -- the host explicitly flags this result may no longer hold and hasn't been re-tested.
