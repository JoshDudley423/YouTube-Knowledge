# How Much Do Shaan Puri and Sam Parr Make (My First Million)?
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h0NqzKitEII | Published: 2024-05-02

Chris Koerner and business partner Nik Huska (Co-Founders) riff on several business ideas/analyses.

## Pet crematorium ("Sunbeam Paws", Dallas)
- Idea rationale: pet ownership boomed post-pandemic, meaning more pet deaths and demand for cremation services.
- Startup approach: rather than buying a crematorium outright (~$100,000 minimum), they outsource cremation to an existing competitor ~45 minutes away.
- Trade-off: outsourcing yields ~80-85% gross margins from day one vs. ~93% if they owned the burner themselves — but with far less capital and setup time.
- Equity split: Chris + Nik 15%, partner Steven 15%, an advising veterinarian (also their first customer) 5%, and the operator running day-to-day 65%.
- They financed a $115,000 refrigerated van via a personal loan (credit union rate 7.75%) rather than routing it through the business — deliberate, since Chris judged he could earn more than 7.75% deploying his own cash elsewhere, and wanted personal skin in the game.
- Launch-day projection: ~$20K MRR, driven substantially by the 5%-equity veterinarian being the first paying customer, plus 2-3 more lined up through the operator's existing vet-clinic connections (operator previously worked rolling up veterinary clinics in DFW).
- Timeline: from initial idea (looking at ABA/autism clinics with the same partner, pivoting to pet cremation) to launch was about 8 months.

## Commercial kitchen/hood cleaning business investment (Tampa)
- Sourced via a "searcher" met on Twitter; Nik/Chris are taking a minority stake (under 10% equity).
- Business is commercial kitchen and appliance cleaning (hood cleaning is only one small component) — described as a stable, growing home/commercial-services niche.
- Rationale for getting involved: Chris/Nik's skills (cold outreach/cold calling to build customer base, acquisition-transition experience — opening bank accounts, migrating systems) add real value to a searcher-operator once the business is running.

## Podcast ad-revenue economics (using "My First Million" as the worked example)
- Business podcast CPM (cost per thousand downloads/listens) runs roughly $40-$80.
- Napkin math: 1-hour podcast, 3 ad spots, 1,000 downloads → ~$50/ad slot → $150 per 1,000 downloads across the episode.
- My First Million publicly reports ~1.2M downloads/month → back-of-envelope ad revenue >$200K/month if fully selling that inventory as ads.
- Reframe: advertisers need 3-10x ROAS; if a company like Shopify buys out all of MFM's ad space for ~$200K and gets 5x return, that's ~$1M of value extracted — which is why influencers (e.g., Cody Sanchez) increasingly launch and sell their own products via their own podcast/audience instead of just selling ad slots to others ("why sell ads when you can have your own Shopify").
- Radio vs. podcast reach: cited stat — 41% of Americans listen to a podcast monthly vs. 82% who listen to radio monthly, meaning podcasting is still a smaller-audience channel overall despite hype, though share is shifting from radio to podcast.
- Local radio ad test result: bought a "prime hour" ad slot (9-10am ET / 7-8am local) on a small Tampa station; used a trackable phone number as a call-to-action and got zero calls, versus a viral tweet with 600K views generating only 3 form submissions from a linked Typeform — illustrates that low direct-response conversion doesn't mean an ad had no reach, just that most viewers don't act on a CTA.
- Aside: stations without Nielsen ratings (~$10K/month to subscribe to) can't verify real listenership; back-of-envelope estimate for a market of ~2M residents with ~40-50 competing podcasts on air suggests only ~10-40K potential listeners per station at any given time.

## Mr. Beast e-commerce revenue estimate (Feastables chocolate + Mr Beast merch store)
- Method demonstrated for estimating a Shopify store's sales without insider data: place an order, read the sequential order number, then use the Wayback Machine (web.archive.org) to find when the store first went live; (order_number / days_open) gives an average daily order rate.
- Feastables (chocolate): ~420,000+ orders in just over 2 years; at an assumed $30 AOV, ~$13M direct-to-consumer revenue over that period (~$1M+/month) — excludes Walmart/retail distribution sales, which a source close to Walmart procurement implied make DTC just a small fraction (~5%) of total sales, putting total Feastables sales potentially above $100M.
- New Mr Beast merch store (Shopify): 770,000 orders in its first 5 months; at ~$39 AOV, ~$39M in revenue in 5 months (~$8M/month) — merch outperformed chocolate by a wide margin, contrary to their initial guess.
- Margin discussion: e-commerce/DTC food (chocolate) shipped nationally has thin net margins (estimated 5-12%) once shipping costs are factored in, even though wholesale chocolate margins are typically high — the stated strategic rationale for a low/no-margin online chocolate business is brand awareness, not near-term profit.
- YouTube ad-revenue CPM comparison: a "high-value" niche audience (e.g., business content, per Cody Sanchez estimate) can command $7-10 CPM; Mark Rober (STEM/education, per his own disclosure on a podcast) gets about $2.13 CPM; Mr Beast's audience, being mostly international and young, is estimated at a much lower ~$1.50-2 CPM — meaning views alone are a poor proxy for monetizable audience value; audience demographics (US-based, older, higher income) drive CPM far more than raw view count.

## Roll-up business model (explained generally, with SaaS example)
- Roll-ups create value two ways: (1) economies of scale — combining multiple small operators (e.g., dental practices) lets you hire specialized staff instead of one generalist per location, cutting administrative overhead; (2) "multiple arbitrage" — smaller businesses sell at lower EBITDA multiples than larger ones, so combining them into a bigger entity unlocks a higher resale multiple.
- Stated multiple bands by EBITDA size: under $1M EBITDA sells around 3-5x; $1-2.5M EBITDA around 4-7x; $2.5-5M EBITDA around 10x.
- Worked example: buy five $1M-EBITDA companies at 5x ($25M total), combine into one $5M-EBITDA entity, sell upstream at 10x ($50M) — a 2x return purely from the multiple change, independent of any operational improvement.
- Real example cited: "Dev" (via acquire.com) rolls up small educational SaaS companies targeting college students, criteria under $110K/year ARR; some acquired businesses went from ~$2,000/month net income standalone to ~$7,500/month after being rolled up, via cross-selling to a shared customer profile and leveraging his university network — a cash-flow-focused roll-up strategy rather than a venture-scale outcome.
- Koerner/Huska's own RV park roll-up strategy: buying RV parks/campgrounds at a 10-11 cap rate, improving operations to a ~15 cap valuation, with the exit thesis of eventually selling a fully vertically-integrated portfolio to private equity at a lower (5-6) cap rate.
- General framing: "Home Services" and niche rollup verticals (gutters, dental, OBGYN/fertility clinics, marinas, storage sheds) are seen as fertile because there are hundreds of under-consolidated niches nobody has targeted yet.

## Misc business ideas raised (informal, untested)
- "Stadium experience" businesses: renting out stadiums during off-hours for novelty activities — e.g., an existing business charging ~$80/person to golf inside pro stadiums (reportedly selling out); floated variants include pop-up gourmet/farmers markets, giant traveling bounce-house events, Nerf/laser-tag/capture-the-flag games, inside stadiums.
- Framed as approachable: "it's probably just a phone call" to a stadium manager to ask about rental rates — the barrier is perceived difficulty, not actual difficulty.
- Caveat raised: liability/property-damage risk (e.g., golf balls damaging turf, injury liability for contact games) is a real concern stadium owners would need to weigh.
- General observed pattern: parents will reliably pay $10-20/kid for supervised, novel entertainment — a recurring demand niche cited via a small-scale example (a strip-mall storefront in Alabama charging $5/kid to use indoor bounce houses).

## Framing on career/business growth stages
- Discussion of "hardest phase of growth": $0-1M, $1-10M, or $10-100M in revenue. No consensus reached; Chris says $0-1M is hardest for him personally (~70 businesses started, i.e., extensive reps at that stage specifically); Nik argues $1-10M is where "everything breaks."
