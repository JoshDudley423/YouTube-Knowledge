# How Do You Grow an 8 Figure Destination Wedding Business?
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D42D-GqMwU4 | Published: 2024-04-09

Live call-in Q&A episode covering six different callers' businesses. Distilled by segment.

## Destination wedding planning business (family biz, Mexico/Caribbean)
- 30 years in business, no real marketing; plans 80-100 weddings/year (~7,000 guests), 80% of leads from YouTube channel, rest from referrals.
- Unit economics: ~60-70 guests/wedding, ~$1,000/person avg spend, ~20% margin per person to the planner; gross revenue per wedding $60K-$100K, translating to $8K-$12K revenue to the business per wedding. At 80-100 weddings/year that's roughly $700K-$1.2M/year business revenue (near the "million dollar threshold" that's hard to break through).
- 8 staff manage groups; each could handle 20-30% more volume, so realistic growth ceiling ~120-140 weddings/year without new hires.
- Email list: ~7,000 new emails/year from travelers; ~10-12K people are legally double-opted-in (last ~1-2 years), roughly double that (20-24K) are transactional-only (not opted in but have a relationship, so still fair game to email).
- **Advice given (segmentation + reactivation email strategy):**
  - Segment list into: opted-in vs. non-opted-in; bride vs. groom; by wedding year.
  - Use a Chrome extension like Gmass, or standalone tools like Mixmax or Lemlist (not MailChimp) — trickle campaign starting at 50 emails/day, A/B testing opted-in vs non-opted-in response rates.
  - Send **plain text only** emails (no graphics/design), from a personal-feeling sender (whoever historically communicated with that guest), highly personalized (their names, wedding location, couple's names).
  - Ask directly: "do you know anyone else getting married?" — target guests who were 25-33 at time of wedding, now prime age to know other engaged couples.
  - Offer generous referral incentives ($500 Amazon gift card, $1,000 Venmo, etc.) and test thresholds aggressively since ticket size is $8-12K; worst case is breaking even to learn the channel.
  - Short/no-frills subject lines outperform (fewer words = higher open rate); avoid fancy subject lines.
  - Use ChatGPT to generate location-specific facts/content for past destinations (e.g., "did you know most people who go to the Bahamas also do X") to make follow-up emails engaging.
  - Once a winning formula is found, automate into an evergreen drip campaign.
- Caveat: emailing non-double-opted-in guests is legally fine per advice given because of an existing "relationship" (they received transactional emails), but Koerner is not a lawyer — treat as informal guidance.

## Board game business ("Lost Lands of Ver" — fantasy board game)
- Story-based game combining elements of Monopoly/Catan/Risk; expandable via new cards/expansions (not a static, one-and-done game like chess).
- Raised $20K via pre-order crowdfunding through word of mouth only, no paid marketing; boxes shipped nationally (Toronto, Chicago, LA).
- Unit economics: sells for ~$100 (premium/deluxe metal-piece version), manufacturing cost $48.99 (~50% margin) at MOQ of 500 units. A planned plastic version would cost ~$24.99 to manufacture (would roughly double margin) — molds already made are reusable for plastic version.
- ~40 more unit sales (~4K) needed to reach breakeven on the first production run.
- Feedback universally positive; one buyer played 7 nights straight with different groups — strong word-of-mouth/viral loop because game requires 3-4 players so each buyer exposes multiple new people.
- **Growth options discussed:** Kickstarter (going beyond existing community to strangers), scaling via paid ads/small business loan (TikTok/Instagram/Facebook, board game conventions), or bringing in an investor/VC who can add distribution connections or open doors, in exchange for equity.
- **Recommended approach: seeding, not paid ads.**
  - Prioritize launching the cheaper plastic version specifically because it's a better "seeding" product (lower cost to give away) and is the growth engine since the game can't be played solo.
  - Scrape meetup.com for board game/gaming meetup groups; give the game for free to the group leader and beg them to actually use it (the hard part isn't giving it away, it's getting people to actually try it).
  - Also target Facebook groups.
  - Get it into local board game stores (already planned).
  - Sample size approach: get it in front of ~30 people, aim for 10 to actually play, then judge if it's a winning strategy before exhausting all meetup groups.
  - Define target customer persona beyond "board gamer" — suggested non-obvious segments: parents of large families, non-drinking/non-smoking communities (cited Utah/Mormon community and Muslim community as heavy board-game demographics) since they socialize via board games instead of drinking/nightlife.
  - Study successful long-lived (non-viral, non-COVID-bump) games like Ticket to Ride, Monopoly, Catan, Codenames — read founder interviews/articles; ignore one-hit-wonder viral products (e.g., Cards Against Humanity) or COVID-era spikes as non-repeatable patterns.
  - Avoid paid ads as primary channel — algorithm risk, cost is unpredictable/can spiral (host cites his own experience scaling Facebook ad spend to $5K/month with poor ROI clarity).

## RV park occupancy (68-pad park)
- Rents raised from $300/month to $600/month over ~4 years (still below 2 competing parks in area); property taxes rose ~50% last year forcing the increases; currently ~89% occupancy (getting close to full).
- Traffic sources: Craigslist, website, just started a Facebook profile (not yet using Facebook Marketplace).
- All units tenant-owned (no park-owned rental units).
- **Advice given:**
  - Improve communication to build goodwill and counter negative reviews: send a monthly newsletter (~1 hour/month of owner's time) that (1) addresses rent increases directly with market comparisons to other named local parks, (2) includes a "tenant spotlight" (photo + personal story) to build community goodwill, (3) distribute via an SMS list linking to a website blog post (paper newsletter also an option given tenant demographic skews non-tech-savvy).
  - Proactively solicit 5-star reviews from tenants the on-site manager identifies as happy; getting average review above 4.0 tends to naturally increase occupancy.
  - Tell tenants the future rent-increase schedule in advance (transparency reduces backlash).
  - Use Facebook Marketplace to advertise vacancies (unboosted first, boost only if that underperforms).
  - Google Ads called out as generally not useful for this ("I've never seen Google ads be useful" for a business like this); Facebook/Meta ads targeted at lower-income people in the area recommended as fallback if Marketplace boost doesn't work.
  - Last-resort lever if occupancy still lags: buy 3-4 nicer used RVs and seller-finance them to new tenants ("never once had an issue getting new tenants in if I deliver them a unit with a bow on it") — check local regulations first.

## Monument/headstone (gravestone) business acquisition
- Caller wants to approach the owner of a local monument business about buying it (personal connection: the business cared for his late sister-in-law).
- **Approach recommended:** Build relationship slowly rather than making a cold offer.
  - First visit: introduce yourself, lead with genuine compliments and personal connection story, no ask.
  - Follow up later (e.g., a week after) with a soft, humble inquiry: "would you ever be interested in selling?" Expect hesitancy/hedging rather than a firm yes or no — hedging is itself a signal the owner has considered it.
  - Talking point to use: cite the (approximate, unverified) stat that roughly 2/3 of small businesses never sell and just close, destroying real value; many owners don't realize their business (even a one-person operation) has sellable value.
  - Even if it doesn't lead to a purchase of that specific business, treat it as a relationship/mentorship opportunity that could lead to acquiring a different similar business.
  - Expect to offer some form of seller financing; the stronger the personal relationship built first, the more likely the owner agrees to that structure.
  - Caveat: business is likely to be resilient/growing due to demographics (aging population), which host says he'd factor in without needing much additional research.

## Digital signage management business for schools
- Business converts basic written communications from schools (e.g., a principal's email) into engaging video content, pushed via a CMS to on-site screens.
- 42 customers, only 1 non-renewal in year one; some customers expanded from 1 screen to 2+.
- Current acquisition channel: direct-mail ads to schools (only channel tried, plus early-stage attempts at building relationships with district-level decision makers, which the caller says is hard to cultivate). Planning first in-person event for 300-350 school leaders.
- **Advice given:** don't diversify into other verticals (e.g., factories/industrial) yet — schools are already one of the hardest customer types to acquire, so if it's working, scale what's working rather than chasing a new market.
  - Direct mail requires volume and repetition to hit a tipping point — host's own analogy: his old iPhone-parts business (LCD Cycle) needed 3-5 rounds of postcards with consistent branding/theme (different offers, same visual identity) before response rates picked up ("Pavlov dog" recognition effect); one especially effective offer was personal/quirky (e.g., an invite to go boating with the owner) rather than a generic discount.
  - Suggestion: try "chunky mailers" — include a small physical trinket (e.g., a pencil, something school-themed) to increase response/memorability.
  - Bottom line: if a channel already works with near-zero churn, don't spin wheels on new verticals — just scale mailer volume.

## Dumpster rental startup (Salt Lake City)
- 6 weeks old; founder flew to Texas, bought a truck + 11 dumpsters, drove them back to Utah. Idea originated from his house-flipping background (he used to rent dumpsters constantly and ran the numbers himself).
- Startup capital intensity: each dumpster costs ~$5,500-$6,000 fully outfitted (incl. paint/decals); rents for ~$350/week to homeowners; 12 more dumpsters arriving soon (would bring fleet to 23).
- Currently 90% of customer acquisition from Google Ads (~$200/day spend → ~1.5-2 rentals/day, roughly translating to a ~$700K/year revenue run rate target for the year); remainder from ~200-300 unproductive cold calls to roofers/remediation companies/home builders (most already have an existing dumpster vendor) plus early-stage networking with realtors/moving companies.
- Commercial contract example: one Park City client on a monthly contract pays $550/month for one empty + $200 per additional swap — same operational effort as a 3-day residential rental at $299, but far more profitable and provides more consistent (weather-independent) demand vs. weekend-driven homeowner demand (business is very lumpy: Wed-Fri busy, Mon-Tue often near 0% utilization).
- Considered but not yet pursued: partnering with junk-hauling companies (a mid-size junk removal operator said he'll upsell dumpster-seekers into a junk removal job instead since he doesn't own dumpsters — those operators work on thin margins so may be open to referral fees); targeting smaller handymen/GCs (harder to find — often no website/no Google presence, rely on referrals).
- **Advice given:**
  - Hire a local GMB (Google My Business) SEO agency (~$1,000-$1,500/month) to improve Google Map Pack ranking organically — cheaper than scaling paid ads further and has clearly visible/attributable results (ranking position) unlike vaguer agency services.
  - Experiment with aggressive surge/dynamic pricing: drop prices steeply during known low-demand days (Mon/Tue) to convert bookings even near breakeven (better than an idle asset sitting in the yard, and captures a remarketable customer email); conversely could test raising weekend prices since demand is inelastic then.
  - Use the physical dumpsters (bright color + decals + QR code + phone number) as "moving billboards" — have the driver do low/no-margin deliveries during slow periods partly for the remarketing/brand-visibility value.
  - Produce one polished, funny 60-90 second brand video (cites Dollar Shave Club's viral ad as the model) for the homepage — since it's a commodity market, personality/relatability can differentiate and lift landing-page conversion.
  - Redirect cold-calling time/budget toward organic digital: post on Nextdoor, local Facebook Buy/Sell/Mom groups, KSL, Craigslist, Facebook Marketplace for a few weeks instead of cold calling.
  - Tell a personal founder story on the website/marketing (e.g., "I flew to Texas and drove a truck of dumpsters back to serve the valley") — humanizes a commodity business and drives engagement/trust versus being "just another dumpster company."
  - Continue realtor/commercial-realtor referral relationships but pause active cold-calling to GCs while testing the above.

## General/cross-cutting caveats raised across segments
- Paid ads (Google/Facebook) are treated as a last resort or supplement, not a foundation — described repeatedly as a "black hole" you can sink unlimited money into with unclear attribution, versus organic/relationship-based channels that compound over time.
- Personal story/authenticity in marketing repeatedly cited as a differentiator in commodity businesses.
