# How to Start a $90K/Month Cold Email Agency
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ixJ89BIJ_Lo | Published: 2024-08-26

Interview with Sam Thompson (growth hacker, runs Quicker HQ — a holding company of digital service providers — and co-founder of Creative OS). Wide-ranging discussion of niche cold-email agency models and viral-video-driven lead generation for physical products.

## Niche cold email agency #1: guaranteed podcast guest placement
- Observed real example: an agency (contact "Sharon") cold-emails podcast hosts pitching guests, with a templated/scraped script; has 60 clients paying $1,500/month each for a guarantee of 4 podcast interviews/month — roughly $90,000/month, described as "basically all profit" from cold email automation given the low overhead of a pure outreach service.
- Business model note: guaranteeing the placement (not just doing outreach) is called out as the differentiating/valuable part.

## Niche cold email agency #2: helping international students get into US universities (anonymized example)
- Model: applies standard B2B cold-email techniques (personalized outreach flows, cold sequences) but targets university admissions advisors on behalf of international students trying to get into specific schools.
- Customer/pricing: sold directly to "highly motivated" (parents willing to pay a premium) parents at $3,000-$5,000 per student, one-time (not recurring) — explicitly niche (won't take unrelated clients, e.g., a podcast-guest cold email client).
- Scale/economics: averages 40-60 paying clients/month; estimated 80-90% margin since it's a lean team sending a relatively small number of highly targeted emails (not mass cold email) reusing a static list of school/advisor contacts across every client, eliminating the "where do I get leads" cost/complexity common to other cold email agencies.
- Churn dynamic discussed as a double-edged tradeoff: churn is inherently high (a customer's need ends once their kid is admitted — typically resolved within a couple months), but the total addressable market perpetually replenishes itself every semester as new students/parents enter the pipeline — likened to a "zipline business" (no recurring revenue, but a constant fresh crop of new customers) versus a subscription/membership business model (e.g., Massage Envy) that depends on retention instead.

## Niche cold email agency #3 (smaller/lower-ticket concept, discussed but not yet built): brand-deal outreach for micro-influencers
- Proposed model: for creators with modest followings (e.g., 30,000 Instagram + 30,000 TikTok followers) who want brand deals but lack the reach/negotiating leverage of major influencers, build a directory of DTC brand marketing contacts and send a batch of cold emails per month on the creator's behalf pitching their niche (e.g., fitness, fashion) to relevant brands.
- Proposed pricing: ~$500/month for ~100 emails sent per creator — framed as applying an existing playbook (they'd previously done something similar helping musicians get more streams via Facebook ads) to a large, underserved market of people wanting to monetize a modest social following.
- Explicitly framed as unproven/risky — the hosts note that when nobody is doing an obvious-seeming niche variant, it can mean either (a) genuine unexploited opportunity, or (b) it's been tried and doesn't work, and people reverted to proven models.

## Mass cold email strategy (opposite end of the spectrum from ultra-niche)
- Observation: most cold email agencies operate in the "middle" — sending thousands of emails/month to a moderately sized list — but new tooling (mentioned: Mailscale, Instantly) lets you spin up ~100 email accounts (with distinct proxies), warm them all over a ~21-day period, and then send tens of thousands of cold emails per day/month at scale.
- Concrete historical case study (Sam's own past business, an Instagram-creator community/agency): using ~50 G-Suite accounts sending 1,000-2,000 emails/day each programmatically via Google Sheets + GMass, sent 1.4 million cold emails in 11 days to a list of Instagram creators (list-sourcing method undisclosed), with subject line "Brand opportunity for @[username]." Result: 100,000+ applications, 30,000-40,000 people ultimately joined their community/ecosystem (brand deal matching, digital products, software signups).
- Key caveat: mass cold email only works for a mass-market product/offer — you need a large enough addressable list that you don't "run out of emails," and it doesn't work for a narrow niche service.
- Proposed replicable niche for a mass-cold-email agency: UGC/affiliate-program recruitment for e-commerce brands — i.e., mass-emailing content creators to invite them into a brand's affiliate/ambassador program (offering a discount code for their first purchase as the incentive) rather than pitching a product sale directly. Cited real example: an unnamed hat/lifestyle brand runs all its Facebook ad spend toward recruiting affiliates/ambassadors (not direct product sales) as its actual top-of-funnel strategy — mass cold email is framed as another channel that could feed the same mechanism. Unanswered/unverified questions raised as worth investigating: cost per recruited affiliate, percentage of affiliates who actually convert to producing content/sales, and average LTV of an affiliate versus a standard customer.
- General market-gap framing: cold email agencies as an industry are estimated to be actively selling into only a small fraction (guessed ~1%) of the roughly 3,000 distinct industries that exist — analogized to how few of thousands of industries have been "rolled up" by private equity, implying broad unexploited niche-application potential for the same core tactic (cold email) across many verticals.

## Personal example: an anonymized "how to spot scraped/generic cold email" trick
- Host mentioned deliberately putting a rocket-ship emoji in his LinkedIn display name specifically so he can identify when an inbound cold email/DM was generated from a scraped/bulk list (the rocket emoji shows up verbatim in the message, revealing it wasn't personalized).

## Viral-video-driven physical product/lead-gen tactics ("Neon Bears" case study and generalized playbook)
- Case study: "Neon Bears" — a single LA brick-and-mortar retail business (~20-30K social followers, some viral posts) sold an in-person "paint your own bear" date-night activity using acrylic/plastic bear molds, but did not sell the painting kits online (only a blank bear via their website footer) — described as "good at art, bad at business," since 99% of viral-video viewers could never actually visit the physical store to buy the experience.
- Sam's replication approach: sourced the identical bear-mold kits directly from AliExpress (8 different color kits), built a one-product Shopify store in an afternoon using "Solo Drop" (a conversion-optimized one-product Shopify plugin), created ad creatives via his own company's tool (Creative OS), and ran paid ads — in some cases directly repurposing the original business's own viral video footage with an added text overlay, framed as a legitimate creative-testing tactic (not a personal recommendation to copy others' footage without consideration).
- Result: roughly break-even in the first week (~"a couple thousand bucks") between ad spend and COGS; acknowledged margin could improve significantly by bulk-ordering (e.g., 5,000 units at ~$1.50/unit) instead of one-off drop-shipping from AliExpress, but ultimately judged not worth the personal time investment (opportunity cost) to scale further.
- Generalized playbook derived from this: look for physical/local businesses that have gone viral on social media but are not capitalizing on that attention online (a recurring theme; car dealership example: dealerships that remix trending video formats into local ads which don't drive purchases, since almost no viewers can act on a hyper-local car-dealership CTA).
- Concierge/white-label service idea for exactly this car-dealership scenario: build (or use an existing model like "Presto Cars," a business the host co-founded ~10 years earlier with his business partner Nick) a white-labeled concierge car-buying service — customers pay a flat fee ($800-$1,000 cited from the earlier Presto Cars example) for a no-haggle purchase at wholesale pricing, sourced via auctions and shipped nationally — then pitch dealerships to simply post about/refer to this white-labeled service (they do zero operational work) as a value-add on top of their existing viral content, since dealerships are structurally focused only on driving in-person showroom visits and would never build this themselves.
- Framing point: this kind of "less glamorous but real income-doubling" business (example given: a car salesman making $80,000-$120,000/year potentially growing to $300,000-$400,000/year by running such a service) doesn't need to be a venture-scale outcome to meaningfully change someone's life/income — contrasted with the common entrepreneurial fixation on building something huge.

## Scaling the tactic: systematically finding other viral high-ticket products to arbitrage as a lead-gen/sales business
- Proposed method: search for existing viral videos (Instagram/TikTok) advertising niche, high-ticket physical products/equipment that "enable entrepreneurship" for the viewer, then create your own value-added remix/commentary video (e.g., green-screening yourself over the original footage explaining how you'd build a business around the product) and post many of them, expecting only roughly 1 in 10 to go viral itself.
- Named concrete examples of source products used/considered: a $20,000 mobile wood-drying kiln that attaches to a 20-40ft shipping container (previously discussed in another episode re: barbecue-smoking-wood scarcity/negative-COGS tree-trimming partnership idea — see hXpn-3UczWQ notes for the fuller version of that specific business concept); a perfume vending machine business (see below); mobile wall/exterior printers (~$8,000 units, an idea the host had already covered in a separate video that reportedly didn't take off).
- Real example that DID work for the host: a previously-discussed "perfume vending machine" business (mentioned as a prior guest topic from Sam back in March) — the host revisited old footage, remixed/re-posted it with fresh commentary, and it went viral: thousands of people reached out wanting to buy the machines; the host built a landing page to capture leads, and as of recording his sales team had sent six figures worth of invoices to interested buyers (not yet paid/confirmed at time of recording).
- Monetization mechanic once a video goes viral and captures leads: approach the actual product manufacturer and either (a) sell them the leads directly, (b) negotiate to become their exclusive reseller/distributor for a region (ideal scenario: a manufacturer that doesn't sell direct-to-consumer at all, only through distributors — then all inbound interest gets funneled specifically to you), or (c) if the manufacturer is uncooperative/dismissive of an outside lead source, discard the leads or sell them to a competing manufacturer instead ("forcing a partnership").
- Cited real risk observed: when the host's perfume-vending video went viral, many viewers independently Googled the product and contacted the manufacturer directly, meaning the manufacturer may not value or need the host's captured leads if they're already receiving organic inbound demand.
- Content strategy/testing philosophy explicitly stated: treat social content as "shotgun then sniper" — post a high volume of low-effort test videos (analogy: baseball "at bats," expecting a low individual hit rate, e.g., 5%, so posting more, like 20/day, to reliably get "one home run" per day) and only invest heavy production/funnel-building effort into the few posts that show organic traction, rather than trying to predict winners in advance. Framed as inverting typical "build product then find market" logic — go to the market first (via cheap/fast content tests) and let response data tell you what to build, guaranteeing product-market fit because the product only gets built once demand is already validated.
- Related pattern cited from the guest's own SEO/content agency work: for a pet-cremation business client, an article titled "signs your dog with diabetes is dying" drove the large majority of that site's total traffic; for a separate dog-waste-removal client, "why do dogs eat cat poop?" was similarly their top-performing content — used as evidence that you often cannot predict in advance which piece of content will actually perform, reinforcing the "post many things, then double down on what works" approach over trying to hand-pick a few "high quality" pieces upfront.

## Adjacent business idea raised: "physical business starter kits" bundled with existing course/curriculum sellers
- Observation: many creators already sell $6,000+ courses/curricula teaching people how to start a specific home-service business (e.g., window cleaning, screen cleaning, cleaning businesses) — separate from the physical equipment itself.
- Proposed idea: build a company that sells the actual physical "starter kit" of equipment needed for a given business (e.g., a ~$800-1,600 screen-cleaning machine, or a full window-cleaning kit that "fits in the back of a Prius"), white-labeled/drop-shipped (e.g., sourced from AliExpress), and partner with existing course creators as an affiliate upsell — course sellers already have a "captive audience" of buyers who will need this equipment anyway, so the starter-kit company pays them an affiliate cut for referring their existing customer base. Discussed as an appealing, low-effort-to-fulfill business (a "little Shopify store" model), though explicitly described as an off-the-cuff brainstorm rather than something either party had built or validated.
