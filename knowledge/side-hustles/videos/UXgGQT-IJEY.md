# I Just Found the Perfect Business for you to Start (yes, you)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UXgGQT-IJEY | Published: 2024-12-09

Interview with Kyler Liston (Orem, Utah, 23 at time of filming) about his washer/dryer rental business — an earlier/different interview than the later case study video (see also ID UCdWRg1KQLY, same business, different specific details/numbers/timeline). This note focuses on details unique to this recording.

## Origin story and validation method
- Trigger: grandparents owned ~30 rental apartments and had stopped supplying washers/dryers; Kyler had also done third-party appliance delivery in Utah County and learned installation, plug-swapping, and common repair issues on the job.
- First-ever rental business (predates washer/dryer idea): rented out a "nine square" PVC yard-game set (church/Utah-culture activity) — bought a name-brand, non-DIY version for **$800** specifically because most competitors were selling homemade PVC versions, differentiating on quality. Rented for **$30/weekday, $50/weekend**; made several hundred dollars/month and paid back the $800 multiple times over before he lost interest and mostly stopped.
- Validation method used repeatedly across ideas: **post to Facebook Marketplace before owning the item**, gauge real inbound interest, then buy inventory only after seeing demand. For the washer/dryer idea specifically: posted a simple "rent a washer/dryer month-to-month" ad, went on a 7-day cruise, came back to strong interest, and only then started buying units.
- Explicit philosophy on soliciting opinions: "everyone's biased" — friends may be jealous and discourage you, family may blindly encourage you — the only real signal is posting to the free market (Facebook Marketplace / a landing page) and seeing if anyone actually pays or shows real interest.
- Framing on risk: describes his large capital commitments as "calculated," not reckless — always ran the "worst case, I break even or lose only X" math before committing (e.g., worst case on the washer/dryer sets was reselling them individually on Marketplace for a likely profit; worst case on an earlier idea was "I lose three months of my life door-knocking in the sun," not money).

## Capital and unit decisions
- Used ~**$30,000 (half of his ~$60,000 life savings)** from a summer door-to-door sales job to buy an initial batch of washer/dryer sets in bulk from a wholesale contact, sight-unseen relative to demand data (based on just one week of Facebook Marketplace interest) — found the wholesaler by posing as a property manager ("I'm looking for 50 sets, what's the discount") at one of several wholesale appliance outlets in Utah.
- First 3 units were bought retail from RC Willey (a Utah big-box store) at **~$1,100 each**, new, with warranty (LG, glass-top) — done to fulfill the very first validated customers quickly, even though he already knew this wasn't a scalable sourcing channel.
- Pricing logic: targeted a renter profile of someone signing a 1-year apartment lease without an in-unit washer/dryer; priced rent to be slightly cheaper than the implied cost of buying a similar new machine over 12 months, landing on **$60-85/month**. Later realized used units command the same rental price and same demand as new ones ("they just want a washer and dryer that works") — used units are "probably a better business model" since margins are much higher for the same achievable rent.
- Retrospective regret: bought too many **new** units early on; believes he could have reached the same scale with roughly **half the capital** by sourcing used units from the start (tradeoff: new units need less maintenance).

## Adjacent side hustle: paid appliance delivery/install for scratch-and-dent stores
- Separate, smaller side hustle used to build cash flow, credibility, and delivery experience: local "scratch-and-dent" appliance resellers (who buy overstock/damaged-box units from Costco/big-box chains) typically don't want to handle delivery/install themselves. Kyler left his phone number at 3-4 such stores in Utah County (about 15-20 minutes apart) and, because he was responsive and reliable ("most people won't pick up their phone"), became their default referral for delivery jobs.
- Pricing: charged **$100-200 per delivery+install**; some days brought in **$400-500** total. Used his 15-year-old brother as a paid helper on fridge deliveries, paying him **$50-75 per job** (fridges: >$200/job, ~1 hour of labor each, i.e., **$25+/hour for a 15-year-old**).
- Suggested scalable playbook for readers/listeners in any city: use **Outscraper** to scrape all local furniture/appliance stores' phone numbers, call each pretending to be a customer asking "do you deliver?", flag the ones that say no, then physically visit only those with a flyer offering delivery services — could build a network of a dozen+ referring stores.
- Equipment used: no truck — a Toyota Highlander with an aftermarket trailer hitch, plus a **stair-climber hand dolly (~$800 on Amazon)** capable of lifting 800 lbs up stairs via powered rotating tracks, which let him do all installs solo (previously needed his brother's help for any second-story delivery).

## Common objections and this operator's rebuttals (rhetorical/sales-facing, useful for framing the business to skeptics)
1. **"No one will actually pay for this"** — rebuttal: demonstrated real paying demand via Marketplace before committing capital; needed only <1% of Utah County's population to make the unit economics work.
2. **"You need insurance / liability is too risky and expensive"** — rebuttal: got 3 quotes and purchased a **$2 million liability policy for ~$600/year** (structured as $1M coverage for injury e.g. someone stepping on a screw, $1M for property damage e.g. flooding an apartment complex). Operated uninsured for the first 2 months before securing this.
3. **"Units will get stolen"** — rebuttal: acceptable business math even if some units are lost/stolen or damaged, analogous to single-family rental economics ("95 of 100 renters are great, 5 will trash your unit, but volume covers it"); optional mitigations mentioned: AirTags or other tracking devices in units (not something he'd fully implemented at time of interview).
4. **"Delivery logistics (stairs, weight) are a dealbreaker"** — rebuttal: the stair-climbing dolly solved this entirely, letting him install every unit solo.
5. **Late payments / declined cards / customer churn**: explicit policy — **never charges late fees**, on either this business or an unrelated mobile home park business he also runs, as long as the customer keeps communicating (e.g., texts back when a card is declined). Rationale: it's cheaper/easier to wait for payment than to retrieve the unit; the only trigger for enforcement action (eventually "knocking their door") is if the customer goes fully unresponsive/"ghosts" him, since ghosting correlates strongly with non-payment.
- Personal safety note: has delivered/installed in neighborhoods with bad reputations (Rose Park, West Valley in Utah) without incident; frames concerns about "sketchy areas" as overblown relative to actual experience.
- Demographic/ethical framing: pushes back on the idea that he's "predatory" or a "slumlord" — claims roughly half his customers live in brand-new townhomes (Vineyard, Eagle Mountain, Saratoga Springs) paying $2,000+/month rent who simply don't want to deal with buying/hauling a washer/dryer, not only lower-income renters. For the segment of customers who are lower-income/less financially sophisticated (host raises this candidly), frames the business as ethically preferable to alternatives: cheaper than a laundromat, and safer than buying a used $400 unit off Marketplace from a seller who won't stand behind it if it breaks a month later (his stated real anecdote). Draws an analogy to standard retail markup logic (pest control chemicals cost ~$5, treatment sold for $150-180; pool chemicals ~$5, service sold for ~$100) — the margin is the price of convenience/reliability in any service business, not exploitation per se.

## Operations, tools, and maintenance (lightning-round specifics)
- Contract evolution: started with a Google Doc "contract" and manual Venmo/PayPal/Zelle collection with personal text reminders; migrated to **Stripe subscriptions** for automated recurring billing once volume made manual collection unsustainable — also began collecting a **backup credit card on file** per customer (host notes this is common practice specifically because failed payments are more frequent in this customer segment than average).
- Startup tool cost: roughly **$50-75 in basic tools** (screwdrivers, channel locks) plus a moving cart/dolly; a trailer hitch costs about **$400** and a trailer about **$1,000**, and most passenger cars can tow the ~1,000 lb combined weight of a washer/dryer set.
- Repair resources named: **RepairClinic.com** and **ApplianceRepair.com/Appliantology** (approximate names as spoken) — searchable by model/serial number, each showing ~30 how-to videos per model covering the ~9 most common failure modes, with linked part numbers that can be cross-purchased on Amazon for roughly a third of the manufacturer/repair-site price.
- Maintenance load: averages roughly **one service call per week** across ~100 units; core mechanical failure points are limited (a dryer is essentially just a motor spinning a belt-driven drum plus a heating element, the latter costing ~$12 on Amazon), keeping repairs cheap and infrequent. Vintage/interchangeable-parts machines (era when Whirlpool owned Maytag and other brands and used shared platforms) are specifically easier to repair due to parts commonality — consistent with the "pre-2013 Whirlpool direct-drive" recommendation given in the related case-study video.
- For remote/scaled operation: built a referral relationship with a local independent repair technician who will service/replace machines when Kyler is traveling, charged at **$75-100 per callout** (he considers this a fair floor price and encourages other technicians to charge at least that much).
- Time commitment: roughly **5-10 hours/week** on a normal week; explicitly did not sacrifice personal life (traveled to Colombia, California, spent 3 months seasonal pine-nut picking) while running the business, pushing back on the "ball and chain" criticism the idea often draws online.
- Website mentioned at time of filming: "Utah Appliance Rental" — described by the guest himself as a "ghetto"/half-functional site, but functional enough to capture leads via a contact form.
