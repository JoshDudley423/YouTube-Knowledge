# Billy Quit his Job to Start an AI App Agency: Here's How
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tFP7wnWkX4w | Published: 2025-04-21

Interview with Billy Howell (stupid-simple-apps.com), a self-taught "vibe coder" who built a 4-person AI app agency after a defense-consulting-then-marketing-agency background.

## The business: building small custom apps ("stupid simple apps") for business clients using AI coding tools
- "Vibe coding" = building software using natural-language prompts to AI tools (Replit, Cursor, Lovable, Bolt, v0, Windsurf) rather than writing code by hand.
- Tool categories: **local coding environments with AI assistants** (Cursor, Windsurf) — more suited to people with some coding background, since you still manage files/stack/terminal yourself; vs. **browser-based, beginner-friendly builders** (Replit, Bolt, Lovable, v0) — you just type or sketch what you want and it generates a running app, no local setup needed. Billy's personal default/go-to is Replit, partly because it integrates well with OpenAI's API.
- Recommended approach for beginners: don't commit to one tool — build the same simple one-feature app across several tools (Replit, v0, Lovable, Bolt) side by side to learn each one's strengths (e.g., Billy uses v0 specifically for UI generation, then imports that UI into his main Replit-built app).
- Origin story of his first paid app: found a client on Upwork — a garage-owner-turned-coach who wanted a data-entry/tracking tool (tracking how many cars mechanics see per week) and assumed he needed a no-code stack (Airtable + Softr) costing ~$250/month. Billy built a custom Replit app instead and demoed both side-by-side via a Loom video; client preferred the custom app. Billy charged **$50/month** to run it (vs. the $250/month no-code stack quote) — a direct cost-savings pitch.
- Tactic for winning Upwork jobs: build a fast clickable MVP/demo (even in ~5 minutes using a tool like v0) during the proposal/pitch stage to make the idea tangible before being hired — recommended especially for your first jobs when you need to prove yourself.

## The "last 10%" problem
- Getting an AI-built app from working prototype to a genuinely production-ready, sellable product is disproportionately harder and slower than building the initial 90% — Billy states this explicitly ("the last 10% doesn't even take as long as the first 90% — it takes longer than the first 90%").
- Even experienced developers reportedly find it faster to rewrite someone else's AI-generated code from scratch than to untangle and finish it, since they don't have the mental model of how it was built.
- Shortcut mentioned: Replit has a "bounties" marketplace where you can pay (example cited: ~$150 for 3 hours of dev time) to get vetted developers/agencies to finish the last-mile work; Billy tried this once without success but sees it as worth trying again.
- Scope-reduction tip: niche B2B tools built for only 1-2 actual users are much easier to finish, because you don't have to worry about edge cases, scale, or redundancy the way a broad consumer product would require.

## Business model advice: avoid consumer/direct-to-consumer as a first app
- Explicit recommendation: do NOT start with direct-to-consumer apps — called "a money pit, a pit of despair" for beginners, because you lack the judgment (without real paying customers) to know what features actually matter; friends'/Twitter's opinions aren't reliable signal.
- Preferred path: get someone to pay you to build a custom B2B tool, get their real usage feedback, then consider reselling a similar (not identical) implementation to other clients in the same niche, since most clients want customization anyway.
- Two consumer apps Billy has personally built as side/hobby projects (self-funded, not yet monetized), illustrating the appeal/trap of consumer ideas even after advising against them: (1) a recipe generator (generate-recipes.com) that takes a household's available ingredients and generates recipes via ChatGPT, with a save/share interface — planned pricing is a flat ~$0.25 per recipe rather than a subscription, explicitly to avoid adding "more recurring SaaS to the world"; (2) an unfinished plant/bonsai "time-lapse" photo-tracking app (log daily photos of a struggling plant with AI feedback, then view the change over time) — Billy suggests the same time-lapse concept could work for a consumer "watch yourself age" selfie app.
- General B2B app ideas Billy is bullish on: (1) a stripped-down, single-purpose CRM alternative to bloated platforms like HubSpot, built per-niche (e.g., a CRM only for real estate or only for car dealerships, with only the features that niche actually uses) — pitched as a one-time-build-and-own alternative to opaque recurring SaaS pricing; (2) custom document templating tools — businesses have overlapping-field paperwork (client name, date, signatures) for onboarding new clients but mostly still just hand-edit Google Docs because existing e-signature/document tools (DocuSign etc.) are too clunky for their specific repeated templates — essentially "a custom DocuSign" built around a business's own existing documents.
- Reselling the same underlying app/build to multiple clients: Billy has done this specifically with generic, non-proprietary functionality like CRUD apps and data visualization dashboards (example: a custom HubSpot revenue-attribution dashboard built to fill a feature gap that no off-the-shelf SaaS covers, built for one client who explicitly agreed he could resell it to others) — the ethical/practical line is that data-viz/CRUD building blocks don't leak anyone's actual competitive advantage. Suggested lead-gen tactic: scrape G2 (SaaS review site) reviews for negative-sentiment keywords about specific missing features in a platform like HubSpot, then build and sell tools that specifically plug those documented gaps.
- Bigger industry thesis floated (speculative): as AI makes custom software cheap to build and APIs let you pull as much data as you want from big SaaS platforms, businesses may increasingly pay for one seat of an expensive SaaS tool plus a custom "headless" app that pulls only the data/functionality they need, rather than paying for many seats of the full platform — potentially pushing more software toward open, API-first/headless models.

## Finding clients on Upwork (tactical playbook)
- Profile setup: use a professional shoulders-up headshot; list your location as the nearest major metro area (not an obscure suburb) to appear more findable/credible.
- Timing matters most: check for new job postings frequently/at random times during the week to catch clients right when they're excited about posting — "the main thing is timing," not necessarily building elaborate application-alert automations.
- Differentiate every application with a **Loom video** showing your face — clients who've been burned before (bad freelancers, or people misrepresenting their location via VPN as US-based when they weren't) want reassurance you're real, trustworthy, and where you say you are; also show/link concrete past work whenever asked.
- Use Upwork "Connects" (paid currency, ~$0.15/connect) aggressively to boost your proposal to the top of the list and see competing bids — Billy treats this cost as trivial relative to landing a job that could become a high-value/recurring relationship (framed as "a great CPA for a potentially thousand-dollar job and lifetime relationship").
- How to vet a job posting as worth applying to: check the client's Upwork hiring history (have they hired before, don't be their very first hire — risk of flaking), how much money they've spent on the platform in total (look for a client who's spent "a couple thousand dollars"), their freelancer-given star rating, and the tone of the job post itself (overly defensive language or excessive unusual up-front requirements can signal a difficult client); also check what rate they've historically paid other freelancers — a client only ever paying ~$5/day is unlikely to pay you a real rate.
- Advanced research tactic: create both a **client account and a freelancer/agency account** on Upwork (not just one) purely to study the marketplace — browse jobs posted in your target industry from the client side, and browse competing freelancers' profile packages/pricing from the freelancer side, to reverse-engineer what services and price points are in demand.
- Related tactic: look up people/companies you already have a personal or community relationship with by searching Upwork for their industry's typical technology/workflow keywords, to see what kinds of jobs and freelancer service-packages already exist for that niche — then pitch that same service to your existing contact directly, bypassing Upwork's marketplace fees/competition entirely.
- Lower-competition job types cited as good entry points for beginners: troubleshooting/fixing an already-broken existing automation (e.g., a broken Make.com or GoHighLevel setup) rather than trying to win a full from-scratch build — these smaller-scope jobs are often skipped by bigger agencies as not worth their time but are accessible for someone building up a track record. General "infinite demand" categories cited: copywriting/editing and automations requiring a human in the loop.
- Bots/AI-spam problem: job posters now receive floods of AI-generated proposals within minutes of posting; a tactic to filter for real applicants is to embed an instruction in the job post itself (e.g., "put the word 'orange' in your proposal") and reject any application that fails to include it.
- Progression strategy: use small one-off jobs (an SEO audit, a "why are my pages ranking like this" troubleshooting call) as a foot in the door, then convert the client into ongoing paid consulting rather than one-off project work — Billy explicitly argues **consulting scales/retains better than one-off project builds**, and if you don't have implementation capacity, stick to being the "tell you what's wrong and how to fix it" consultant rather than overcommitting to build work you can't staff.
- Reality-check on "just work Upwork full time": Billy agrees you could realistically go from zero to a full-time freelance income within 2-3 weeks by treating Upwork applications as a full-time job, but warns of two failure modes: (1) inability yet to filter for good-fit clients (early on you'll take on high-maintenance/bad-fit clients until you learn your own capabilities), and (2) running out of personal bandwidth once you land enough work — requiring you to learn to hire (including hiring offshore freelancers on Upwork yourself to delegate to) or you end up stuck on a "deliverable treadmill." Notes some people successfully "arbitrage" this by winning US-only-restricted jobs as an American front and then subcontracting the actual work to offshore Upwork freelancers — acknowledged as workable but difficult (Billy: "I made a lot of bad hires overseas... it's a muscle you have to train").

## Personal decision-making / avoiding "shiny object syndrome"
- Primary filter for whether to pursue a side idea: is anyone actually paying (or trying to pay) you for it right now? If not, it's probably not worth the time investment.
- Exception: ideas that "keep you up at night" even without payment are worth quickly building a rough prototype of (now feasible in ~45 minutes with AI tools vs. the 8-hour, multi-week troubleshooting slogs of building by hand a year prior) just to "scratch the itch" and see if it's actually as compelling as it seemed — often it isn't, and you move on quickly instead of losing months to it.
- Billy estimates he built roughly 40 throwaway Replit projects and 20 local Cursor-built directories/tools before ever selling his first AI-coded app — framing all those "wasted" side projects as necessary skill-compounding, not wasted time, and pushing back on the idea that pivoting/restarting repeatedly is failure ("you just have to stay in the game and you'll win"). Tactic: give yourself hard time/scope constraints (e.g., "build this in 2 hours with only 2 features") rather than open-ended scope.

## Distribution / audience-building
- Billy has not found a reliable distribution hack outside of direct B2B relationship-based sales (leveraging existing contacts/niches) — this is why he leans on Upwork and referral/consulting relationships rather than broad marketing.
- His personal Twitter/YouTube following grew significantly after he started recording and posting videos of himself actually building apps in Replit for real client/personal projects; a retweet from Replit's CEO on one such video meaningfully boosted his reach. General advice: pick a tool "stack" and audience niche (e.g., "Bolt apps for enterprise" or "Lovable apps for teachers"), consistently publish your build process, and be ready to capitalize when any post unexpectedly takes off (Billy already had an agency landing page and paid Calendly booking link ready when his content started getting traction, after years of inconsistent posting that "didn't stick" until this particular content angle worked).
- Caveat: this build-in-public content strategy doesn't work for regulated/confidential client industries (finance, healthcare) — for those, use your own personal side projects as the shareable content instead of client work.

## Caveats
- The "last 10%" of any AI-built app is explicitly flagged as harder and more time-consuming than expected, even for people paying for outside dev help.
- Reselling a custom-built app to other clients only works cleanly for generic/non-proprietary functionality (CRUD, dashboards) — and ideally with the original client's explicit consent, as in Billy's HubSpot dashboard example.
- Consumer app ideas are explicitly discouraged for beginners due to weak distribution/validation signal, despite Billy's own ongoing hobby exceptions to this rule.
