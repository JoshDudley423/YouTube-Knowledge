# Everyone's Overlooking This Marketing Channel: Why You Shouldn't
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UPWH-nbGztU | Published: 2025-03-28

Co-hosted idea/business-philosophy episode with recurring co-host "Nick." Covers a founder-scaling framework, a Turo-style Cybertruck rental idea, and the title's core idea: AirDrop-based guerrilla marketing. (A long closing segment theorizing about Jack Dorsey being Satoshi Nakamoto is a tangent unrelated to business ideas and is omitted here as not relevant to the knowledge base.)

## Framework: four phases of removing yourself from a business
- Key idea: in the early stage of a business, the founder personally IS the product/differentiator (skill, judgment, relationships) — trying to remove yourself too early destroys the thing that made the business work in the first place.
- A "business" (vs. a "job") is defined as an entity that can run and generate cash flow without the founder for **at least 3 months**, supported by systems/processes/SOPs.
- Four-phase outsourcing sequence recommended, in order:
  1. Do everything yourself (you ARE the business).
  2. Outsource **non-differentiated** commodity work first (payroll, AP, admin).
  3. Outsource **differentiated-but-not-your-genius** work next (e.g., sales process or marketing tactics that are distinctive but not your top skill).
  4. Only last, outsource your own **zone of genius** — the thing you're uniquely best at.
- Common founder mistake flagged: trying to exit "the weeds" immediately for a passive-income fantasy before the business is big enough (~$200K/year revenue) to have the systems needed to run without the founder — you need systems + people together, not one before the other.
- Caveat on measuring outcomes: you can't A/B-test your own life/business decisions — a founder who steps back and sees growth slow from 30%/month to 20%/month may feel fine about it, not realizing seasonal timing means it "should" have been 40%/month; the counterfactual is invisible.
- Analogy used: Tesla vs. Waymo in self-driving — argument that Tesla is losing partly because Elon Musk's attention is split across many companies (SpaceX, Neuralink, Boring Co.) with hired CEOs who "will never care about the business as much as the owner," causing decline in Tesla's core "zone of genius" (self-driving) area specifically; Apple post-Steve-Jobs is cited as a similar pattern (still profitable, but no longer as cutting-edge). Explicit caveat: this trade-off is more affordable at Musk's/Apple's scale (a market-cap hit is absorbable) than for a smaller business (e.g., a $100K-200K/year agency) where the same move could be existential — "you're not Elon... get to a million dollars in revenue first" before delegating your core differentiator.
- Related mindset offered: define what "good vs. great" means for yourself explicitly — host states he optimizes for personal quality of life, and is fine having "good" (not maximally optimized) companies if that trade-off produces a great personal life.

## Idea: Turo-style hourly/short-term rental of "novelty" vehicles (e.g., Cybertrucks)
- Observation: Turo (Airbnb-for-cars) works best with unique/novelty vehicles (like unique Airbnbs need a standout "treehouse" angle) rather than commodity cars (a Camry won't perform).
- Proposed niche: rent out **Cybertrucks (or similar novelty/exotic vehicles) by the hour or even by the half-hour**, not just by the day — full-day Cybertruck rental is ~$300; the idea is to buy/rent one for a day yourself and then sub-rent it in shorter blocks (e.g., **$100/hour, up to ~10 times per day**) to people who just want a short novelty experience (a date, a test-drive-style joyride).
- Logistical/operational challenge flagged by co-host: turnover between renters (pickup, cleaning, prep) makes per-hour economics harder than per-day; the idea would get much easier with self-driving vehicles removing the need for a human to shuttle the car between renters.
- Variant discussed: wrap the vehicle with a QR code and drive it around high-foot-traffic areas (downtown, bars, outdoor shopping) as a "walking billboard," letting people flag it down for an impulse ride (e.g., $50 for 30 minutes) — Uber-driver-style opportunistic pickup rather than scheduled rental, with speed governors installed for safety/insurance mitigation.
- Explicitly flagged risk: **insurance** is called out as the "hard part" of this whole concept; no concrete solution offered, host says he wouldn't personally let that stop him but acknowledges it as a real obstacle.
- No numbers on unit economics, startup capital, or margins are given for this idea — it's presented as an unvalidated brainstorm, not a case study.

## Core marketing idea (the episode's title topic): AirDrop-based guerrilla marketing ("ebass" = "ethical bait and switch")
- Concept: use iPhone AirDrop to send a link (podcast, product page, discount offer) directly to strangers' phones in dense, captive, high-relevance physical gatherings — a channel the hosts say they've "never heard of anybody taking advantage of."
- Key targeting principle emphasized repeatedly: find the intersection of (a) **many people physically gathered in one place** and (b) **the wealthiest/most relevant subset of that crowd**, then pair it with (c) a **high-ticket, highly relevant offer** — because AirDrop only reaches dozens of people per hour (proximity-limited), so a low-ticket/low-intent offer (like a free podcast) wastes the channel (host explicitly ran the math: airdropping ~1,000 people at a conference for a free podcast might net only ~5 subscribers — "a numbers game" with poor ROI for the effort). Compared unfavorably in intent-quality to Retention.com (a real, existing "billion-dollar business" that scrapes visitor email addresses without opt-in) — same "low-intent, high-volume" trap.
- Recommended execution patterns discussed:
  1. **Competitor conference crashing**: buy a ticket to a competing/adjacent industry conference and AirDrop a targeted discount offer (e.g., "30% off if you book today") to the attendees — framed as ideal because the audience is captive, topically relevant, and has already proven willingness to spend (they just paid for that conference ticket), which qualifies them financially.
  2. **Premium-seating targeting at sports/entertainment events**: buy the cheapest ticket to an event, then physically walk through the expensive/premium sections (where the wealthiest attendees sit) airdropping a relevant high-ticket offer (example given: sports memorabilia sold to fans at a game featuring that team/era).
  3. **Vertical-specific B2B example** (a real, currently-being-tested idea from the co-host): a relative owns 60+ skilled nursing facilities and struggles to get a product ("Voltera," referenced) in front of individual SNF administrators; plan is to attend the industry's annual meeting/conference (where every administrator from those facilities will be present) and AirDrop a tightly-written pitch document (a short, benefit-led Google Doc, explicitly using AI/ChatGPT to help draft "heartstopping copywriting" concise enough to actually get read) with a trackable link, framing the awkwardness of an unsolicited AirDrop with a direct, honest opener acknowlwedging it ("guess what, that stranger is me, here's why I airdropped you").
  4. **Retail-parking-lot variant** (discussed but explicitly NOT executed by the host out of relationship concerns): airdropping every customer entering/exiting a specific big-box retailer (example named: BJ's) a link to a competing/complementary online retailer targeting that exact shopper demographic — host notes he has done adjacent guerrilla tactics for that same real business relationship (flyers on windshields, sign-spinners at the entrance, a billboard on the highway leaving that specific store location) but avoids the direct AirDrop version specifically to stay in that retail partner's good graces.
- Economics illustration given for the SNF pitch scenario: assumed $500-$1,000/month customer value, near-zero churn, 2+ year retention → estimated **$12,000-$24,000 lifetime value per converted customer**; framed at a ~10x revenue valuation multiple, meaning converting even **1 out of 100 people AirDropped** at that one conference could add roughly **a quarter million dollars of enterprise value** to the business — used as the core justification for why the effort/awkwardness of the tactic is worth it despite low individual response rates.
- General caveat acknowledged: this is a "spray and pray" tactic whose value is entirely dependent on getting the targeting (crowd + wealth segment + offer relevance) right; done against a low-ticket or poorly-matched audience it performs very weakly.
