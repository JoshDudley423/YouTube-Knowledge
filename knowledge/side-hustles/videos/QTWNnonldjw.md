# Forget Dropshipping. Be The Middleman For $800+/Job (Drop-Servicing)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QTWNnonldjw | Published: 2025-12-14

Interview with Jeff (Texas Slab Guys, texasslabguys.com), a concrete leveling ("slabjacking"/polyurethane foam injection) drop-servicing operator in Houston, TX, 3 months into the business alongside a full-time W-2 job.

## The idea
- Concrete leveling / lifting (fixing sunken sidewalks, driveways, parking lots via polyurethane foam injection) sold as a "drop-servicing" model: the operator sells and quotes jobs but subcontracts 100% of physical fulfillment to independent trailer-owning crews. No employees, no equipment ownership required to start.
- Alternate framing pursued later: sell it as a recurring "insurance-like" B2B service to commercial property managers/HOAs/retail property owners (e.g., a set number of free/covered leveling jobs per property per year), for six-figure annual contracts, instead of one-off residential jobs.

## Numbers
- Jeff's status at ~90 days in: ~$20,000 total revenue (majority in the last 60 days), 30-40% gross margin per job.
- Stated goals: $250,000 top-line revenue year 1 (targeting $50-100K gross margin); $1,000,000 top-line year 2 at 20-40% margin.
- Average job: $2,500 revenue, ~$1,000 profit (~40% margin) when subcontracted.
- Smaller standalone job example: subs charge the operator $600-700; operator quotes ~$1,000-1,100, nets ~$400 profit ("charging $800-1,000" range cited in intro for smaller jobs).
- First lead: Facebook ad campaign, cost per lead ~$4.60, converted within 1-2 hours of launch into a $2,500 job (though it ended up with ~zero margin because the crew's drive time an hour outside town wasn't priced in — a mistake he corrected afterward).
- Close/win rate: ~33% (about a third) of qualified quotes; in a recent 30-day window, ~4 jobs closed out of ~35 pipeline leads (~12 "qualified" of those 35).
- To hit $10,000/month net profit: needs ~12 jobs/month (~3/week) at ~$800-1,000 profit each, requiring ~36 leads/month (at 33% close rate) and roughly 10 quotes/week; each quote call takes about an hour if done right — estimated at ~5 hours/week of quoting time for a $10K/month profit business, doable alongside a full-time job.
- Full DIY equipment cost (owning your own trailer instead of subcontracting): ~$120,000 turnkey for a 20-foot trailer rig; a smaller van-based setup (~half the trailer's size) runs ~$60,000-70,000. Raw material (2-part polyurethane foam, A+B) costs ~$2/lb per 55-gallon drum, running $2,000-3,000/month depending on job volume. The injection "gun" itself is the highest-value/highest-risk single component, costing ~$5,000 to replace (a clogged/broken gun stops all fulfillment). Single-use accessory items ("caps"/nozzles per hole) cost "a quarter or less" each, bought by the hundred.
- Monthly overhead for Jeff's remote/drop-serviced version: ~$500-600/month total (CRM subscription [HighLevel, unlimited sub-accounts tier] plus Replit hosting ~$100 incremental). He pays no insurance himself — each subcontractor must carry their own $1-2 million liability policy.
- Extra low-cost upsell: bundles a free post-job power wash (his cost: $100-150, covers the whole property) into residential quotes, then texts the customer 2-3 days later offering a paid add-on (e.g., $100 for the backyard) — subcontracted to a separate pressure-washing company.
- Payment structure: 50% deposit at sale, remainder due on job completion; jobs are deliberately never booked the same week they're sold.
- Depth/complexity note: sidewalk voids are typically drilled to depths up to 24 inches; largest void Jeff has seen was 7-8 feet deep (a cracked sewer line had created an underground "river"), which turned an expected $1,500 job into a $5,000 job — a real cost-blowout risk if voids are misjudged.

## Process / how to do it
1. Find a niche home-service category that is old/proven (not novel) but has weak differentiation among existing solo-operator competitors — concrete leveling in Texas was chosen partly because Texas accounts for ~20% of all new concrete poured in the US, with Houston and DFW as the two largest markets, and because "leveling" fixes the same problem across every home built there, giving a large recurring addressable pool.
2. Business model: don't own equipment or hire employees — find and vet independent trailer-owning subcontractors (crews) who already do the physical foam-injection work. Common trait Jeff cited: many trailer owners are veterans (the leading equipment manufacturer, "HMI," reportedly favors veterans), and he's found subs to be easy to work with and honest.
3. Quote everything remotely/virtually by phone using photos the customer sends — do not roll a truck to visit every site like most competitors do. This lowers per-job win rate (Jeff estimates ~25% vs. maybe 50-60% for an in-person quote) but lets him quote 5-10x more jobs in the same time, netting more total revenue.
4. Lead generation channels used, in order of Jeff's mentions: Facebook ad campaigns (cost-per-lead ~$4.60), Nextdoor (both organic posting and ads), Google Business Profile / Google organic search, referrals, inbound website/chatbot form fills, and outbound cold-texting campaigns targeting HOA managers and property managers overseeing multiple properties (a "slow trickle" but a long-game channel worth staying in front of).
5. Website was built for $0 using Replit (a simple landing page with lead-capture forms/chatbot); Jeff says it currently captures ~95% of genuinely interested visitors. He's considering a professional rebuild as volume grows.
6. On the quote/sales call: focus on understanding the customer's actual pain (tripping hazard/liability, resale prep, "neighbor just got a nice driveway" vanity, etc.) rather than hard-selling; keep zero downtime between lead-in and first contact/call.
7. Differentiation pitch used instead of competing on price: guarantee quality workmanship + excellent, responsive communication; explicitly avoid bidding wars, since price-shopping customers tend to be more difficult long-term.
8. Growth/expansion strategy: rather than compete in an already-saturated large metro (Houston), Jeff's plan is to find established solo operators in Houston/DFW who've hit a revenue ceiling and want to "coast," and offer to send them overflow jobs (working within his margin) — as well as expand geographically into underserved mid-size Texas coastal/border markets (Brownsville, Corpus Christi, Port Aransas) with 200-300K population and little/no existing competition, replicating the same drop-serviced playbook market by market.
9. Longer-term pivot under consideration: sell concrete leveling as an annual, insurance-like B2B subscription/contract to commercial property owners, new-home builders, and municipalities (mentions targeting TxDOT, City of Houston, Harris County as aspirational future customers) — higher-ticket (six figures/year), more predictable revenue, and better utilization justification for eventually owning equipment.
10. Koerner's suggested sales angle for the B2B pivot (not yet implemented by Jeff): sell on liability/fear (trip-and-fall lawsuit risk/prevention) rather than aesthetics, since that framing is often more persuasive to property managers/risk-averse commercial buyers.

## Caveats / risks
- Market is described as "relatively saturated" with existing solo-operator competitors in large metros like Houston/DFW — differentiation (communication, virtual quoting speed, guarantees) is necessary to stand out, not just entering the category.
- Underestimating the void size when quoting is called the fastest way to "lose your tail" on a job — foam material costs can blow past estimates on deep/complex voids (real example: a $1,500 estimate became a $5,000 job due to a hidden 7-8 ft cavity from a cracked sewer line).
- Virtual-only quoting trades win rate for volume — a real tradeoff, not free money (estimated win rate drop from ~50-60% in-person to ~25% remote).
- Full equipment ownership (trailer ~$120K, van ~$60-70K) is a real barrier to entry for anyone wanting to skip the subcontracting/drop-servicing model.
- Early revenue was not all profit — Jeff's very first job had ~$0 margin because he hadn't priced in an hour of subcontractor drive time; the same mistake could recur if travel/logistics aren't fully costed per job.
- Business depends on maintaining a network of trustworthy, high-insurance (min. $1-2M liability) subcontractors; Jeff describes himself as fortunate to have found reliable people so far, implying this isn't guaranteed for everyone entering the space.
- Jeff self-rates as only an "average" salesman, suggesting the ~33% close rate isn't dependent on unusual sales skill, but results will vary with lead quality/qualification rigor.
