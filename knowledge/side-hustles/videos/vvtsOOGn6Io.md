# The Most Overlooked $3K/Day Side Hustle Anyone Can Start (with $0)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vvtsOOGn6Io | Published: 2026-08-07

Interview with Heather, founder of Porch Pumpkins (porchpumpkins.com), a seasonal porch-decorating-with-pumpkins business in the Dallas-Fort Worth (DFW) area.

## The business idea
- Design and install decorative pumpkin displays on customers' front porches for the fall/Halloween season; optional end-of-season pickup/removal (unsold/used pumpkins go to a pig farm).
- Started because Heather decorated her own porch (inspired by the Dallas Arboretum's "Pumpkin Village" display), neighbors asked her to do theirs, and she productized it into fixed packages + a flyer.
- Operates as a seasonal business: ~2 months of concentrated work (September-October) plus about 1.5 months of part-time work around it (order collection starts as early as July).

## Numbers: revenue, margin, growth trajectory
- Year 1 (2020): goal was 30 homes; actually did 250 homes in the DFW area; ~$100,000 in sales; ~50% profit margin (~$50,000 profit) — with Heather personally loading/unloading pumpkins in her own Yukon and doing all the design herself.
- Stated realistic first-season expectation for a new entrant: $30,000-$40,000 revenue, roughly half profit ($10,000-$20,000 net) over the 2-month season if you market well and follow a real strategy — explicitly framed as "not passive income, but realistic," contrasted with hyped YouTube numbers.
- Current scale (year 6 for Heather): sales around/exceeding $100,000/month during season; order volume grown from 250 (year 1) to ~1,300 (this year); margins currently 50-55%.
- Per-job profit example given on camera: a $600 job (package 3 + design) nets roughly $300 profit (~50%).
- A single delivery route of 6 orders (mostly package 3, ~30 pumpkins) represented ~$3,600 in revenue and took about 2-2.5 hours to deliver/unload with one helper using a wheelbarrow.
- Design time per job: ~10 minutes average (for an experienced/fast designer).

## Pricing packages (as of filming)
- Package 4 (smallest): $325, +$75 for design (95% of package 3/4 customers add design).
- Package 3: $525, +$75 for design (includes 2 hay bales); with design and pickup ($100) totals ~$700.
- "Oh My Gourd" (neutral-tone, non-orange colorway package, 3 colorways): $750, includes design — created in response to rising demand for neutral/non-orange aesthetics, especially in full-sun locations where it holds up well.
- Package 2: $800, includes design.
- Package 1 (largest): $1,350, includes design.
- Custom packages: start at $3,500+ (priced high specifically to discourage custom work and protect the owner's time vs. using pre-built packages).
- End-of-season pickup: ~$100 add-on for package 3; ~90% of customers opt in.
- Guidance on offering only a fixed menu (not custom): reduces decision friction/customer confusion (compared to "a restaurant with no menu") and protects owner time by avoiding one-off cost estimates.

## Sourcing pumpkins (the "big secret")
- Sourcing tiers from worst/most accessible to best/most scalable:
  1. Grocery store — least scalable, low margin, only 3-4 pumpkin varieties available.
  2. Nursery — more expensive but better quality/variety.
  3. Produce distributor / pumpkin broker — a middleman who marks up price but offers convenience (this is how Heather started).
  4. Directly from farms — cheapest and best variety/quality once volume justifies it; recommended farm named: Pumpkin Pyle in Floydada, West Texas (multi-generational family farm, praised for communication and easy ordering; not a paid endorsement).
- At scale, Heather now brings in pumpkins via 20 semi-truck ("18-wheeler") loads per season directly from farms.
- Deliberately over-orders inventory (rather than risk running out) — rationale given via a donut-shop analogy: running out of low-marginal-cost inventory (like unsold donuts) is a bigger lost-profit risk than holding a little excess stock, since goods cost pennies relative to sale price. Surplus late-season inventory gets sold to a waitlist (200-300 people) via extra routes, sometimes at higher (not discounted) late-season prices, since latecomers have no other option.

## Operating costs / logistics
- Costs beyond pumpkins: liability insurance, forklift rental, warehouse space rental, trailers (owner now owns several), plus contracted drivers who supply their own truck/trailer (dump trailers preferred for efficient end-of-season pickup).
- Low-cost entry path explicitly confirmed: someone could start cheaply just by owning/renting a single trailer or hiring a driver who already has one.
- Staffing/pay: drivers paid hourly and are responsible for their own fuel; designers paid per design (piece-rate), not hourly; ~99 of the business's workers are described as employees (not contractors).
- Cash flow is favorable: customers pay 100% up front (invoice sent after order, paid via Venmo/credit card), so the business carries close to zero receivables risk.

## Marketing / customer acquisition
- Primary growth channel: Instagram (described as the business's biggest driver of growth) plus word-of-mouth/referrals; the business is highly "viral"/shareable due to visual nature.
- Tactical Instagram advice: follow whatever content format the algorithm currently favors each season (e.g., carousels were trending and outperforming static single photos one season; reels another); watch marketing accounts on Instagram for free, current best-practice tips rather than guessing.
- Reply to every comment in the first ~30 minutes after posting to maximize algorithmic reach (advice attributed to an "Instagram guru").
- Uses boosted/paid promotion of organic posts, targeted to specific zip codes, as a supplement to organic reach.
- Key growth lesson: "the fortune's in the follow-up" — leads/comments/DMs convert far better with active, personalized, one-on-one follow-up than passive expectation that interested people will self-convert. Explicit numbers: of 10 apparent leads, maniacal follow-up might convert 7-8; doing nothing might convert only 1-2. A mass email blast to 200 contacts yields only ~3-4 orders; personal one-on-one follow-up (though it "doesn't seem scalable") is described as much more effective.
- Willing to expand delivery radius to any area with a large enough population base — heuristic mentioned: "how far are you from a huge population? Say yes, go there, and you can grow" (put a dollar figure on how far you'll travel based on expected order size, e.g., ~$200+ orders justify driving further out).

## Pricing philosophy / mindset
- Heather designed her package tiers based on her own preferences/willingness-to-pay as a proxy customer ("I am the average consumer... what would I do, what would my friend do") — treats even an n=1 sample (her own demand) as valid market signal, contrasted with guessing purely from theory.
- Explicit stance on charging enough: don't price based on "I could get a job for $18/hr so I'll charge $20/hr" — instead price based on what it's worth to give up time with family; if the business can't work at that price, don't build it.

## Market saturation / competition take
- Heather (who pioneered this specific business model ~6 years prior) states more competitors entering the space grows the overall market rather than just splitting a fixed pie — more visibility of "porches with pumpkins" as a category increases total demand for everyone, rather than only capping any single operator's ceiling.
- She is now expanding into business coaching and licensing a Porch Pumpkins franchise model for others to replicate the business in other cities.

## Caveats
- Both hosts stress the $30-40k first-season figure is realistic/conservative, not a guaranteed/typical outcome — Heather's actual 250-order first season was well above her own 30-order goal, achieved partly by "saying yes" to every order/area rather than turning down out-of-network customers.
- Requires real physical labor and driving during a compressed ~2-month window; not passive income despite high margins.
- Design talent is described as a bottleneck; Heather has successfully recruited designers by asking non-paying customers who self-design to send a photo, then hiring on the spot if it's good.
- Heather estimates the interview covered only ~10-15% of the "secret sauce" needed to fully replicate the business's success.
