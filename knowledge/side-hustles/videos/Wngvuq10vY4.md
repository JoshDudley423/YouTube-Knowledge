# My 3 Favorite Farmer's Market Side Hustles
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Wngvuq10vY4 | Published: 2024-12-17

Format: two hosts riffing on several distinct side-hustle ideas, only loosely organized around "farmers markets" as a theme. Covers a food-branding idea, a farmers-market pizza stand, a hot-sauce case study, two farmers-market platform/marketplace ideas, and a pet-supplement distribution model.

## Idea 1: "Boring commodity + premium branding" food products (e.g., authentic Texas-brisket-style jerky)
- Core concept: take a commodity food everyone already buys, reposition/rebrand it as premium, and charge more — cited examples of the pattern working: Liquid Death (canned water rebranded with edgy branding), premium/branded jerky.
- Specific pitch: no jerky brand replicates authentic Texas brisket flavor/technique (real offset smoker, post oak logs at ~14% moisture, cooking at 250°F, brisket trimmed lightly, 50/50 salt-pepper rub, wrapped at 165°F, pulled at 205°F, rested an hour) — most "smoky" jerky uses liquid smoke (an artificial flavoring), not real smoking.
- Host tested this himself: smoked a brisket, sliced/marinated half normally and cured the other half like real brisket, made jerky at home same day. Result: tasted "mid" — didn't outperform commercial jerky, attributed to commercial products' engineered flavor additives/preservatives (analogy: McDonald's Coke tastes different from store-bought Coke due to filtration/process, not just recipe).
- Presented as an open "public call to action" idea, not something the hosts are pursuing themselves — no revenue/cost data since it's untested as a business.
- Caveat: hosts note food businesses in general are hard to scale (see hot sauce case study below).

## Idea 2: Portable industrial pizza oven at farmers markets
- Concept: bring a high-output pizza oven (host owns a propane-powered one, brand "Ooni"-like, called "gozney" in transcript — likely Gozney) to a farmers market and sell simple, single-option pizzas.
- Cook time: ~60 seconds per pizza at ~900°F using quality ingredients (fresh mozzarella, San Marzano tomatoes).
- Unit economics cited: sells for ~$15, ingredient cost (Costco/Amazon-sourced) ~$1.80/pizza → roughly 80-90% gross margin.
- Operating model recommended: keep the menu to ONE item/size (e.g., just a Margherita) — no build-your-own, no drinks — to keep throughput fast; one person takes orders/payment, one person cooks. Modeled on In-N-Out's limited-menu approach rather than a "build your own" concept like Blaze Pizza, specifically because a narrow menu scales operational speed.
- Regulatory note: requires a food handler's permit in at least some jurisdictions — described as "not too hard" to obtain but is a real requirement, and refrigeration requirements can push you into "restaurant" licensing tier (see cottage-law note below).
- Framed as a good way to "test entrepreneurship" cheaply, but hosts flag a hard scalability ceiling on single-operator food stall businesses.

## Case study: Lucky Dog Hot Sauce (cited example of the scalability ceiling)
- A hot-sauce maker (previously featured on Hot Ones and on the host's podcast) sources nearly all ingredients within a 60-mile radius (only the glass bottle is imported, from China).
- Sells mainly through local farmers markets.
- Revenue: ~$300,000/year, described as a strong product with award wins, but a one-person operation with a real scalability problem — host estimates the owner personally nets roughly $100K/year, essentially "a job for himself" rather than a scalable business.
- Takeaway framework offered: decide upfront whether you're okay with a lifestyle-level $200-300K/year single-operator business, or whether you want to design for scale from the start (in which case farmers-market food stalls are a poor vehicle).

## Idea 3: Use farmers markets as a free market-research tool
- Framework: visit multiple (e.g., 4) different farmers markets in a day and observe which vendors have long lines vs. which categories are missing entirely at a given market but popular at a nearby one — reveals local "supply/demand imbalances" you can fill (e.g., a market lacking a pizza vendor half an hour from one where the pizza vendor has long lines).
- Extension: for vendors selling packaged/shelf-stable goods (not made-to-order) who don't yet sell online, offer to help them set up e-commerce (Shopify store, digital marketing/paid ads) as a service, or as a way to source businesses to invest in/partner with.

## Idea 4: An "Etsy/GoldBelly for farmers markets" — online marketplace platform
- Concept: an online marketplace where farmers-market vendors (food and non-food — cutting boards, jewelry, other handmade goods were cited as common alongside food) can list and sell nationwide/worldwide, extending their reach beyond the physical market.
- Suggested go-to-market: start hyper-local by digitizing one specific farmers market (e.g., "the McKinney farmers market") — build the vendor directory/online store for that one market, keep branding broad enough to expand to additional markets later.
- Related idea: approach private landowners who currently rent 10x10 vendor spots for $100-$300 apiece and offer to manage all vendor admin/onboarding in exchange for building "the [town name] Farmers Market" brand — creates both a distribution/e-commerce layer and a marketing/scheduling tool for the physical market. Landowners are often just holding land for appreciation and have no strong existing use for it, making this an easy incremental-revenue pitch.
- Barrier-to-entry note: a farmers market can be extremely low-capex to run — a grassy field with no power, gravel, or parking lot needed; vendors bring their own generators if needed.

## Regulatory note: cottage food laws
- Texas has a "cottage food law": non-refrigerated baked goods made at home can be sold to the public without any food license/permit. Refrigeration requirement is the key differentiator that pushes a product into restaurant-level licensing (this is why the pizza idea required a food handler's permit).
- Regulations vary significantly by state — California cited as much more stringent even for farmers-market sales. Advises checking your own state's cottage food law variant before starting a home food business.

## Idea 5: Pet supplement distribution via existing niche business lists (not really "farmers markets" but covered at length in this episode)
- Market sizing cited: US pet spending grew from $34B (2004) to $150B (2023); pet ownership grew from 66M to 87M households over the same period. Pet supplements alone are a ~$3B/year category, projected to double over the next 10 years.
- Case study (a podcast guest, "Nic," referenced as running a pet-supplement business):
  - Product: a 3-month "leaky gut" protocol for dogs (new food + a detox oil to kill yeast in the gut + reintroducing/repopulating healthy gut bacteria), followed by an ongoing maintenance product.
  - Business metrics: ~300-400 active customers, ~$115,000/month current revenue, ~50% margins, but has sold to 5,000+ total customers historically — implying high churn.
  - Acquisition channels: Facebook ads, a TikTok account (~2,000 followers), and a purchased list of 70,000 French Bulldog owners (bought from a third party) — French Bulldogs were targeted specifically because the breed is reputed to suffer from leaky gut issues more than other breeds, making it a natural niche entry point.
- Idea extrapolated from that case study — "distribution-first" approach to launching a pet product line:
  1. Use BuiltWith.com to find small (<$5M/year revenue) Shopify-based pet businesses, filterable by product category (pet food/treats, pet insurance, pet supplies, pet grooming, pet accessories, pet technology/trackers).
  2. Pick a product category (e.g., supplements) that's adjacent to, but not competing with, an existing business's core offering (e.g., dog-toy sellers who don't sell supplements).
  3. Cold-pitch those businesses to list/sell your product to their existing customer base — proposed deal structures: affiliate commission, equity stake, or outright buying their email list.
  4. Fulfillment stays simple: when a sale happens on their site, an automation notifies you to ship the order directly to the end customer (drop-ship style) — no need to build your own distribution from scratch.
  5. Alternative sourcing for defunct/target lists: browse business-for-sale marketplaces (Flippa, Quiet Light) to find pet-specific email lists or dead/dying Shopify stores to acquire cheaply for their list value alone (even if the underlying store isn't wanted).
  6. Marketing channel suggestion: Facebook groups for specific pet breeds are seen as a strong targeting/marketing channel (mentioned buying/running a niche-breed Facebook group as a strategy raised in an earlier episode).
  7. Niching strategy: start with one breed/species niche (dogs and cats are the largest markets; more exotic niches like ferrets were floated as further long-tail options) before broadening.
- Related side note: Shopify itself runs a marketplace for selling Shopify stores (an "eBay for Shopify stores") verified directly through Shopify's own revenue/tenure data, and notably doesn't appear to take a commission — rationale given is that Shopify would rather a struggling store get sold/kept running (continuing to pay Shopify fees) than shut down. A now-defunct rival marketplace ("The Exchange Marketplace," decommissioned ~2 years prior) was raised as a gap/opportunity: hosts speculated many listings there were of stores about to be shut down anyway, meaning a savvy buyer could lowball owners just before closure (using a website-change-tracking tool like Distill.io to monitor stale listings) — presented as a hypothetical opportunity, not something either host has executed.

## Caveats across the episode
- The jerky idea is untested/unvalidated by the hosts (their own attempt tasted "mid").
- Single-operator food-stall businesses (pizza oven, hot sauce) face a real scalability ceiling; hosts explicitly frame this as a lifestyle-business tradeoff, not a scale play.
- The pet-supplement case study has high customer churn (5,000 total sold vs. 300-400 currently active) despite decent margins — repeat/retention is a real weakness in that model.
- Food-safety/licensing requirements (food handler permits, refrigeration rules, cottage food law limits) vary by state and can materially change what's legally sellable without a commercial kitchen/license.
