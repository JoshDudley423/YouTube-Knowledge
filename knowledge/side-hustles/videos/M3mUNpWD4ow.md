# You Don't Need a Big Idea. AI Can Clone These Giants for You
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M3mUNpWD4ow | Published: 2025-07-27

Conversation with returning guest Sam Thompson (unlimitedcontent.com — an SEO/content agency; also runs hummingbirdfood.co) covering: (1) the Cluely viral-clipping-farm playbook, (2) cloning expensive SaaS ("AppFolio for $23/month"), (3) leveraging public new-business-registration data, (4) "form factor" product opportunities, and (5) a live backlink-marketplace test Sam ran during the recording.

## 1. Cluely's viral "clipping farm" playbook

- Context: Cluely (an AI tool originally built to cheat on coding interviews, branded "Cheat on Everything") went viral, got its founders expelled from Columbia, then raised a **$5.3M seed (April) and a $15M Series A led by a16z**, reaching a **$120M valuation within ~3 months**, and reportedly hit **$7M ARR within 2 months**.
- Cluely's growth tactic, per a founder's public breakdown (treated as a literal playbook to copy):
  1. **Find clippers by scrolling** Instagram/TikTok yourself — whenever you see a well-made clip of anything, DM that account with "paid clipping opportunity"; reported to get responses ~9 out of 10 times. Many active clippers are described as "hungry" teenagers, often based in Eastern Europe (Slovakia mentioned specifically) — cheaper and more responsive than going through formal clipper marketplaces.
  2. **Pay per view, not per video**: Cluely paid clippers **$0 per video**, only a bonus per 1,000 views — this means you lose nothing on unproductive clippers and can pay top performers more once identified.
  3. **Power law applies to clippers**: expect roughly 1% of clippers to generate ~99% of total views; identify and proactively reward that top tier to retain them (same logic recommended for employees generally).
  4. **Content-supply is the real bottleneck**: Cluely's founder does at least 2 podcast appearances/week specifically to generate raw footage for clippers to cut — framed as recording for input volume, not audience-building per se. The stated ceiling on how many clippers you can profitably run is how much source content you can generate, not clipper availability.
  5. **Don't try to track direct ROI on clipping** — treat it as nearly-free viral-amplification of moments that might otherwise flop; Cluely reportedly hit ~100 million views in weeks using 700+ clippers cross-posting across "thousands" of burner/personal accounts.
  6. Best suited to YouTube Shorts/TikTok/Instagram specifically.
- Discussion of who benefits most: content creators/podcasters (pure "impression farming") get the easiest lift since they already generate abundant long-form content to clip; DTC/consumer brands see lower effective CPM than paid ads but struggle because they usually lack enough raw long-form content to feed clippers — this is called out as the actual constraint for brands wanting to copy the model.
- Real-world reference: **Steven Bartlett** (Diary of a CEO) reportedly credits much of his podcast growth to systematic clipping — he has a physical button under his desk he presses mid-interview to flag "clippable" moments in real time, and reportedly pre-designs interview questions specifically to engineer viral-worthy answers.
- Suggested variant/extension discussed live: instead of feeding clippers your long-form podcast, give them your **already-cut short-form clips** to re-clip/re-post (potentially in other languages) since those already have "viral vibes"; also floated directly recruiting/paying people who are already unofficially re-posting your content (e.g., a foreign-language account already re-uploading your videos) rather than resenting the "theft" — pay them per 1,000 views or per referred subscriber (using UTM-tagged links to track it), since they're doing the work anyway.
- Framing/measurement point: results from clipping (and similarly, billboards) are attributed via **incremental "lift"** rather than direct attribution (no trackable link/QR code) — compared explicitly to how the host measures billboard ad effectiveness by watching a specific city's relative market-rank move, not by a coupon code. Podcast download numbers are cited as unusually predictable day-to-day, making any unexpected spike after starting a clipping campaign a plausible (if not provable) signal of its effect.
- General mindset advice recurring through the episode: **bias toward action over deliberation** — e.g., "don't ask 'should I do clipping,' just go hire 5 clippers and see" — because concrete results (even negative ones, like "zero calls booked from these ads") generate a more useful next question than abstract idea-comparison ever will.

## 2. Cloning expensive B2B SaaS with AI ("AppFolio for $23/month")

- Core thesis: many legacy enterprise SaaS tools (property management CRMs like **AppFolio** and **RentManager.com**, and by extension things like Salesforce) are increasingly "commodities" that AI coding tools can now replicate cheaply — a shift the guests say only became feasible in roughly the last year.
- AppFolio/RentManager charge **~$5/unit/month** (thousands of dollars/month for larger property managers) and typically hide pricing behind "schedule a demo"/"call us" — a strong signal of high-margin, entrenched enterprise pricing.
- **Concrete lead-gen tactic**: use **BuiltWith.com** (or, to avoid BuiltWith's ~$450/month cost, post a small paid task on Upwork asking a freelancer to compile the export) to scrape every website running AppFolio/RentManager's tracking subdomains/scripts (e.g., `appfolio.js` on a property site) — this yields a target list of every company currently paying for these tools, ready for a cold outreach/undercutting campaign (e.g., "I built an AppFolio clone using AI — you're paying $2,300/month, I charge $23/month for unlimited units").
- Named comparable example already executing this playbook: **PostBridge.com**, built as a much cheaper alternative to social-media scheduling tools like Buffer.
- Strategic discussion of the pricing "race to the bottom" risk: undercutting by 90-99% is powerful but can trap you in a pure price war; the guests note customer stickiness matters more than marginal price differences (a customer paying $23/month won't churn to save $4/month to a $19/month competitor) — implying there's a pricing floor where switching costs make further undercutting pointless.
- Prediction: incumbents (Salesforce, etc.) will respond not by cutting prices but by bundling AI co-pilot features to justify their premium — lower/mid-market customers are the most winnable segment for cheap AI clones since large enterprises have too much switching friction to bother moving.
- **AI-assisted competitive research tactic demonstrated live**: pull a big SaaS product's page on **G2.com** (software review aggregator), feed the URL to an AI agent tool (**Manis/Manus**, ~$20/month plan) and prompt it to identify the small number of features referenced most positively across reviews — the goal being to identify the ~3 core features that actually drive usage (the guests' example: most Salesforce users just search by customer email) so you can build a deliberately minimal ("feature-light") competing product covering only what people actually use, rather than trying to match a bloated feature set.
- Related idea floated: an **AI email-agent rollout/consulting service** — install/manage AI email-drafting tools (mentioned by name: **Kora** and **Lindy**, both AI email summarization/drafting agents) across an entire company's inboxes at $10/month/seat, positioning yourself as the integration/rollout specialist for these tools inside larger organizations (analogous to how enterprise software vendors sell implementation services).

## 3. Mining public new-business-registration data

- Trigger: a community member noticed that some cities/states (example: Baton Rouge, Louisiana) publicly publish lists of newly registered local businesses.
- Existing (low-effort, low-quality) version of this exploit already in the wild: scammy direct-mail operators (especially active in Texas, per the hosts) auto-mail every newly registered LLC generic offers like "pay us $99 to claim your EIN" (something obtainable for free) or fake "you need to buy mandatory workplace posters for $80" letters — described as "smart" opportunistic targeting but crude execution (mail-only, no personalization, easily ignored alongside other lookalike mailers).
- Proposed AI-upgraded version: build an agent (e.g., via **Replit**) that scrapes new business filings every morning, finds each new owner's contact info (LinkedIn, Manta, etc.), and sends a **personalized cold email** same-day (e.g., "Hey John, I do marketing for restaurants — want to hop on a call?") — argued to dramatically outperform generic direct mail specifically because it's individually relevant and arrives in an inbox/text rather than a pile of identical mailers that go straight to the trash.
- Two monetization paths discussed for this same data/workflow:
  1. **Run it yourself as a $2,000/month local marketing/lead-gen agency**, selling directly to the new businesses identified.
  2. **Sell the leads** to existing local marketing agencies for a flat referral fee (example figure given: ~$50/booked call).
  - Explicit tradeoff framed: selling leads at $50/call is faster/easier to start but caps your income; running the $2,000/month service business yourself is more work but scales to real income faster than trying to build/sell software from day one. General advice given: if your core goal is replacing a 9-to-5 income quickly, a service business (not software) is the more direct path — many successful software products later grew out of service businesses that "proved the model" first.

## 4. "Form factor" reframing — a lens for spotting low-invention product ideas

- Core idea: you don't need to invent a new product category — you can often just take an existing proven-demand product/ingredient and repackage it in a different physical "form factor" for a different use case or convenience level.
- Examples cited as validation of this pattern:
  - **TryCreatine.com** — same creatine powder, sold as gummies instead of powder.
  - **Neurogum** — caffeine (same active ingredient as Red Bull), delivered as gum.
  - **Run Gum** — caffeinated gum marketed specifically to runners; cited revenue **$28 million**.
  - **"Bites"** (name uncertain per transcript) — turned toothpaste into a chewable tablet form, positioned as a faster alternative for people who feel they don't have time to brush.
  - **Kodiak Cakes** — turned pancake mix into a protein-fortified baking product; still fundamentally "just pancakes."
  - A hands-free toothbrush product cited as doing "tens of millions" in sales purely via a form-factor/convenience change to a near-universal product (toothbrushes).
- Practical sourcing suggestion: for a simple product-form idea (e.g., adding caffeine to a snack), you can find a contract manufacturer via **ThomasNet** and a separate packaging/co-packer partner, rather than needing to build manufacturing capability yourself.
- Explicit reframing offered as a psychological unlock: aspiring entrepreneurs often feel pressure to be "inventors" (comparing themselves to Musk/Zuckerberg-style category creators) rather than "business operators" who take something people already buy and package/market/operate it better — the guests argue the latter path is far more accessible and still highly lucrative.
- Applied to Sam's own agency: he is applying the same "form factor" thinking to **SEO backlinks** — instead of selling backlink-building as an opaque, heavily-marked-up agency retainer service, package it as a transparent, self-serve **"Costco-style" membership/marketplace** for backlinks at wholesale prices with a flat, disclosed markup (mentioned: his agency Unlimited Content charges a flat 15% markup and is transparent about which tools/providers are used, versus competitors that mark up 400% while hiding their sourcing).

## 5. Live backlink marketplace test (Sam's real-time experiment during the podcast)

- Sam built and launched a $1 trial funnel **overnight**, with no actual product/platform built yet, purely to validate demand for a "Costco of backlinks" offer: **$83/month** for direct wholesale access to vetted backlink placements (no markup/middleman), with a 100% money-back guarantee on placements.
- Landing page tool used: **Perspective.co** (a funnel/landing-page builder — Sam is an affiliate).
- Ran cold Facebook ads directly to a $1 Stripe trial-payment link to see if strangers would pay anything for the concept before building it.
- Results shared live: **average cost per lead/acquisition of ~$21** against an $83/month target price — Sam described this as promising for eventual profitability depending on trial-to-paid conversion rate and trial length (was still deciding between a 3, 5, or 7-day trial as of filming; explicitly ruled out a 30-day trial given cash-conversion-cycle concerns).
- Anyone who actually paid the $1 during the test was immediately refunded and told the platform wasn't ready yet — the test was purely to validate willingness to pay, not to actually onboard customers.
- Sam noted an "unfair advantage" fueling credibility for this specific idea: his existing agency (Unlimited Content) already buys backlinks at scale for clients, so he can use real client results/case studies rather than fabricated ones — framed as a broader principle that a personal/professional unfair advantage (industry background, existing client base, unique access) is a strong signal for which idea to pursue.
- Underlying case for why backlinks specifically are valuable: a single well-chosen, high-domain-authority backlink can move a page from (for example) rank #11 to #9 for a competitive keyword — described as functionally similar to a paid ad, except it's a mostly one-time cost with a durable, compounding effect (potentially "paying dividends for a decade"), versus a Facebook/Google ad budget that stops working the moment you stop paying. The strategic sweet spot named: target keywords where you're on the edge of a higher click-density tier (e.g., moving from position 11 onto page-one, or from position 9 into the top-3 "high click density" band) rather than optimizing rankings that are already similarly-performing (e.g., position 7 vs 8).
- Domain-authority mechanic explained (using the host's own example): an unsolicited mention/backlink from Barron's (~89 domain authority per Moz's free checker) only drove 17 direct clicks that day, but is valued for its long-run effect on overall keyword rankings and domain authority — distinguished from a backlink that's both high-authority AND topically relevant (ideal) versus one that's high-authority but irrelevant (still valuable, just less so).
- Half-joking idea floated live: let customers pay for backlinks via **buy-now-pay-later** (e.g., Klarna-style 4 equal payments) given how normalized BNPL has become even for trivial purchases.
