# ex-tesla guy turns school bus into a fortune
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2WUqdw5xtlg | Published: 2025-02-26

Interview with Kevin Throop, founder of Toasted Tours — a wine-tour business that started with a converted school bus and grew into a converted semi-truck/shipping-container "trailer bus."

## The idea
- Bootstrapped wine-tour business built around an unusual, visually striking vehicle (first a renovated school bus, later a shipping-container-on-semi-trailer "trailer bus") to generate free virality/marketing instead of paid ads.
- Explicit strategy: build something visually beautiful/differentiated first ("lipstick on a pig"), let the product/experience market itself rather than chasing customers — modeled directly on Tesla/Apple's design-led, minimal-marketing approach (Throop is a former Tesla fleet sales employee).

## Numbers — Phase 1 (school bus)
- Startup capital: ~$65,000 total life savings; bought a used school bus off eBay sight-unseen for $3,000.
- Renovation: budgeted ~$30,000 but actually spent ~$60,000 (double) due to inexperience — redid flooring and electrical three times each; took about 12 months of self-labor (16-hour days) to complete, done secretly in a driveway (hidden from HOA/neighbors with tarps) due to zoning/HOA issues.
- First-year revenue: ~$250,000-$300,000/year.
- Pricing at launch: $139-149 per person; 8 person minimum, 20 person maximum; 4-5 hour tours; 18 miles covered per tour at 40-50 mph on country roads; stopped at 3 wineries per tour.
- Ran 2 tours/day on Fridays and Saturdays (10:30am-2:30pm and 3:30pm-7:30pm), 1 on Sundays, occasional weekday tours based on demand → roughly $1,300-$3,000/day in revenue on a single-tour day, double that on 2-tour days.
- Variable cost: ~$32/person (mostly wine cost, which the company brokers/marks up from wineries; wineries charge the tour company based on headcount served); driver tips alone typically exceeded this per-person variable cost, meaning gas and insurance were effectively the only real out-of-pocket costs.
- Insurance: ~$40,000 for the first year on the bus alone (paid upfront, with interest if not paid in full) — required because operating a passenger vehicle with 15+ capacity mandates $5 million in general liability coverage; this was ~10% of that year's topline revenue. Throop had exceeded the 15-passenger threshold by just one seat, triggering the requirement, with no way to fix it retroactively.
- Food included in tour price from the start (helps manage alcohol-related risk/safety); explicit policy: no hard alcohol ever, and wineries are spaced/sequenced deliberately to keep guests at a manageable buzz rather than overly intoxicated.

## Numbers — Phase 2 (semi-truck/shipping-container "trailer bus")
- Cost: ~$140,000 all-in for tractor + shipping container conversion + build-out (tractor units alone were relatively cheap, ~$13,000 each for good used trucks; the shipping container/trailer conversion was the bigger expense, partly due to California emissions/regulatory compliance costs). Built the container conversion himself over ~2 years (same "do it myself" approach as the bus, done at even greater physical cost — described welding burns, metal shard in his eye).
- Insurance jumped to ~$75,000-$80,000/year for the one trailer-bus vehicle.
- Capacity: went from 40 people (bus, per tour) to effectively 80 people in a single 5-hour period on Saturdays, by running two staggered simultaneous tour groups (Group Alpha picked up at 10:15, Group Beta at 11:30, shuttled between the same wineries on offset schedules) — meaning revenue capacity more than doubled despite insurance also roughly doubling.
- Pricing raised modestly post-launch of the trailer bus: from ~$139-149 to $179/person (only a ~$10 increase from prior levels despite surging demand, deliberately resisting inflation-driven price hikes).
- A viral video of the trailer bus reached ~20-22 million views across platforms (Facebook ~10M, Instagram/TikTok ~6M each) — driven entirely by organic sharing/tagging, no paid marketing; drove significant newsletter signups and brand awareness.

## Process / how it was built
1. Validate idea cheaply: bought the cheapest reliable used vehicle available (school bus) instead of a purpose-built tour vehicle.
2. Self-renovate to control cost (despite lacking any handyman/design skill) rather than hiring out, accepting a much longer timeline and higher effective cost from redoing mistakes.
3. Hired an interior designer (Erica, "Tiny Lane Interiors" — her first client) specifically for aesthetic/visual appeal, since design/beauty was treated as the core marketing lever.
4. Launched with just an Instagram account (Canva templates used for consistent visual branding) and word of mouth — no ad spend; was transparent/authentic about being a brand-new business in early posts to build trust.
5. Took payments informally at first (Venmo, handwritten paper contracts) before eventually moving to a basic Squarespace website/booking setup.
6. Pricing was set purely by observed demand ("the market sets the price" — raise if overbooked, lower if underbooked), not fixed in advance.
7. For the semi/trailer-bus expansion: before building anything, called California Highway Patrol directly to get written confirmation the concept ("trailer bus" — an existing legal vehicle classification in CA) could be made legal, then separately confirmed with an insurance carrier that it would be insurable before investing further; deliberately researched a legal precedent (a 1970s Orange County mega-bus program) to give regulators a reason/justification to approve a first-of-its-kind vehicle rather than have to take individual risk in approving it themselves.
8. Marketing: relied entirely on natural virality of the visually extreme/"illegal-looking" vehicle — asked customers to tag the business on social media; did no formal marketing budget or agency spend.

## Tools/platforms named
- Instagram, Canva (initial branding/marketing)
- Squarespace (booking/website platform)
- Venmo (initial payment collection)
- California Highway Patrol and California Public Utilities Commission (regulatory approval path for the trailer bus / "trailer bus" vehicle classification)

## Caveats / risks
- Insurance costs are extremely high and scale with passenger capacity thresholds (crossing the 15-passenger line triggers a mandatory $5M liability policy) — a single-seat miscalculation locked in the higher insurance tier with no retroactive fix.
- Very high personal/physical cost of self-building the vehicles (redone work due to inexperience, physical injuries from welding/metalwork) — Throop states he would hire a professional builder for any future vehicle rather than do it himself again.
- Significant regulatory/legal risk and a ~2-year uncertain approval process for the novel trailer-bus concept — described enormous personal anxiety that a regulator could reject the entire project at the last step for any reason.
- The "engine risk" of a single-vehicle business: with only one bus as sole income source, a major mechanical failure could zero out revenue — cited as a reason for diversifying to a more robust semi-trailer platform (redundant tractor units, ~$13K each, are cheap to swap in if one breaks down).
- Virality/novelty is explicitly expected to fade over time as the concept becomes less unique (compared to Cybertrucks going from rare novelty to common) — Throop has already planned for what a shift toward more product-quality-driven (vs. novelty-driven) demand curve looks like.
- Anticipates a first serious accident on any given trailer-bus (statistical inevitability) could generate outsized negative backlash and threaten the wider trailer-bus concept/industry (drawing a parallel to public backlash after early Tesla battery fire incidents, disproportionate to actual comparative safety statistics).
- Company plans to eventually franchise the trailer-bus concept (~4-5% revenue share model, no upfront onboarding fee) specifically to establish and enforce safety standards across any future adopters, partly to manage this systemic reputational/safety risk across the category.
