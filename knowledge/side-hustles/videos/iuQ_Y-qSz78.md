# The Most Overlooked $13K/Day Business Anyone Can Start
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iuQ_Y-qSz78 | Published: 2026-03-06

Field interview with Hunter Schenewark (417striping.com), who started a line striping / seal coating / crack filling business after hearing about the industry on a previous Koerner Office episode (the Cinco Winston / True Line Striping episode, see htehh7Ionr4 / hu8m5FUz3g0 for related content). Hunter went from zero to ~$46,825 revenue in his second month.

## The business: line striping + seal coating + crack filling for parking lots
- Started with just line striping (parking lot line/stencil painting), but demand for seal coating (asphalt resurfacing) and crack filling was so high from prospective customers that Hunter added both within his first couple months — he says he would NOT have hit his big second-month revenue with striping alone.
- Key market insight: in this space there is "way more demand than supply" — when Hunter tried to subcontract seal coating work to existing local providers, they either quoted a month+ backlog (meaning they're already profitable/busy and don't need his referral business) or were companies with bad reputations nobody wants to work with — pushing him to learn and do it himself instead.

## Starting with almost no capital
- First "job" was free (for a nonprofit) — 5 lines, materials cost just $5 in paint, done to practice/learn on a real site after practicing with plain water on an old parking lot first.
- Believes someone could realistically start this business with a $20 paint roller and a bucket of paint, no machine — tedious (lines must be taped for straightness) but functional; recommends earning your first ~$3,000 in revenue this way before buying a machine, rather than buying equipment upfront.
- His own path: bought a used line striping machine (a Graco 3400, the smallest/entry model) for $2,300 used (new cost for the same model: $6,000-$7,000); also bought a set of contractor-grade stencils for $800 (heavier/thicker material, worth the extra cost per Hunter).
- Equipment recommendations if upgrading: Graco brand specifically recommended (used by Hunter, describes it as the best available, "painted America, you see it everywhere"); a Graco 3900 (dual-gun, "a little fancier and bulkier") is the step-up model from the 3400.
- Crack-filling machine: bought new from a company called "Seal Right" (Missouri) for $1,500 — no issues, happy with the purchase (unlike some used-equipment parts headaches on the striping machine).
- Seal coating equipment: a trailer with tank and pump, bought used for $6,000 — happy with this purchase and would repeat it.
- Paint cost/sourcing tip: PPG's "Flint Fast Dry" brand runs about $100 per 5-gallon bucket (yellow/white); the other major brand, Sherwin-Williams, runs $150-160 per 5 gallons and, per Hunter's and others' experience, clogs the machine more and needs straining 2-3 times — a real cost/quality tradeoff worth knowing.
- Water-based paint is used for asphalt; oil-based paint is available too but should be avoided if the surface has been seal-coated, since oil-based paint turns yellow/hazy over a seal coat.

## Customer acquisition
- First free customer: posted in the local town's Facebook group asking people to vote on which local business most needed a parking lot restriped ("Hey, I'd love to help out a business — vote below for who you want to see have a new lot") — generated engagement and name recognition, and after picking a "winner" he reached out to other nominated businesses to see if any would pay.
- Primary paid-customer channel: cold email. Buys scraped lead lists from a freelancer found on Upwork for $5-6/list rather than building lists himself ("not worth the time"). Sent ~50 emails on day one, ~300 total in the first week or two.
- Email pitch used: casual and disclosed being new — "Hey, I'm new to this. I will give you a good price. Let's make your church look good for Christmas" — explicitly leading with honesty about being new rather than hiding it (in hindsight, he thinks leaning into "I'm brand new" more explicitly, rather than just "I'm here to help," would have worked even better, since "people want to help you" and are generally receptive).
- Close rate: about 40-50% overall; higher when quoting in person versus remote quoting (some customers don't want an in-person meeting and are quoted without one).
- Tried door-knocking first but found it not a good use of his time/personally uncomfortable, so pivoted to email — explicit point made that door-knocking (which works well for others in this niche, e.g., Cinco Winston) is not required for success; different founders can use different acquisition channels based on personal fit.
- Church-specific dynamics: churches were an early, disproportionate customer base — practical advantages cited: lots are typically empty on weekdays (easy to block off and work without disrupting traffic, unlike his very first job which had to be done at night due to daytime car traffic), and churches are seen as low payment-default risk ("they're not going to run away on me"). Downside noted: church jobs often require board approval, adding a multi-week sales-cycle delay before a quote converts.
- On-site marketing/lead-gen tactic: use branded barricades/cones (rather than generic ones) to block off active job sites — doubles as advertising to passersby and other business owners; a larger competitor does this at scale. Yard signs also planned. Cones alone get driven over/moved by impatient drivers, so branded barricades serve a dual purpose (blocking + advertising).
- Perceived professionalism matters more than appearance/equipment: Hunter closed a $26,000 job (see below) while personally showing up in a beat-up $2,000 personal minivan (which had a raccoon damage the interior and had two gallons of striping paint spilled in the back) rather than a truck — his stated view is that professionalism is about behavior/respectfulness, not vehicle, though he acknowledges his close rate did improve after eventually getting a truck.
- Invoicing/payment: uses a software called "Quote IQ" for sending quotes; most church customers pay by check (no card processing fees). For striping-only jobs (low material cost, ~$1,000-1,500), payment collected at completion is fine; for seal coating (much higher material cost), he charges 50% upfront and the remainder on completion (or within about 7 days).

## Pricing (concrete, itemized)
- Line striping: priced per line. Started at $4.50/line, moved up to $5/line as baseline; charges up to $7/line for harder jobs (e.g., traveling further, or lines so faded they must be re-measured/re-aligned with a chalk line rather than just repainted over existing lines).
- "No parking" hatching areas: charged as a flat multiplier — roughly double a standard line's rate (~$10 per hatched area), not scientifically calculated, just a practical rule of thumb.
- Handicap stencils: $25 each (separate line item from regular stencils at $10-25 each depending on complexity).
- Fire lane markings: adds "a couple hundred bucks" to a quote.
- Some operators (not always Hunter) charge extra for switching paint colors mid-job since it requires flushing the machine — mostly a time cost, not material cost.
- Crack filling: priced by linear foot at $0.30-$1.00 per linear foot (he described it as being quoted per linear foot, though also mentioned per-square-foot phrasing in the same breath — treat the $0.30-$1 range as the effective per-unit price). For jobs with extensive/scattered cracking, he doesn't measure every single crack — he'll estimate and quote a lump sum rather than itemize exhaustively.
- Seal coating: quoted primarily from satellite imagery/measurement software (not always requiring an on-site visit) at a customer-facing rate of $0.15-$0.25 per square foot. His raw material cost from his asphalt supplier is about $0.06/sq ft — roughly a 3-4x markup over material cost, before other overhead.

## Unit economics (concrete worked examples, walked through job by job in the video)
- Small church line-striping job: 82 parking stalls, quoted at $760 total (mostly $5/line lines, plus $10 hatching areas, $25 each for handicap stencils, one missed/undercounted small area given as a "freebie"); took 2-2.5 hours; materials cost ~$130 (mostly white paint, a little blue); net: ~$550-560 profit for 2.5 hours worked, i.e., roughly $220-250/hour profit as a solo operator.
- Best single-day revenue: $13,000 from a one-day seal coating job alone (line striping for that same property was billed separately the next day for an additional $1,700).
- Biggest job overall: $26,000 (a large church) — this was the job he showed up to in his $2,000 personal minivan.
- Seal coating worked example: a ~46,900 sq ft (~1 acre) lot quoted around $9,000+, completed in a single ~10-hour work day; material cost from supplier ~$0.06/sq ft → about $2,800 in materials (~46,900 x $0.06); labor for 2 hired workers at $30/hr for a 10-hour day = $300 each ($600 total); Hunter's own rough breakdown: roughly 10% of revenue to labor, 30% to materials/costs, leaving about 60% gross margin — and even fully outsourcing execution to hired workers, he estimates around 50% net profit is achievable on a job like this (~$4,500 profit in a day even without doing the labor personally).
- Crack filling economics: heat-up time ~20 minutes (propane-heated material) plus about 2 minutes of actual application per typical crack section; a $38 crack-fill line item example given (38 linear feet at $1/ft); smallest crack-filling job he's done was just over $400, but most run $2,000-3,000; material cost for a small $38 job was only $5-10 — implying gross margins of 80-90% when quoted correctly. Important caveat/cautionary tale: he once spent 2 full days on a crack-filling job and walked away with zero profit because he misjudged crack depth (some cracks were 6-7 inches deep, consuming far more material than a shallow crack would) — his revised rule of thumb is to budget around 70% gross margin (rather than assuming 80-90%) to account for this variability risk.

## Revenue and profit trajectory (concrete numbers)
- Started the business with just over $3,000 in initial capital (equipment + stencils), though he continued buying additional equipment during the first two months, which reduced apparent early "profit."
- Month 1 revenue: just over $2,800; roughly 75% profit margin since he was doing all labor himself (i.e., roughly $2,000+ net for the month) — modest, but validated that the model worked and he already had larger jobs lined up for the following month.
- Month 2 revenue: $46,825 — driven by the pipeline built in month 1 finally converting/closing, not by any new marketing push; worked 10-12 hour days, 6 days a week for a couple of weeks to keep up, and hired helpers to handle volume.
- Month 2 actual take-home profit: just under $20,000 (explicitly distinguished from "profit on paper" that stays reinvested in the business — this was money he personally took home), even after buying more equipment and subcontracting out some overflow work during that period.

## Caveats / lessons learned
- Pricing crack-filling work by rough estimate (rather than precisely measuring every crack) carries real risk of underquoting if crack depth is greater than it appears — his own worst-case example resulted in 2 days of work for zero net profit.
- Church customers bring sales-cycle delay (board approval can take a couple of weeks) even when they're a reliable, low-payment-risk customer type.
- Subcontracting seal coating/crack-filling to established local providers is often not viable early on, because established players are either too busy (booked out a month+, meaning they don't need referral overflow) or are poor-quality operators nobody wants to work with — reinforcing that new entrants may need to build in-house capability themselves rather than relying on partnerships.
- Believes appearance (a beat-up personal minivan) does not have to block landing large jobs, though he acknowledges his close rate improved after eventually acquiring a proper truck — professionalism in communication/behavior was the bigger lever in his experience.
