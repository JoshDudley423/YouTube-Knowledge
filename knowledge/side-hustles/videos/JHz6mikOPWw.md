# He Can't Code but His AI Agents Make Him $5K/Month
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JHz6mikOPWw | Published: 2026-04-08

Interview with Vadim (18 years old, no coding background), founder of Vugola (vugolaai.com), an all-in-one video-clipping/scheduling/captioning SaaS tool built entirely via "vibe coding" (natural-language AI prompting, no manual coding). Positioned as a direct, smaller competitor to Opus Clip (which has raised $50M).

## The business idea

- **Vugola**: an all-in-one tool combining video clipping, scheduling, and auto-captioning for "clippers" (people who repurpose long-form podcast/stream content into short-form clips for pay) and marketers/content creators. Differentiator vs. competitors (esp. Opus Clip): combines clipping + native social scheduling in one ecosystem, and is built "agentic-first" (designed to be usable/operated by AI agents, not just humans).
- **Underlying market thesis on "clipping"**: creators/streamers pay clippers roughly **$0.01-$1 per 1,000 views** (CPM-style) to repurpose their long-form content into short clips distributed across many small sub-accounts, multiplying total reach/impressions far beyond the creator's own channels (example: one streamer cited as having 1,000+ clippers). Vadim's thesis: clipping is an early, permanent, and growing branch of marketing as short-form video keeps growing (referencing platforms/programs like "WHOP" and "Content Rewards" that connect clippers to paying creators/brands).
- Distinguishes clipping's effectiveness by content type: for **podcasters**, clips that deliver real value tend to convert short-form viewers into long-form subscribers well; for **streamers** whose clips are purely entertainment-driven, conversion to the long-form channel is comparatively weaker (his own unverified read, based on running a prior clipping agency).

## Concrete numbers

- Started building the product in **October** (previous year) with zero coding knowledge, using the no-code tool Lovable; the founder describes his early prompting as "the noobiest vibe coder you've ever seen."
- First actual paying customer/dollar: **February 2nd**.
- First full month of revenue (March): **$5,000 MRR**, at an **~80-85% profit margin**.
- One day, after implementing upsell flows, revenue spiked to roughly **$900 in a single day**.
- Current user base at time of interview: **267 paying users** (no free tier anymore).
- Total clips generated across the platform: **10,000+**.
- Pricing tiers: **$9/month, $39/month, $99/month** (monthly recurring); also offers yearly plans and post-purchase upsells (a pop-up offering a better deal — e.g., quarterly — right after selecting monthly, plus a custom checkout page upsell for one-time additional "credits").
- Tooling costs: Claude Code Max subscription at **$200/month via the web app (vs. $250/month via the mobile app**, due to Apple's ~20% app-store cut) — plus a second AI coding subscription to OpenAI's Codex as a backup/rotation when Claude Code's usage limits are hit. Each $200/month plan is described as unlocking roughly **$15,000 worth of underlying compute/token credits** — Vadim says he deliberately maxes out his usage limits every week to extract full value.
- Backend AI cost structure: uses **Gemini API** (not Claude) for the actual video-analysis/clip-generation pipeline specifically because Gemini has a strong VLM (visual language model) that can analyze the video itself (speaker identification, context, e.g. "this is a podcast" vs. "this is a stream"), not just the transcript. Targets 70-80% gross margins on the AI-cost side when pricing plans.
- Estimated equivalent human-labor cost of what his AI agents currently do: roughly **$50,000-$70,000/year** for a single functional area (e.g., customer support/analytics) — versus paying a few hundred dollars a month in AI subscriptions to get comparable output.

## Process / how he built and grew it

1. **Deliberately researched market timing before building**: instead of building many small/simple apps and seeing what sticks (a common startup-community pattern he explicitly critiques), he picked one thesis (clipping = a durable multi-year trend) and committed to building one complex, defensible product around it.
2. **Competitive research process**: manually tested every competitor's product, read customer reviews to find complaints (e.g., that Opus Clip is expensive, clipping is slow, and reuses overlapping clip segments across multiple outputs — which can get flagged/deprioritized by platform algorithms as duplicate content) — then designed Vugola to directly address those specific complaints.
3. **Vibe-coding tool progression**: started with **Lovable** (found it too expensive — burned ~$500 of credits in 4 days — and produces recognizable "AI slop" UI/design) → switched to **Claude Code** in the terminal, which he credits as the point everything "started working out."
4. **Recommended minimal stack for a non-coder building a real web app**: Supabase (backend database), Vercel (hosting/domain), GitHub (code storage/version control — "like Google Drive for your code"), and an AI coding agent in a terminal (Claude Code or Codex) — argues this skips needing consumer no-code tools like Replit or Lovable entirely once you're willing to work with a terminal-based agent.
5. **Free-to-paid conversion experiment**: initially launched with a free tier (mirroring Opus Clip's own free plan) but saw almost no revenue — users repeatedly created multiple free accounts to avoid ever paying, burning through his AI credits. Switched to a **paid-only model starting at $9/month** in late February; revenue immediately began flowing. Lesson drawn: a paid tier (even a cheap one) filters for real intent far better than "free + hope for upgrades," at least for this specific product/audience.
6. **Initial free-user/feedback acquisition via an AI marketing agent (built on "Open Claw")**: configured an autonomous agent to scan Reddit, LinkedIn, and X/Twitter for people complaining about clipping tools or specifically about Opus Clip (e.g., during a reported 48-hour Opus Clip outage), compile a list of usernames/posts, so Vadim could personally DM them an offer to try Vugola free — this generated his first ~450 users and a large volume of product feedback, even though most were non-paying.
7. **Marketing channel split**: organic growth has come almost entirely from **X/Twitter** (personal brand, ~27,000 followers) via consistent "build in public" posting; he considers Twitter actually a suboptimal audience fit for a video-clipping tool compared to Instagram/YouTube Shorts/TikTok (which he's just starting to post on) but hasn't needed to diversify yet because Twitter alone has driven paying growth.
8. **AI agent stack for running the company itself** (not just coding): uses a multi-agent setup nicknamed like an internal team — a "CEO" agent, a "brain bot," a marketing/growth agent, a customer-support agent (using PostHog analytics + cron jobs), and a coding agent — describing this as functionally running an entire small company's worth of departments via AI for a few hundred dollars a month in subscriptions instead of $50-70k/year salaries.
9. Switched his primary autonomous-agent framework from "Open Claw" to **"Hermes Agent"** midway through the project, citing Open Claw's weak/inconsistent memory (had to repeatedly re-teach it things, contradictory memory) versus Hermes Agent's built-in persistent memory and 40+ pre-built skills, plus better autonomy (it proactively works through blockers and only pings him when genuinely stuck, versus needing constant hand-holding).
10. **Analytics**: uses **PostHog** (on its free tier) to track full user journeys — signup, first clip, first download, first post, first caption, cancellation points — configured by asking his AI agent to design and implement the tracking events, since he didn't know what to track himself. Acknowledges he didn't set this up from day one, which limited his ability to analyze free-to-paid conversion rates in retrospect.
11. **Future direction discussed**: exploring partnership talks with Constant Rewards (a clipper-payout platform, founder Daniel Bitton) to build a fully agentic clipping pipeline — an AI agent that would find a target video, clip it, add trending sounds/B-roll, schedule/post it across platforms, and submit the resulting links for CPM payout, with no human clipper involved at all.

## Prompting / process tips for building with AI agents (not side-hustle-specific, but part of the "how")

- **"If the AI gives you a bad result, it's a skill/context issue on the user, not the AI"** — repeated framing throughout; better outcomes come from more specific prompts and more reference context (explicitly recommended: take screenshots of reference designs/websites you like, write out in your own notes exactly what you like about a nav bar/header/etc., and feed that to the agent — rather than just saying "copy this site").
- **Ask your own agent what tools/API keys/skills it needs to become more autonomous** (e.g., how he discovered "Chrome DevTools MCP," which lets an agent open browser tabs, click, and take screenshots on its own).
- **"Reverse prompting"**: configure the agent to ask you clarifying questions back, rather than guessing at ambiguous instructions.
- **A standing "keep working until done" instruction** ("the let-them-cook prompt," in the host's words): explicitly tell the agent to work through blockers autonomously and only message you when the task is fully complete or it's genuinely stuck — reduces need for constant supervision.
- **General "agentic-first" business philosophy** discussed with the host: framed as the next step in a historical progression (internet-first in the 2000s → mobile-first after the iPhone/Instagram → AI-first ~3 years ago → agentic-first now), where a business should be architected so that AI agents (not just humans) can operate parts of the workflow, and eventually so that other companies' agents can interact with your product via API/"agent skills" rather than requiring a human user interface.
- **Framework for identifying a good niche/opportunity** (Vadim's own, non-AI-assisted method): spend real time (a week or two) doing manual research into a market — competitors, where the industry/technology trend is headed — then explicitly mind-map how you could build an "agentic" version of an existing manual workflow in that niche (recommended tool: **Miro** for mind-mapping). Emphasizes becoming a genuine subject-matter expert in the chosen niche as the actual edge against well-funded competitors, rather than just chasing whatever's trending.

## Caveats / risks

- Vadim explicitly frames this as "simple, but not easy" — reiterates that building even a "simple" agentic SaaS still required pushing through real technical roadblocks (e.g., getting face-tracking and video-cropping features to work reliably) and that he seriously considered quitting multiple times.
- His stated $5,000/month figure is very recent (first full month) and self-reported; margin and growth claims are not independently verified.
- Heavy reliance on multiple $200/month AI subscriptions (Claude Code Max + Codex) as a matter of course — this is a real, non-trivial recurring cost structure for solo builders trying to replicate the model, even though it's framed as far cheaper than hiring humans.
- Conversion-tracking/analytics were not set up from the start, so his own understanding of what drove monetization (vs. what didn't) is incomplete by his own admission.
- The pivot away from a free tier worked for this specific niche/product, but Vadim notes his direct competitor (Opus Clip, well-funded) does successfully run a free tier — implying this lesson may not generalize to every SaaS, only to Vugola's specific user behavior pattern (multiple-free-account abuse).
