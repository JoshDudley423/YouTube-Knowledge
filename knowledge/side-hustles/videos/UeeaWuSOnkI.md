# How to Buy Big Things with Little Money
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UeeaWuSOnkI | Published: 2026-02-10

Solo host episode (Chris Koerner) on creative low/no-money financing tactics for buying real estate, businesses, equipment, franchises, or equity — not a specific business idea, but a toolkit relevant to anyone trying to acquire an existing business or asset as a "side hustle" path instead of starting from scratch. Host cites his own track record: 17 years buying real estate, 7 years buying RV/mobile home parks, bought an $800K business for $50K down, backed a friend's $2.2M business purchase where the friend put only 10% down.

## Core philosophy
- "Constraints equal creativity" — lack of capital forces better negotiation questions (what does the seller actually want? what terms/timeline are flexible vs. the price itself?) rather than reliance on cash, credit, or standard financing.
- Explicit argument against raising venture capital for most startups: having a large cash cushion removes the forcing function of creativity (leads to premature hiring, overhead) — cites that VC-funded startups statistically don't outperform bootstrapped ones.
- Central relationship principle (the "mom vs. stranger" analogy): the entire game is about moving a seller from "stranger" (near-zero chance of extending trust/financing) toward "mom" (near-certain trust) on a trust spectrum — via repeated in-person visits, follow-up, genuine interest in their expertise, and finding authentic commonality ("that other weirdo who reminds them of themselves at that age"). Concretely estimated: doing this can move a seller's odds of saying yes to a favorable deal from ~1% to ~10-20%.
- Don't get emotionally attached to any single specific asset/deal — must be willing to walk away from any deal; treat it as pure math, not a "must-have."
- Be flexible on **geography, asset size, or specific asset type** within an asset class you love (e.g., "an RV park" generally, not "this specific RV park an hour from me") — being rigid on a single deal/seller removes your leverage since you can't force anyone to sell.

## Tactic 1: Build in public (document the journey on social media with zero audience)
- Example cited: a friend of a guest from a prior episode (Isaac French, who built and sold Live Oak Lake, a cabin resort near Waco, TX, for $7-8M on ~$1.5-2M of cost) wanted to build similar short-term-rental cabins with no money — started an Instagram account from zero followers, posting raw unedited iPhone videos documenting the entire process (planning, fundraising, building). Grew to **130,000 followers** by launch, which produced a ready pool of potential investors and future first customers before the property even opened.
- Tactical execution advice: study accounts already succeeding in your niche — note their hooks, background music, posting frequency/days, and asset type; scrape/copy their video comments into ChatGPT and ask it to identify recurring themes (e.g., if people keep commenting about "quitting your job to do this," that's a signal to lean into that narrative in your own content).
- Platform optimization logic: prioritize **comments and shares over likes** as the target metric, since a comment takes far longer to produce (5-50+ seconds) than a like, signaling stronger engagement/session time to the algorithm; also optimize for average view duration relative to video length.
- Explicit trade-off named: public accountability/risk of visible failure or algorithmic obscurity, but framed as a clear asymmetric bet (low cost of posting vs. large potential upside of building an audience/investor pool for free) — advises shedding ego, since "people aren't thinking about you" regardless.
- Applies to any acquisition context, not just real estate: building a business, buying a business, buying a franchise, or even selling a business.

## Tactic 2: Seller financing (the episode's main focus) — structures and how to increase odds of a yes
General framing: seller financing (rather than borrowing from friends/family, which risks personal relationships across many people for one large amount) concentrates risk into a single relationship (the seller) and can be win-win if framed well — sell your personal story (host's example: "growing up riding in your dad's truck fixing up rental properties") as an intangible that can be worth as much to a seller as an extra $5,000 in cash down payment. Caveat: getting sellers to publicly claim "100% seller financing is easy and common" is often exaggerated — most sellers want cash to pay off their existing note and bank it; true 100% seller financing is easier for service businesses (goodwill-based) than real estate (harder due to higher stakes).

1. **Balloon payment structure**: minimal/no money down, small monthly payments for a set term, then one large lump-sum payment due at the end; the buyer's plan is to refinance or sell/exit before the balloon is due. Worked numerical example given: buy a landscaping business generating $100K/year profit at a 2x multiple ($200K purchase price) with $10K down and $5K/month payments ($60K/year, funded from the business's own profits, not the buyer's pocket); improve marketing/reduce costs to grow profit to $200K/year, then sell 2 years later at a 3x multiple ($600K) — the balloon note gets paid off entirely at that sale's closing, meaning the buyer never personally has to produce the balloon sum. Same logic said to apply to RV parks, mobile home parks, self-storage, multifamily, duplexes/fourplexes. Risk: if you can't refinance or sell by the balloon due date, the seller (typically first-lien holder) can reclaim the asset.
2. **Interest-only payments**: pay only interest for a set period, no principal reduction — eases near-term cash flow for a tight-margin business, but interest keeps compounding and the full principal note eventually comes due, carrying the same "balloon" risk as above.
3. **Graduated payments**: start with very small monthly payments that step up over time (example given: $500/month for 6 months, then $700, then $1,000, then $1,500, then $2,000). Framed as a trust-building alternative to a longer no-payment period — shows the seller from month one that you're not a scam/flake, which matters when they don't yet know if you'll follow through on improving the asset.
4. **Partial seller carryback** (a.k.a. seller second/carry-back note): combine traditional bank financing with a seller note to fill a down-payment gap. Worked example: a $1M apartment complex (worth $1.2M as-is, believed to be worth $2-3M post-value-add); buyer has $50K, bank requires $200K down, leaving a $150K gap — seller carries a $150K note, bank funds $800K, buyer puts in $50K. Named risk: the seller note is typically in **second lien position** (subordinate to the bank), meaning the seller could be left with nothing if the buyer defaults, making sellers more resistant than to full seller financing but still more commonly obtainable in practice than 100% seller financing. Real example cited: host's best friend bought a $2M+ business using an SBA loan for ~80%, seller held ~10% of the note, and the buyer's own 10% down payment was itself partly funded by friends/family/the host's own investment — illustrating combining multiple financing sources.
5. **Shared appreciation mortgage**: instead of a higher sale price upfront, the seller takes a percentage of future profits/rent growth. Example structure floated: "once rents reach X or property value reaches Y, I'll start sending you Z% of collected rents." Can be used as leverage against a seller who claims the asset is worth much more than they're pricing it — instead of arguing or walking, offer to let them share in that future upside in exchange for better financing terms today.
6. **Lease-to-own / master lease agreement (with option to purchase)**: lease the entire asset from the seller for a fixed term at a price derived from its current cash flow, with a predetermined future purchase price/option — the seller keeps title (and thus keeps first-lien-equivalent security) throughout the lease term. Detailed real example from host's own deal-making: a Louisiana RV park, seller was asking ~$2M for a park worth ~$23M once fully stabilized (per host's projections). Structure proposed: lease the park for 2 years at $7,000/month (below the seller's current $12,000/month gross rents — the difference the seller forgoes is compensated by certainty and future upside), with a firm agreement to either buy the park in 2 years for $4M or walk away entirely, leaving the seller with the property back plus 24 months × $7,000 = $84,000 collected (host's script for that scenario in the recording: "no harm, no foul," you keep what you had plus that lease income). Frames this as effectively risk-free for the seller (they always keep the property if the buyer's turnaround plan fails) while giving the buyer time to prove out a value-add thesis before committing full capital.
7. **Zero-down variants (traded against rate/price)**: negotiate 100% seller financing by offering a higher interest rate or a higher total price in exchange for $0 down (e.g., seller wants 5% interest/10% down — counter with 8% interest/0% down; seller wants $300K at 5%/20% down — counter with $400K at 7%/2% down). Host suggests using ChatGPT to rapidly generate many alternative structures along these trade-off axes.
8. **Asset swap / services in lieu of down payment**: offer consulting, marketing/agency services, or other in-kind value instead of cash as part or all of the down payment; alternatively, offering collateral (a vehicle, a house, another property) in place of cash.
9. **"Subject to" (Sub2) existing debt**: take over the seller's existing loan payments directly; the underlying debt legally remains in the seller's name while the buyer makes the payments and gains control of the asset with little to no cash out of pocket. Works best when the seller is highly motivated/tired of the asset and the existing debt balance is relatively low.
10. **Delayed down payment**: agree to a standard sale (e.g., 20% down on a $1M asset) but negotiate to pay the down payment 12-24 months after closing rather than at closing; if it's never paid, the seller (already in first-lien position) reclaims the property.

## Personal anecdotes used to illustrate "everything is figure-outable"
- At 21, as a broke college server making $2.13/hour + tips in 2008, bought a $90,000 house with **3.5% down** using government first-time-buyer grants plus savings; when the bank required PMI (mortgage insurance) that would raise payments from $535 to $700/month, he pushed back and the bank ultimately offered an alternative loan package without PMI still at 3.5% down — because the bank preferred a less-profitable loan over losing the deal entirely (a real financial-crisis-era anecdote, not a guaranteed general outcome). He later received an $8,000 first-time-homebuyer tax credit (Obama-era policy) ~9 months later and used it as a down payment on a second home nearby, his first rental property. Owned the original home 11 years, rented it for 9.
- Analogy to a custom-home build experience: was told mid-construction "we can reverse, change, fix, or add anything at any stage — the only question is how much you're willing to pay," reinforcing his "everything is figure-outable" framing.
- Vexillology (flag design) analogy: a flag must be legible when shrunk to index-card size — used as a metaphor for stripping a financing negotiation down to its essential, simplest terms rather than relying on complexity, big down payments, or perfect credit.

## Caveats
- Standard disclaimer: all of these structures carry real risk; the host explicitly says this is "the tip of the iceberg" and outcomes depend heavily on individual risk tolerance and situation — not financial advice.
- Second-lien seller notes (partial carrybacks) leave sellers financially exposed in a buyer default, which is why they're harder to get than full seller financing but easier than many assume.
- Sponsor segment (Quo/OpenPhone rebrand, a business phone/CRM tool with an AI answering agent "Sona") is a paid ad read, not part of the substantive content, and is not a business idea/tactic itself.
