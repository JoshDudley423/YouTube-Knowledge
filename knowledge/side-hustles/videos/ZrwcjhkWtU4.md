# $250k Profit/Year Print Shop Profit but Feeling Trapped
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZrwcjhkWtU4 | Published: 20240528

Q&A-format episode with three separate callers: Aaron (wants to learn marketing to help a family friend's grill company), Alejandro (wants to help a friend grow her dog-training business), and Daniel (owns a wedding/event print shop in Brooklyn, feels operationally trapped). Organized by caller below.

## Caller 1: Aaron — commission-only marketing role for an $8-figure grill company
- Aaron (ex-mechanical/software engineer, $185K salary, no sales/marketing/e-commerce experience) was approached by a family friend's company that makes a premium ($750-1,000+) USA-made, marine-approved electric grill, trying to expand into new markets (condos where gas grills are banned, RVs). Company: ~50-100 employees, low-eight-figure revenue estimate, currently markets only via trade shows, retail, and basic e-commerce — no direct-response digital marketing.
- Host's framework for evaluating a commission-only sales opportunity: look for a "sweet spot" between ticket size, sales-cycle length, and difficulty — a $1,000 grill with a straightforward buying decision is a good candidate for **direct Google Ads** (estimated cost-per-acquisition ~$40-140, assuming decent margins support paying a commission on top).
- Recommended approach: don't disparage the company's existing (old-school) marketing — offer to test *additional* new channels alongside it. Suggested specific tactics: use a scraping tool (e.g., Outscraper) to build lists of condo association owners/property managers as a B2B outbound target (potentially getting a grill mandated across every unit in a building); use paid keyword-research tools (moz.com or ahrefs.com, ~$50-150/month) to find low-competition/high-intent long-tail search keywords for Google Ads.
- Negotiation advice: since a commission earned via a $40 Google ad spend looks very different from one earned via cold-calling a condo owner directly or paying an influencer per sale, negotiate **separate commission structures for each acquisition channel** up front, rather than a single blanket commission rate. Influencer deals often need some guaranteed payment (not pure rev-share) because top influencers won't take on that much risk themselves.
- Recommended risk management: since this is commission-only, ask the company for a small dedicated ad budget (e.g., a few hundred dollars/month for Google Ads) rather than risking Aaron's own money, framing it explicitly as "I'm learning, but this will cost a little money."
- Practical advice: give yourself a defined 60-day trial window to evaluate whether this opportunity is working, especially since the relationship is with a family friend — protects both sides from resentment if it doesn't pan out. Take a basic Google Ads course (YouTube or Udemy) in the meantime.

## Caller 2: Alejandro — growing a friend's small dog-training business (Dallas-Fort Worth)
- Business context: solo dog trainer, ~$1,500-2,000/month revenue, charges ~$125/session (packages of 4-5 sessions ≈ $500 total), 100% word-of-mouth, no real digital marketing, let her domain name lapse (someone else bought it), inconsistent/minimal Instagram presence, no booking software.
- Host's recommended tactics:
  1. **Document, don't produce**: put a camera on a tripod during every training session; 95% of footage will be unremarkable, but the rare interesting/shareable moment gets posted as short-form content — low effort, ongoing content pipeline.
  2. **Reverse-engineer competitors' viral posts**: identify 5 direct local competitors, look at their historical social posts (including via web archive) and specifically find posts that dramatically outperformed their average engagement (e.g., a post that gets 70 likes/comments vs. their usual 6-7) — then read the *comments*, not just the post, to understand exactly what resonated or what pain point was struck.
  3. Applied example given: if a competitor's dog-training video showing use of a choke collar gets negative comments calling it unethical, that's a signal to build differentiated positioning around "ethical/kind dog training" — directly analogous to how Amazon sellers mine competitors' negative product reviews to find design/positioning improvements to highlight in their own listing.
  4. Domain name: don't over-invest effort trying to reclaim a lapsed domain from a third-party buyer (very hard to negotiate down) — pick a new, available name and move forward (alternative like "AscendDogTrainingTX.com" was available in this case).
  5. Speculative aside from host: no dedicated "marketplace" (an "Uber for dog trainers") seemed to exist in this niche at the time — flagged as a potentially underserved niche-marketplace opportunity worth researching, not confirmed to exist or not.

## Caller 3: Daniel — Brooklyn print shop specializing in wedding/bar mitzvah invitations, feeling trapped
### Business overview and numbers
- Niche: printing specifically for weddings, bar mitzvahs, and similar events — differentiated from generic commercial print shops by stocking event-specific materials (specialty papers like "funny papers," blush pinks, metallic tapes) and understanding what event planners specifically want design-wise; commands a premium markup the way anything wedding-related does.
- ~10.5 years in business; inherited debt-free from his mother-in-law (who ran it originally as a smaller address-printing service) rather than purchased.
- Annual revenue ~$650,000-700,000; personal take-home ~$250,000/year due to high margins.
- Core customer base: about a dozen recurring event/party planners who order multiple times a month, several thousand dollars per order; 3-4 of these are outsized "big fish" accounts.
- No real full-time employees (a sister-in-law helps ~4 hrs/day, 3 days/week on graphic design); Daniel personally works 14-15 hour days, has done so for 2-3 years since losing a strong prior employee (who relocated for cheaper rent post-COVID). Replacement hires have been consistently either overqualified/dismissive of the work's difficulty or underqualified/unreliable.
- Wants to sell the business or acquire a second, more "9-to-5" business to diversify income, but feels stuck in a "messy middle": makes too much to justify hiring full-time help affordably, but not enough slack/margin to comfortably absorb that hire cost, and is the sole irreplaceable operator — making the business currently unsellable (a buyer would just be "buying a job," not a business).

### Host's core diagnosis and advice
- **You can't sell (or scale) an owner-dependent business until you replace yourself** — a buyer offering 3x earnings still won't want to run it personally; a business with no employees beyond the owner has no transferable operational value.
- Any task can be outsourced/delegated, even ones that feel uniquely dependent on the owner ("a graveyard is full of irreplaceable men") — the discomfort of delegating doesn't mean it's actually impossible.
- Diagnosed a "shiny object" pattern (wanting to buy an unrelated new business, e.g., a laundromat, imagining it as more hands-off) as likely premature — "the grass is always greener" — advised fixing the current business's structural/staffing problem before entertaining acquisition of a second one.
- **Market sizing exercise**: ~100,000 party/event planners exist nationally (~3% in NYC by population, conservatively); assuming even ~10 direct competitors doing the same specialized wedding/bar-mitzvah print niche in NYC, that implies roughly 300 potential wholesale (planner) customers per competitor at proportional market share — versus Daniel's current ~12 clients. With ~56,000-60,000 marriage licenses issued annually in NYC alone, host frames this as strong evidence of a large underpenetrated market ("print shop 2.0" opportunity) rather than a maxed-out business needing to be replaced by something new.
- **Pricing**: Daniel had already floated/tested a modest price increase and gotten pushback, but hadn't rigorously measured whether the pushback outweighed the net revenue gain (host's framing: a 20% price increase with only 10% customer attrition is a net win — measure it explicitly rather than going on anecdotal discomfort). Given the small number of concentrated, high-value clients, recommended handling any price change via direct, honest one-on-one conversations with each of the ~12 clients rather than a blanket announcement/email.
- **Referral/viral-loop test that didn't work**: Daniel had tried printing his contact info in small font on the back of wedding materials for ~2 years — got only one response (someone wanting free use of his design work, not a customer). Host's read: doesn't necessarily mean the tactic is worthless — recommends using a dedicated tracking phone number/email for any specific marketing initiative going forward to properly attribute leads, since referral-driven leads can also take months or years to materialize and easily get misattributed.
- **Lead generation "hooks in the water"**: suggested hiring a low-cost overseas cold-caller via Upwork (example: ~$8/hour) to do low-pressure outbound calls to prospective event planners, with a daily report on calls made/interest shown/opt-outs — emphasized needing multiple simultaneous outbound channels ("hooks in the water"), not relying on one method.
- **Cross-marketing/co-op discount idea** (untested by Daniel specifically in this form): offer clients a price discount in exchange for allowing Daniel's branding/business card to be included with the printed materials they distribute to their own guests — analogous to a web designer offering a lower project price in exchange for a footer credit/backlink.
- **Delegation as an immediate unblock**: told Daniel to commit, by end of day, to posting two Upwork jobs — one for a part-time cold caller, one for a bookkeeper (~$300/month estimated) — to start offloading non-owner-critical tasks (bookkeeping/QuickBooks transaction categorization specifically flagged as outsourceable via a $50 Upwork gig with a Loom screen-recording explaining the exact problem).
- **Email marketing as a fast, nearly-free lever**: recommended sending a weekly (not just occasional seasonal) personal email/newsletter to the *entire* contact list (customers and non-customers alike) — framed as a low-effort (~30 min/week), authentic "journal" of what he's working on, wins, struggles, and recent work photos — rationale: referral-based businesses often fail to stay "top of mind," and consistent, personal outreach compounds over time even to non-active contacts.
- **Reframe on "escaping" the business**: host's closing point — Daniel may not actually need or want a second business; he may just need to fix his current one (staffing, pricing, marketing systems) to the point where the original urge to sell/replace it disappears. Advised him to resolve that core question before pursuing an unrelated acquisition.

## Caveats / risks
- This episode is call-in Q&A advice specific to each caller's individual situation, not general how-to guidance for starting any of these business types from scratch.
- Grill company opportunity for Aaron is explicitly speculative/commission-based with no guaranteed income; host frames the downside as limited mainly to time invested (a family-friend relationship reduces some risk).
- Daniel's core structural problem (owner-dependency, family-only staffing history creating discipline/process friction) is presented as the central risk to resolve before any further business development (selling, scaling, or acquiring) makes sense.
