# You Don't Need to Be a Genius! Just Do This.
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7rhoNM9IGE0 | Published: 2025-05-05

Podcast conversation (Chris + guest "Sam") covering: selling a side-project app via Twitter DMs, why "make money online"-style info products (courses, niche PDFs) actually sell and why refund rates stay low, how to discover a profitable offer by listening to customers, "steal the offer" competitive research tactics, the rucking/niche-fitness-info-product market as a case study, and building a custom no-code productivity tool with Lovable in 2 hours.

## Deal 1: Selling a stalled side-project app peer-to-peer over Twitter DMs
- Guest built an AI cooking app (paid ~$1,000 to have it built) but let it sit unused for 3 weeks because he didn't have bandwidth to run growth/ads on it.
- Found a buyer inside his own paid community ("Grow Faster") who was already running a different consumer app (a day-trading journaling app) and wanted to learn the same ad-growth playbook.
- Deal structure: sold the app for the same $1,000 he'd paid to build it, PLUS kept 20% of future revenue, PLUS will personally run/teach the ad strategy for it. Buyer handles all App Store logistics/headaches; seller only runs ads. Framed as a "free roll" (no cash risk) for the seller.
- Entire transaction — deal terms, hand-off, ongoing coordination — was conducted through Twitter DMs, no formal contract process, "we sent one email."
- Lesson emphasized: momentum and follow-through matter more than the specific idea; an unused/stalled asset can still be monetized via a quick opportunistic deal rather than being abandoned entirely.

## Idea discussed but flagged with churn risk: "AI business co-founder" subscription app
- Concept: upload your LinkedIn, complete a personality assessment (introvert/extrovert, sales/writing experience, etc.), the app recommends 5 business ideas tailored to you, then acts as an ongoing accountability partner/scheduler (day-1 task: buy a domain, etc.), $10/month.
- Host's critique: as a recurring/monthly "make money online"-style offer, churn would be severe — most buyers get excited in the moment of purchase but stop doing the actual work within days ("flame out on day 3"), then blame the product ("this AI co-founder didn't make me a million dollars") rather than their own lack of follow-through.
- Suggested fix: charge a one-time lifetime price instead of monthly (though this doesn't eliminate the underlying behavior problem, just changes the revenue/refund dynamics).
- General insight on "scammy" course-seller reputations: most complaints stem from buyers never completing the material (host cites people completing "6%" of a program) or buyer's remorse for not doing the work — not necessarily fraud. Course sellers are financially incentivized to price high upfront and capture buyers "in the moment" of excitement, since that's when purchase intent peaks.
- Host's own data points: his $3,000 tree-business bootcamp and $6,000 RV-park course each have zero refund requests despite only a minority of buyers ever actually starting the underlying business — buyers report being satisfied regardless, and some repeat-purchase his other courses on trust ("it's Chris's, it'll be good"). His earlier $300 "Launch OS" course (~100 buyers) produced only 3 people who actually launched a business through it — but he notes those 3 people likely would have succeeded anyway; the course just caught them at the right moment, not that it caused the outcome.

## How to find a sellable offer: listen to what customers actually ask for, then build/quote it on the spot
- Cited example: on a sales call for their content/SEO agency ("Unlimited Content" — normal offer: 30 articles in 30 days), a lead with 3,000 existing blog posts didn't need more content, he needed backlinks. They improvised an entirely new $500/month "backlinks only, we manage everything, you set the budget" offer on the call and closed it immediately — the offer wasn't planned in advance, it was reverse-engineered from what the market was asking for.
- General principle stated repeatedly: "leads are the lifeblood" — get on the phone with real prospects and let them tell you what they'll pay for, rather than guessing/theorizing about pricing or packaging in isolation.
- Cited example from a friend who consults with trade schools (e.g., HVAC licensing programs): standard sales calls in that industry run 2-3 minutes ("here's the cost, here's the duration, yes or no"); his "secret sauce" upgrade is a 90-minute discovery-heavy call where he only asks questions to learn exactly what the prospect wants/needs, then builds the pitch entirely around their own stated answers — reported to increase close rate by ~80%.

## Competitive research shortcut: "steal the offer" via Wayback Machine + Facebook Ads Library
- To find a proven offer/pricing structure for a new niche (example given: a Google Business Profile optimization agency), identify 5-10 competitors already operating and charging in that space, then look up their site history on the Wayback/web archive (archive.org).
- You don't need to trace the full iteration history — just look at their current homepage. Any business that has iterated its offer/site over time (adding testimonials, changing pricing structure, etc.) has effectively already done the market research; their current, most-optimized offer is the one most likely to convert. Copy it directly rather than reinventing it.
- Same logic applied to ad creative: use the Facebook Ads Library to see what your direct competitors are currently running as ads and adapt/swap the creative rather than starting from scratch — cuts the creative learning curve significantly.

## Case study: digital info-product economics in a niche fitness trend (rucking)
- "Rucking" (walking with a weighted vest/backpack) cited as a still-under-the-radar fitness trend (guest noted people his age at church hadn't heard of it) that nonetheless supports paid digital products.
- Example site named: pathfinderrucktraining.com — sells structured rucking training programs for $150-250 (guest suspects AI-generated content), getting "tens of thousands of hits a month," despite the underlying activity being trivially learnable for free (buy a weighted vest on Amazon, start walking).
- General insight: people aren't paying for the raw information (which is often freely available), they're paying for the shortcut/structure/handholding from point A to point B. Parallel examples cited: a "Dunk Now" jump-training program (mailed a physical poster of workouts for ~$150 in the pre-internet era) and a mini-course teaching handstands that reportedly did ~$500,000 in sales.
- Framed as a durable pattern ("people will always pay for the shortcut") applicable to nearly any activity/skill niche (named explicitly: cookbooks as a classic point-A-to-point-B product), now made cheap and fast to produce with AI ("takes two minutes to create something valuable" — competitive advantage shifts to marketing execution, "may the best marketer win").
- Cautionary tale referenced: an Australian wellness influencer (implied: Belle Gibson) who claimed to cure her own cancer with whole foods, built a health app (one of the first in the App Store, promoted by Apple, reportedly tens of millions of dollars in app + sold-out physical cookbook revenue) before being exposed as a total fraud — illustrates how compelling narrative/marketing can outsell any concern about legitimacy in the info-product space, though obviously an extreme/unethical example, not a recommended template.
- Discussion (unresolved) on whether AI-generated influencers/personas will keep proliferating to sell such products, or whether platforms (Meta) will crack down — speculation that platforms are incentivized to allow whatever content keeps users engaged/on-platform, regardless of authenticity, as long as it doesn't cross into clear impersonation.

## Tangent: building a custom productivity/task-management tool with Lovable (AI/no-code) in ~2 hours
- Guest, dissatisfied with every existing task manager (Asana, Trello, Notion, etc. — "none of them do exactly what I want"), built a personal Kanban-style task tracker from scratch using Lovable in about 2 hours, with no coding background.
- Features built: task title, quick notes, estimated time to complete, priority level (color-coded: red/orange/yellow), a company/project tag, due date (color-coded by urgency), and a built-in timer that tracks actual time spent per task once started (Pomodoro-style) — intended to eventually build toward a personal "revenue per hour by company/project" dashboard by integrating Stripe and Google Calendar data.
- Explicitly resisted the urge to immediately turn it into a SaaS product to sell (a joke about how "vibe coding" projects tempt everyone to think "billion dollar business"), framing it purely as a personal tool, though he floated trying to share the Lovable project with the host to test if others could easily adapt it (tags were hardcoded rather than user-configurable, a known limitation).
- Lessons on using AI/no-code app builders like Lovable effectively: think in terms of the three components of any app — the data/backend structure, the manipulation/logic layer, and the front-end display — and get the data structure right early (e.g., if you want a numeric sum of "estimated time" across tasks, that field must be stored as a structured number, not free text). Don't over-engineer prompts with "code-like" instructions; plain natural-language requests work well since Lovable is fairly intuitive. When it makes an error, just tell it to fix it; if a fix attempt goes sideways, restore to a previous version and re-prompt with a clearer request rather than continuing to patch a broken state.

## Caveats
- Selling a stalled app for cost + rev share worked because of an existing trusted community relationship — not necessarily replicable as a formal marketplace process.
- Info-product/course businesses can look "scammy" from the outside, but the host argues the core issue is buyer follow-through, not necessarily fraudulent selling — still, this is the seller's own framing and should be weighed against the survivorship-bias risk that many buyers never see meaningful outcomes.
- The Wayback Machine / Ads Library "copy the optimized offer" tactic assumes competitors' current state actually reflects genuine optimization (via real testing) rather than inertia; it's a starting point/shortcut, not a guarantee of fit for your own business.
