# Deep Dive on the Gas Delivery Business. HOLY PROFITS!
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dc_tVmO4SSs | Published: 2024-11-14

Interview with Korey McDavid, founder of Juiced Fuel, a mobile fuel (gas/diesel) delivery business in Charleston, SC, serving residential customers, boats, and fleets.

## The business idea
- Mobile fuel delivery: drivers with truck-mounted fuel tanks deliver gas/diesel directly to customers' vehicles, boats, generators, or equipment (scheduled via an app) instead of customers visiting a gas station.
- Idea originated from founder's personal pain point (wife hated gas station trips with young kids) combined with his day job as a terminal manager at a bulk liquids fuel terminal, where he noticed no one served small businesses, boats, or individual consumers with delivered fuel (only large-volume commercial accounts were served by existing suppliers).
- Confirmed as a broader national trend before launching (found other companies doing similar things in other cities) but no one was doing it in Charleston the way he envisioned. Notes competitors existing elsewhere didn't deter him — "plenty of room for growth and competition," market not zero-sum locally.

## Timeline and launch
- ~2 years from initial idea to first delivery (December 2022 first gallons delivered; "beginning of 2023" treated as real month-one); the gap included a career change and general life delay, not continuous planning.
- Ran as a side business alongside his full-time remote day job for the entire first year (him + spare time only); brought on his brother full-time after year one to handle deliveries, allowing founder to stay in his day job and focus on growth instead of driving the truck. Still has his day job at time of interview (~2 years in).

## Startup capital and assets
- Built the app FIRST, before buying any physical equipment — used an "incubator" program that helped build an MVP version of the app (very limited scope initially — couldn't even take payment). Founder found a bug/backdoor in the MVP, used it to identify and directly recruit the app's actual developer to keep building with him going forward (still working together).
- Used the working (if limited) app demo to raise a $100,000 friends-and-family round.
- Asset costs (all-in, under $100K to reach revenue-generating operation):
  - Used delivery truck: ~$40,000
  - Fuel "skid" (the tank/pump/metering equipment that drops into a truck bed): ~$20,000; current skid config holds 400 gallons across four separate 100-gallon compartments (~2,600 lbs fully loaded)
  - App development: ~$10,000-$15,000
- App functionality: customers register vehicle/equipment info (including non-vehicle assets like generators, boats, excavators — with a free-text notes field for gate codes, special access instructions), submit fuel requests, a driver claims the request, push notifications track status (accepted → en route → complete), in-app payment and invoice.

## Customer acquisition
- Pre-launch validation: ran Facebook ads driving to a landing page for a business that didn't exist yet, to gauge appetite/click-through rate; used that CTR data to build a 3-year revenue model (founder says the actual results were "way off" from that model, but it was directionally useful enough to justify investing).
- Post-launch: resumed the same Facebook ads (now that the service was real) plus organic word-of-mouth; got early "stranger" (non-network) customers this way.
- Key customer-behavior challenge identified: prospects like the concept in the abstract ("oh that's great") but don't sign up because they "don't need gas right now" — required ongoing customer education to shift the habit from reactive (wait until near-empty) to proactive (schedule ahead) fuel purchasing, since the app isn't built for same-hour emergency turnaround.
- Was deliberately conservative about growth pace early on ("we were really careful about not growing too quickly") to protect customer trust/experience while the business was still a side hustle for the founder.

## Pricing, margins, unit economics
- Subscription model: $24/month covers 2 vehicles, +$5/month per additional vehicle.
- Fuel price benchmarked to the lowest price within 3 miles (though the company was, as of the interview, moving away from this dynamic-match approach toward a fixed price model, because third-party gas price data sources like GasBuddy/Waze are unreliable — outdated, "fat-fingered," and ambiguous about cash vs. credit pricing).
- Fuel margin: ~15-20 cents/gallon typical, targeting 30 cents/gallon; explicitly low margin because gas is a commodity and customers have easy access to alternative gas stations — the differentiator has to be the low-overhead delivery convenience, not price.
- Average household spend: ~$2,500/year per vehicle; a two-vehicle family averages ~$5,000/year; typical refill cadence is every 7-10 days (3-4 refills/month per family).
- At a ~15-20% overall margin on subscriber spend, founder notes the residential-only line alone was "not going to be viable long term" — pushed expansion into higher-margin, higher-ticket segments: fleet delivery and (especially) marine/boat fuel delivery.
- Churn: described as "almost no churn" — customers who sign up tend to stick even if usage is infrequent, partly because pricing is low enough that some keep it just as an emergency/convenience backstop (e.g., bad weather days, giving a teenager access to fuel without a credit card).
- Revenue trajectory: $350,000 in year 2 (interview time), up ~500% from year one.

## Business lines and expansion strategy
- Three concurrent business lines at time of interview: residential/personal vehicles, commercial fleets, and marine (boats) — run by a team of 4.
- Marine identified as the standout opportunity ("our bread and butter") — no local competitor offers dockside bulk fuel delivery at that volume, avoiding the need for a boat to leave its dock to refuel.
- With hindsight, founder says he would have started with boat/marine delivery first, not residential — in his market it was a bigger unmet need with an easier sale (getting fuel to a boat is expensive/inconvenient by comparison), so conversion would have been simpler.
- Planned future direction: franchising / owner-operator model for geographic expansion, reasoning that fuel pricing and trust are hyper-local, so a franchise needs a local operator embedded in the community (draws an analogy to an over-the-road truck driver, who might earn $80-100K driving, being able to run this as a local business instead with more upside). Also considering eventual vertical integration into wholesale fuel supply once enough volume is reached (leveraging founder's industry background), and floated a longer-term idea of the app becoming a broader mobile-services marketplace (e.g., adding oil changes, tire service) once it has a recurring engaged user base checking in for fuel requests.
- Growth partly organic/viral via transparent day-in-the-life social media content (has inspired copycats and requests from people wanting to franchise/replicate "Juice" in their own city).

## Caveats
- Fuel delivery is inherently a low-margin, commodity business on the fuel itself — the business model depends on stacking recurring subscription revenue and keeping overhead low rather than margin on gas.
- Same-day/emergency fulfillment is not realistic with this model — a real behavior-change/education burden exists to get customers to request fuel proactively rather than when nearly empty.
- Fuel delivery pricing is regionally idiosyncratic (e.g., Charleston has no pipeline access and receives fuel by ship, unlike inland markets on a pipeline) — a launch playbook can't assume uniform economics across markets.
- Note: not applicable to EV owners (explicitly raised by the interviewer) — company was, at time of interview, only in early "diligence"/fundraising-stage discussions about adding EV charging or other mobile services, with no committed timeline.
