# How to Make Millions Teaching Kids How To Use Heavy Machinery
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TdhOk1-BGNw | Published: 2024-06-20

Interview with Jacob, co-founder of Dig World, a theme park where kids (and adults) operate real, modified excavators and other heavy construction equipment. Located on leased land adjacent to a mall in the Houston, TX area. Deep dive into the business model, unit economics, and lessons from launching a novel physical attraction business.

## Concept and origin
- Founder background: started a commercial cleaning company in 2015 with mentor-provided capital (still operating, ~400 employees) before Dig World. Personal motivation for Dig World: his son suffered bacterial meningitis at age 2 (permanent disability) and loved construction equipment; wanted to create a place that "brings people together" across generations around a shared experience.
- Business model precedent: a larger, all-day theme-park-style version of this concept has existed in New Jersey since 2014 (30+ acres, full food and beverage) -- used as a reference point when fundraising ("they've been doing this and are still in business"), though Dig World's own smaller, mall-adjacent model and safety/control technology were built from scratch with no direct template.
- Fundraising: took from 2019 (concept) to March 2022 (launch) -- about 3 years -- and raised roughly $3 million total. Investor objections centered on the "crazy idea" of letting toddlers operate excavators, plus COVID hitting during the fundraising period.

## Location strategy
- Leases 3.5 acres of land from an adjacent shopping mall (land had been vacant/grass since 1999) -- described as a highly deliberate choice for "baked-in traffic": the specific mall draws ~12 million visitors/year.
- Caveat acknowledged: while many malls are declining, the founder argues the best-located malls in growing areas will always retain demand (analogized to how the best retail locations will always support certain business types, e.g., ice cream/cookie shops, regardless of broader category decline).
- Buildings: main office/retail building built from modular units stacked together (like a job-site trailer, thematically fitting); restrooms built from repurposed shipping containers to keep costs down. Zoning/permitting was more complex than typical because officials aren't used to approving "temporary-style" buildings (like job-site trailers) as permanent structures.
- Notably, despite the mall adjacency being a deliberate traffic strategy, the founder later found that direct mall-to-park foot traffic conversion is low -- the mall's real value is that people are already driving to that part of town, not walkover visits.

## Equipment and safety technology
- Roughly 25-30 pieces of real, new Caterpillar-brand heavy machinery purchased outright (not leased, not refurbished/decommissioned units) -- fully functional, job-site-capable equipment.
- Custom-built proprietary control/safety technology restricts each machine's real-world function for park use: e.g., excavators are limited to certain rotation angles, cannot move forward/backward (tracks disabled), and run on a timer that cuts hydraulic function (but not the engine) when a rider's turn ends, avoiding the need for staff to physically remove children. Override/kill switches and reversion to "real machine mode" exist for staff use in park maintenance.
- Built by a dedicated in-house/contracted engineer; framed as a genuinely difficult technical challenge because modern heavy equipment is computer-controlled and required essentially "rewriting the computer in the computer."

## Revenue lines and pricing
- General admission: $24.99 for a 2-hour session, all-inclusive of unlimited machine rides within that window (deliberately not itemized/nickel-and-dimed per ride) -- priced based on market research against a nearby water park and family entertainment venues.
- Contrast cited: a competing/newer facility in Tennessee charges $55 (kids)/$70 (adults) for an all-day, bigger-park, more food-and-beverage-heavy model -- different positioning entirely.
- Every visitor, including non-riding accompanying adults, must buy a ticket -- a recurring customer complaint ("I'm just watching") the founder rejects by comparing it to water parks and children's museums, which use the same policy.
- Gem mining station: a bag-based add-on retail activity, believed (not yet precisely measured due to POS system limitations at time of interview) to be purchased by the majority of visiting parties -- described as a significant, unplanned revenue driver ("an asymmetric bet" a co-founder pushed for based on conference learnings, not originally underwritten into the model).
- Retail/merch: ~1,200 sq ft merchandise store that visitors are funneled through on the way out (toys, books, hats, shirts, flashlights, backpacks) -- some customer friction/complaints about the forced walkthrough, offset by it being air-conditioned relief from Texas heat.
- Food and beverage: intentionally limited to prepackaged items only (chips, candy, bottled drinks, ice cream) -- explicitly avoiding becoming a full food operation (hot dogs/burgers) to keep the model simple; visit length is capped at ~2 hours by design to maximize throughput/turnover.
- Field trips: heavily discounted tickets ($4-8/child) sold in bulk (hundreds of kids at once) during weekday closures (park is open to the public only Saturday/Sunday during the school year) -- high margin due to lower labor needs per visitor and fills otherwise-dead weekday capacity. Growth challenge: schools have entrenched annual field-trip traditions (e.g., always going to the zoo) that are hard to displace; growth here has been slower than initially projected, driven by a mix of organic interest (teachers who visited on weekends) and active outreach.
- Marketing tactic for field trip growth: giving free tickets to first/second graders around major holidays (Christmas, spring break, summer) on the theory that a free ticket for one child brings a family of 3-4 paying visitors ("park math").
- Partnership/sponsorship revenue and in-kind value: Caterpillar and local dealer Mustang Cat are paid sponsors (plus negotiated equipment discounts from bulk purchasing) in exchange for brand visibility throughout the park; Texas A&M University provided free educational curriculum content (signage, field trip materials) in exchange for exposure that supports its own goal of recruiting students into construction-related careers.

## Costs
- Primary startup cost was construction of the park itself, more than the machinery (which was financed/underwritten through equipment financing routes); equipment purchases were also complicated by supply shortages during the tail end of COVID, limiting negotiating leverage at the time.
- Insurance: costs described as "a lot" but proportional to other attraction-style businesses (theme parks, water parks, rodeos, carnivals); estimated at roughly 8-10% of a monthly figure (exact base unclear/redacted in transcript) as a cost line. Found a specialty insurance carrier already experienced underwriting theme parks/rodeos rather than a generalist reluctant to take the risk.
- Staffing: 2 full-time employees (general manager, events coordinator) plus roughly 55-60 part-time/weekend staff on the roster, with 18-25 working any given Saturday/Sunday shift, covering attraction operation, birthday party coordination, admissions, and social media.

## Traffic and growth
- Attendance target: 45,000-50,000 guests annualized at time of interview; facility could handle up to ~75,000 at current capacity/days open, and would need added weekday hours and more machines to push toward 100,000.
- A good day is 450-475 guests; the highest single-day attendance recorded was ~800.
- Primary marketing channel: paid and organic influencer/creator content showing kids operating real machinery -- described as unexpectedly the biggest driver of traffic, more than reliance on the mall's foot traffic. Founder specifically flags that novelty/surprise value ("wait, a 4-year-old is driving an excavator?") is what makes content spread, and that founders can become numb to how compelling their own product looks to outsiders.
- Launch mistake: opened the park 48 hours before it was actually ready (not enough machines had the safety software installed yet), causing severe overcrowding/long lines on opening day; the park was closed again the same night to finish setup -- resulted in negative press coverage of the "grand closing" that outpaced coverage of the eventual real opening, creating a lingering public misperception that the park had shut down permanently.

## Handling criticism / online skepticism
- Recurring negative comment themes on social posts: insurance/liability concerns, safety questions, and pricing complaints -- framed by both Chris and Jacob as a predictable pattern for any novel business concept, and one that can actually help algorithmic distribution (controversy/engagement drives wider reach), citing a parallel example of a pressure-washing franchise getting comments questioning the legality of using bleach (which is legal in all 50 states).
- Guidance on which criticism to weigal ignoring internet strangers' uninformed objections but taking seriously feedback from people who actually understand the specific industry.
- Real complaint volume cited as low relative to visitor volume: roughly 3 negative reviews per 5,000-8,000 monthly visitors -- argued as evidence that a small vocal minority shouldn't drive product/pricing decisions ("the silent majority never speaks up").

## Future plans
- Actively preparing to launch a franchise model (targeted within the same calendar year as the interview) to expand the concept to other cities/operators.

## Caveats
- Figures are self-reported in an interview, not audited; percentage/dollar specifics on some items (e.g., exact insurance cost base, gem-mining attach rate) were explicitly noted by the founder as imprecise due to limited point-of-sale data granularity at the time.
- Regulatory/zoning and insurance underwriting for this kind of business are described as unusually difficult to arrange (required finding a specialty carrier and navigating non-standard permanent-building zoning) -- not a plug-and-play business to replicate without that groundwork.
