# How to Get Your First $1,000 per Month Agency Customer
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ekWRhMEjgx8 | Published: 2024-09-02

Interview with Brandon Doyle (Walaroo Media / Arvo agencies, running agencies since 2008).

## Business ideas discussed
- **Niche agency ideas Brandon says he'd start today** (if barred from repeating past work):
  - Email marketing (cold outbound, not managing existing lists) for a **high-ticket niche**: car dealerships, or **B2B e-commerce** — described as a massive, underserved market (e.g., ads/email for a furniture manufacturer selling in bulk to restaurant distributors, not consumers).
  - Rationale: B2B has much lower CAC and far higher LTV than consumer e-com — once you're an "approved supplier," you keep the account, whereas consumer brands must resell the customer every purchase.
- **"AI-operator" agency model**: position yourself as the person who "pushes the AI button" for clients who don't have time to learn the tools themselves. Be transparent that you use AI (ChatGPT, Midjourney, Runway ML, etc.) for content/image/video production — this signals lower cost and higher output without you needing to say "we're cheaper."
  - Pitch: charge less than the incumbent agency (e.g., $2K vs. their $4K) while delivering *more volume* of the same quality (e.g., emails/month).

## Numbers
- Brandon's first-ever paid client (2009): **$150/month** for ~20 hours of work/month (well below minimum wage). Market rate for equivalent service at the time was **5-6x higher** ($750-$1,200/mo).
- He deliberately raised each subsequent new client's price ($150 → $200 → $250...) until he started hitting "no," then held there for a while.
- Agency revenue history: Walaroo did ~$1.5M in the 12 months before first spinning off sub-agencies. Spun into 7 agencies (ads, email, SEO, web design, social, a smaller ads unit, and NIL deals for college athletes) — none scaled past ~$3M/year combined in a single year at peak; most ran only 5-12% net margins because none reached scale. Later consolidated back to 2 agencies (Walaroo = paid ads, Arvo = SEO/email/CRO), which now do ~$8M+/year combined at roughly 50/50 revenue split.
- Facebook ad/e-commerce heyday example: Casper mattress — customer acquisition cost was roughly **$100 to sell a $1,000 mattress** at 70-80% gross margin, in the "golden years." CAC has since risen sharply and margins compressed for most DTC brands (ad costs up significantly since ~2015/2016).
- Example agency pricing escalation tactic: start a 3-month contract at $3K/month delivering (say) 5 emails/month vs. competitor's $4K for 4 emails — clearly more value. By month 13, ramp output to ~19 emails/month while raising price ~80% ($3K→~$5.4K); client accepts because delivered value scaled faster than price.

## Process / steps described
- **Getting first clients without cold outbound**: Brandon's agency has never done outbound — grew via SEO (they rank well for search terms), referrals, and manual direct outreach (DMing/emailing founders of specific brands he wanted to work with, offering free strategy ideas up front with no obligation to hire).
- **Client retention framework** ("comms x results matrix"): if results are bad but communication is great, you can buy enough time to fix results and keep the client. If results are great but communication is bad, you'll likely still lose the client. Conclusion: over-communicate, even to the point of mild annoyance — share small wins, personal interest in client's industry, frequent updates.
- Prefers taking clients away from **competing agencies** over first-time-to-paid-ads clients, because switchers already understand agency dynamics/expectations (parallel drawn to 3PL/logistics clients).
- Early SEO growth tactics (2008-2010, no longer effective): registering on directory sites (Yelp-like, ~200 of them) for "white hat" backlinks; blog comment links with custom anchor text; spinning up near-duplicate landing pages per city/school (now much faster with AI, but Google penalizes duplicate content today); early platform arbitrage — being first mover on new ad platforms (Facebook ads for e-commerce ~2015-17, first official Snapchat agency partner, first TikTok agency) drove outsized results before those channels got competitive/expensive.

## Tools/platforms named
Reddit and forums (as early self-education source), Stumble Upon (defunct growth channel), Facebook/Instagram/Snapchat/TikTok/Twitter ads, ChatGPT, Midjourney, Runway ML (for AI content production), Google PPC.

## Caveats / risks
- Getting an agency client today is "harder than ever" — sales skill is now a prerequisite, not optional; charisma and/or persistence required.
- Diversifying ad channels too early is a mistake below ~9-figure revenue — recommends ~80% focus on 1-2 channels you've mastered, 20% testing a potential third, rather than spreading thin.
- E-commerce/DTC brands that scaled via Facebook ads in the 2016-2020 window (Casper, Allbirds, Figs) mostly did not stay profitable post-IPO: rising ad costs, margin compression, and copycat competition eroded the moat. Only exception cited: Hims, because it sells a recurring/consumable product, not a one-time durable good.
- Running 7 agencies simultaneously (vs. staying focused on 1-2) meant missing the best years of the core Facebook-ads/e-commerce wave — Brandon estimates the company would be 2-3x its current size had it stayed focused instead of chasing "shiny object" spinoffs.
- Attempts to launch in-house e-commerce brands (as an agency owner) repeatedly failed because staff/agency incentives don't align with multi-year equity payoffs; only started working once treated as a normal paying client of the agency's own services rather than a side passion project.
- If you build an "AI tool" product (not an agency), you risk being leapfrogged in ~6 months as underlying models (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) improve — only viable if you have the focus/resources to continuously stay ahead; otherwise, better to be a service/agency built *on top of* the best available tools rather than build the tool itself.
