# Thinking About Dropshipping? You Might Be Too Late.
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7opaNBRPBYw | Published: 2025-03-26

Podcast conversation (Chris + guest "Connor," an e-commerce operator) on why classic Alibaba-to-Shopify dropshipping is largely played out, which e-commerce categories are still promising in 2025, a reframe on "is it too late" for any given business, and several AI-agent/lead-gen business ideas.

## Core claim: dropshipping (Alibaba → Shopify, low-margin knockoff goods) is oversaturated
- In ~2012, near-any generic low-margin product could be profitably dropshipped with paid ads even if the product itself was mediocre.
- That "arbitrage growth hack" got discovered/copied, ad CPMs rose, and margins collapsed because the dropshipper doesn't own/manufacture the product — framed generally: "the more effective a growth hack, the shorter its lifespan."
- Distinguishes this specific tactic (arbitrage dropshipping of cheap knockoffs) from e-commerce broadly, which is NOT too late — only ~16 cents of every consumer dollar is spent online, so e-commerce as a whole is still "early innings."

## 3 e-commerce categories the guest is most bullish on for 2025
1. **Premium/luxury** — brand and mission loyalty let you charge more for an equivalent underlying product; harder for low-cost Chinese competitors (Temu, Shein-style) to undercut on brand equity even if tariffs shift cost dynamics.
2. **Custom products** — hard to compete against on pure retail/price; also substantially reduces reverse-logistics/returns costs.
3. **Food and beverage** — lower return rates than categories like apparel, consumable (repeat purchase), and less exposed to Chinese manufacturing competition.
   - Caveat/refinement from host (who ran a company, "SendEats," shipping food for other Shopify brands): the food should ideally be under 16 oz (fits USPS First Class), shelf-stable (non-perishable, non-refrigerated), and higher price point to justify shipping cost. Example of what does NOT work economically: shipping ice cream or meat pies, or refrigerated/dry-ice items — host cited working with well-known grocery-store brands doing only ~50 orders/month via this channel because unit economics didn't work; contrasted with ButcherBox, a bootstrapped meat-subscription brand that scaled to a nine-figure business by pricing high enough to absorb dry-ice/refrigerated shipping costs.

## Framework: "too late" is relative and usually wrong
- Analogy: the 2008 iPhone App Store gold rush felt "finite" (someone sold a literal "fart app" for $1M in 2009 believing the window was closing) — 18 years later, launching an app is still a viable business, now easier with AI, despite millions of existing apps; you only know you were "late" in hindsight once the opportunity is fully over.
- Applies the same logic to e-commerce, Amazon, Bitcoin, YouTube channels, Shopify stores: all still considered "early" as long as the underlying trend (online spending share, crypto adoption, etc.) keeps growing — you're only definitively "late" once the trend has ended.
- Roofing companies used as an example of "supply looks saturated" (~10,000 roofing companies already exist in the US) but is actually still exploitable: most roofing businesses still use paper ledgers, require in-person quotes, don't use Google Maps for remote measurement, don't use AI/county records to look up permit history for a property's existing roof, and can't/won't use AI assistants instead of a $75,000/year human assistant — meaning a modernized, AI-tooled entrant can compete even in a "saturated" niche by out-executing on process, not by being first.
- Suggested validation method for whether a "late" market is actually easy to enter: cold-call ~30 businesses in the target niche and ~30 each in two adjacent/tangential niches (their example: roofing, landscaping, pool installers — 90 total calls). If a large share simply don't answer their phones, that signals either poor service or being overwhelmed with demand — both indicate room for a better-run competitor, regardless of how "saturated" the market looks on paper.

## AI agent / lead-gen business ideas discussed
- **"Reverse engineer this business" agent** (building on an idea from a related episode about Shopify/YouTube milestone plaques): an agent that scans social media (Twitter, Reddit, Instagram, etc.) via image recognition for people posting photos of new business milestones (Shopify $1M order plaque, YouTube 100k-subscriber plaque), then triggers a research agent that investigates that business/channel's history (age, video output/cadence, growth rate) and emails the owner (or a subscriber) a report on exactly how to replicate the growth.
- **Milestone-based lead generation**: reframes fundraising-announcement lead gen (a common, easily-scraped high-intent signal, since funding rounds are public via press-release wires) by asking what "hard to get" milestone signals exist that AREN'T already publicly wired/scraped — e.g., scraping Instagram for engagement-ring/wedding announcement photos to generate warm leads for wedding planning services, or scraping for the moment a YouTube channel crosses 100,000 subscribers to sell services to fast-growing creators.
- **AI video understanding for content research**: demonstrated that OpenAI's "Operator" agent (browsing a live Instagram Reels feed) could correctly describe what was literally happening in a silent 15-second video (identifying pool resurfacing/tar work) by analyzing it frame-by-frame, whereas uploading the same downloaded video file directly to ChatGPT-4o (without transcript/audio) failed to describe the actual visual content. Practical use: automating "watch and bookmark high-view (500k+) business-idea Reels" as an overnight background research task, freeing up a human assistant (mentioned: an assistant in the Philippines) for higher-value work like video editing.
- **Distribution/moat discussion**: argues content/social media (and specifically Facebook ads, since anyone can create an ad account with no existing audience) is currently the dominant distribution lever; speculates that as more sellers pile onto Facebook ads, click-through/engagement rates could saturate and decline, pushing marketers toward being more willing to invest in direct-to-consumer channels (email, SMS, phone) or human-centric community-building as the "next lever," particularly since B2C businesses currently rarely do outbound sales/BDR (it's traditionally viewed as unprofitable at typical consumer price points, except in ultra-luxury).
- **Faceless YouTube channel caution**: argues that as AI makes it trivial to auto-generate polished "faceless" content channels (fast editing, trending audio, breaking-news style), genuine human-face-forward creators will retain more durable differentiation and audience loyalty than faceless AI-style channels, which risk becoming commoditized. Counter-argument raised (unresolved): audiences may ultimately care more about entertainment quality/story than about whether the creator/actor is a real human (analogy: fully-CGI films like Avatar succeeding despite no "real" underlying visuals) — framed as different content styles likely coexisting rather than one fully replacing the other (compared to acoustic vs. electric music, or animation vs. live action).

## Caveats
- The "is it too late" framing is explicitly presented as a mental model/heuristic, not a guarantee — the guest still concedes that a saturated niche means a new entrant will only marginally affect pricing and won't automatically win just by entering.
- Tariff policy changes (mentioned as being implemented around the recording date) could shift the competitive landscape for Chinese-manufactured cheap goods (Temu/Shein-style competitors), in an unpredictable direction/duration.
- The wedding-photo/Instagram lead-scraping idea is explicitly framed as a hypothetical/thought experiment ("intangible... hard to get data"), not a built or tested system.
