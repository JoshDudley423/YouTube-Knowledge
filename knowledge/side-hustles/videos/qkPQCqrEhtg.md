# Forget Snack Machines. This One Refills Itself
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qkPQCqrEhtg | Published: 2026-09-14

On-location interview with CJ Lovas (Lovas Realty DFW) in Fort Worth, TX, who runs a small ice/water vending machine business (bagged ice + filtered water vending machines) alongside his real estate business, with zero full-time employees.

## The business
- Origin: previously owned DFW Pool Services (7 trucks, 3 vans); was spending ~$2,000/month buying ice/water/drinks for crew hydration, and looked for a way to turn that cost center into a profit center — led him into owning ice/water vending machines instead.
- Machine types: smaller "Everest" brand water/ice vending stations (~550 lb ice capacity) up to large "Ice House America"-style machines (~1,500-6,500 lb ice capacity, described as "basically a house" — moving one is a major logistical undertaking, unlike smaller cooler-ice units which can be relocated for ~$1,000).
- Currently operates 4 locations, no full-time employees; uses one part-time maintenance tech (~$600/month, up to ~$1,000 in busier periods) to handle jams, cleaning, and part repairs across all machines.
- Machines are internet/app-connected: owner can remotely reset the machine, dispense free water/vend from his phone, and monitor per-machine transaction counters; only physical coin jams require an in-person visit.

## Concrete numbers
- Retail pricing: $2 for 10 lb of ice, $3.50 for 20 lb — compared to convenience stores/CVS charging ~$4 for 7 lb (roughly 3x more ice for the same or less money).
- Cost of goods: ice bag costs ~$0.07-$0.10; all-in cost per bag (including water/utilities) estimated at up to $0.10-$0.15 max at volume — yields roughly 90% gross margins on ice sales.
- Startup cost to get into the business at a bare-minimum level: $500-$2,500 (can pick up a used/distressed small machine for as little as $500, needing only minor part repairs).
- New machine cost: $150,000-$500,000 for a large ice/water vending structure; buying new also requires city permitting, code compliance (e.g., brick enclosure requirements), and can take 6-9 months (best case 60-90 days) before it's operational — versus buying an existing, already-sited machine, which comes with an established customer base and can be cash-flowing within days (one example: a newly acquired existing location did $1,000-$1,500 in sales in a single Saturday during its first week of new ownership).
- Typical machine location lease costs: $500-$1,500/month is common; one particularly strong location (near a lake, multiple road entry points, high traffic) leases for closer to $1,200-$1,500/month.
- Revenue-share alternative to a flat lease: 20-32% of revenue paid to the property owner instead of fixed rent — used at CJ's busiest location (a self-serve car wash getting ~40,000-50,000 cars/day), where CJ's monthly revenue there runs $2,000-$3,000 typically, up to $4,000-$5,000 in peak months, with about $1,000 of that going to the landlord under a ~30% revenue-share arrangement; benefit noted: during downtime (e.g., after a break-in that took the machine offline for a couple months), the revenue-share structure means the landlord shares the loss too.
- His single best "flat-lease" location: leased for $550/month with utilities running ~$500/month (up to $700 in high season) — described as close to 100% profit margin on that specific site aside from utilities, and he's even considering subletting part of the lot to a food truck for ~$500/month as additional income.
- Combined across his 4 locations: targeting ~$15,000-$20,000/month in revenue, netting roughly $5,000/month in profit after all costs (rent, utilities, maintenance tech).
- Acquisition pricing multiples: sellers of existing profitable machines/locations typically sell for 2-3x annual profit (e.g., a machine netting ~$100k/year sold for ~$300k as a real example cited); one location CJ was negotiating to buy was priced around $400,000-$500,000 against ~$15,000/month revenue (~3.25x annual revenue, described as normal for a strong location) — he specifically seeks distressed/undervalued owners to negotiate below-multiple deals instead.
- Financing: does not finance machines; prefers this because financed operators who hit slow periods (e.g., COVID) got stuck with debt payments during downturns and often become motivated (distressed) sellers as a result — buying from these distressed owners is one of his acquisition strategies.

## How to source deals / find locations (the core playbook)
- Network directly with existing owners of Everest, Cooler Ice, Twice the Ice, and similar brand machines in your area — every machine has the owner's phone number printed on it; call/text them directly (this is literally how CJ found one of his sellers).
- Many existing owners (often older, one-machine operators) are burned out/distressed and willing to sell for a steep discount — cited example: a friend bought a broken/beat-up single Everest machine for just $500, put in minor repairs, and it was realistically worth $10,000-$15,000 once fixed; later sold for $15,000-$20,000 to upgrade to a bigger unit.
- Big-brand vending companies (Everest, Cooler Ice, Twice the Ice) will help new buyers of their branded machines find a location if you buy new from them — though brands like Twice the Ice enforce exclusivity zones (won't place two machines within a mile or two of each other, similar to franchise territory rules).
- Location criteria that matter most: high daily traffic count (verifiable via public/government traffic-count data, e.g. .gov traffic data sites), easy parking/pull-through access for vehicles towing trailers (key because core customers are landscaping/pool/electrical trade crews restocking ice/water daily), and street-front visibility.
- Best customer type identified: local trade/service crews (pool techs, landscapers, electricians) who buy ice/water/snacks daily as part of their route — cheaper and more convenient for them than a gas station or grocery store.

## Scaling and market size
- Estimated market size: "hundreds" of ice/water vending machines exist just within Fort Worth proper; CJ estimates his current 4-location footprint represents "less than 1%" of that local market.
- Back-of-envelope scale estimate floated in the interview: if someone went all-in on ice vending across just the Fort Worth market (calling every existing owner, buying/aggregating locations), this could become a $10M+ business.
- CJ's own priority is real estate as his primary business, with ice vending run as a semi-passive side operation — explicitly states this is not his main focus for scaling further.

## Caveats
- Large ice-house-style machines are extremely difficult/expensive to relocate (unlike smaller water-cooler-style units), so a bad location choice for a large machine is a costly, semi-permanent mistake.
- Buying an existing location vs. building new is a trade-off: existing locations often require unknown/hidden repair costs (CJ estimates some acquisitions could need $10,000-$30,000 of deferred maintenance work), while new builds carry permitting/construction timelines and total unknowns about whether the location will actually perform.
- Insurance/security risk: one location was broken into once in ~7 years of operation (machine drilled through), causing a multi-month outage while it was repaired/welded.
- The high (~90%) margin on ice specifically doesn't automatically mean high absolute profit — margins on the water-vending side and overall location profit are more modest once rent, utilities, and maintenance are factored in; overall monthly profit-to-revenue ratio across his portfolio is roughly 25-33% (est. $5k profit on $15-20k revenue).
