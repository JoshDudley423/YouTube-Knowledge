# How to Start this $337k/Yr Service Business You've Never Heard Of
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fqc7dEETGio | Published: 2025-03-27

Interview with Marc Gaskins of Event Kitchens (Charleston, SC) — a commercial-kitchen-equipment rental business serving caterers (convection ovens, ranges, fryers), 12 years old, ~$337K/year revenue.

## The business idea
- Rent commercial kitchen equipment (convection ovens, ranges, fryers, griddles) to catering companies and event planners for weddings/festivals, where the venue lacks a built-in kitchen (common at outdoor/historic wedding venues — a big dynamic in Charleston's wedding-destination market).
- Origin: brother owned a bar; wedding planners asked if the bar could cater events; on-site, caterers repeatedly complained about the poor quality of equipment from the only other local rental company — prompted buying their own equipment to rent out instead.

## Numbers
- Starting investment: ~$10,000 total — 2 convection ovens (South Bend brand, ~$2,800 each), 1 range (~$1,800), 2 fryers (~$900 each), plus ancillary items.
- Current fleet after 12 years: 15 ovens, 9 ranges, 24 fryers.
- Revenue breakdown by equipment category (most recent year cited):
  - Convection ovens: ~$140,000/year (~41% of total sales). Rental rate ~$375–$425 per event depending on whether the customer supplies their own propane, plus delivery ($75 for 0–15 miles, $100 for 15–20, $125 for 20–25, then $5/mile beyond that).
  - Fryers: just under $40,000/year. Rental rate ~$375 (300 + 75 delivery).
  - Ranges: ~$27,000/year.
- Equipment payback: a new convection oven costs ~$5,000 retail; at ~$400/rental (net of delivery), it pays for itself in 10–12 rentals — roughly two "seasons" (spring/fall) for a newly added unit, faster for a well-utilized one (as few as one weekend — 2 rentals — in a high-demand case). A new fryer (~$900) pays back in about 3 rentals.
- Buys some equipment used/discounted opportunistically via Facebook Marketplace and restaurant-closure auctions — example: bought a $5,000 oven for $1,000 from a Chinese restaurant owner who found baked-wing ticket times too slow and no longer needed it; an hour of cleaning made it look new. Will buy used ovens/ranges but explicitly will not buy a used fryer ("gross... disgusting").
- Long-duration/festival rentals (e.g., a 12-day music festival gig) are priced at roughly 2.5x the normal nightly rate rather than a straight per-night multiple, reasoning that charging full nightly rate for that long would exceed the cost of the customer just buying new equipment outright.
- A comparable smaller-scale idea he floated but doesn't do: Blackstone griddle rentals for festivals — griddles cost ~$450 retail (at Lowe's), rent for ~$275 + delivery (roughly 1.5x return per rental, lower margin than the ovens but still viable), popular at festivals; cleanup after festival use is a hassle.
- Idea he mentioned but chose not to pursue as a side hustle: frozen drink / margarita machines for pool parties and events in warm climates — machines cost $1,000–$5,000, he'd charge a minimum ~$350/rental (weekend rental: drop off day 1 to freeze, use day 2, pick up day 3); needs to be insulated/kept out of direct summer sun. Cites a defunct competitor ("Margarita Man") that reportedly was hurt by COVID.

## How the business actually operates
- Equipment is delivered, "test-fired" on site, and picked up; described by the owner as functionally similar to a moving business ("I just move ovens from location to location and clean them in between"). Equipment is re-cleaned/re-inspected the morning after an overnight drop-off so the next customer gets the same quality experience as the first.
- Cleaning burden varies a lot by equipment type: ovens are relatively easy (solid stainless box, occasional degreasing after heavy multi-day use like a festival); ranges and especially fryers are much harder/messier to keep clean.
- Client base is almost entirely catering companies, not individual consumers — individual callers typically balk once they hear pricing; catering companies book recurring seasonal blocks (customers email a full season's schedule as a spreadsheet, and Marc fills his calendar/inventory against it, alerting customers as dates near capacity).
- Growth: entirely organic/inbound referral — zero paid advertising in 12 years, and only a modest amount of unsuccessful cold-calling early on (some cold calls eventually converted years later once a fast same-day fulfillment proved reliability).
- Geographic expansion is opportunistic but bounded by a self-imposed "dollar per hour of driving" threshold — he'll travel to nearby states (Georgia, North Carolina) if the job clears his time-value bar, but turns down distant multi-day-round-trip jobs that don't.
- Competitive dynamic: the sole prior local competitor ran retail + rental as a 9-to-5, Monday-Friday business and couldn't sustainably staff weekend event-driven demand (had to pay staff overtime/double-time); eventually exited the rental business entirely (called Marc directly to say so), letting Marc roughly double inventory overnight to absorb the abandoned demand and eventually dominate the local market.
- Adjacent (unmonetized) service: after training at a commercial-kitchen-repair trade school (Cephessa, C.F.E.S.A., in Rock Hill, SC), Marc now does light equipment repair/maintenance visits for free for his top clients as a loyalty/retention play (they keep renting from him rather than needing a rental for a broken unit) — he estimates he could build a standalone maintenance/repair business from this skill if he chose to but has deliberately not pursued it to stay focused.

## How to validate/launch this in a new market (his advice)
1. Identify the top/high-end caterers in the target city — usually not hard to find, since they do the most on-site cooking.
2. Identify local food/cultural festivals (e.g., Greek festival, Latin festival) and find out who currently supplies their kitchen equipment; ask caterers directly whether they'd consider switching suppliers.
3. Recognize that even in a market with a very different venue culture (e.g., a city where wedding venues typically already have built-in kitchens, unlike Charleston's historic-venue-heavy market), demand may still exist proportionally — a market that "should" support 3 competitors but currently has only 1 (or 0) still leaves room, even if the overall addressable market is smaller.

## Caveats / risks
- Physically demanding: moving 600 lb ovens repeatedly; owner recommends hiring at least a part-time second person for moving/cleaning, and staying physically fit to sustain the work over a full season.
- Deliberately slow, self-funded (cash-only, no debt) growth over 12 years — the owner explicitly frames rapid-growth businesses (e.g., ad-driven e-commerce dependent on a platform algorithm) as more fragile/faster to collapse than a patiently built niche service business, though acknowledges this is anecdotal, not a proven rule.
- Success was significantly aided by a stroke of leverage/luck (a fire that destroyed the owners' original bar business triggered community goodwill and a top local caterer extending an unusually generous first break) — not purely replicable by process alone.
- Business has stayed intentionally single-market/single-owner scale; owner is candid that he does not personally love the work ("going elbow deep in a fryer Monday morning is not my type of fun") but values the steady income, retirement contributions, and bonuses it provides.
