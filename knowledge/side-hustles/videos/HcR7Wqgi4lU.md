# He Made $60K His First Month With This Boring Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HcR7Wqgi4lU | Published: 2026-06-07

Guest: Mike Stuart (Old Cap Coatings, Iowa City, IA — oldcapcoatings.com), a full-time tile installer who started a garage-floor/concrete-coatings business, interviewed by Chris Koerner. Episode contains a HighLevel affiliate promotion (ad-read, not from Mike).

## The business idea
- "Concrete coatings" — commonly called "epoxy flake garage floors" by consumers (a term the business uses in its own marketing/yard signs because it's what registers with customers, even though it isn't accurate — see materials below). Core service: grind/prep a garage floor, apply a base coat, broadcast decorative flake into it, apply a top coat — a decorative, protective floor finish.
- Also does some interior concrete coatings, pool decks, patios, walkways, front porches, and occasional commercial/warehouse work (same product/process), but garages are the large majority of demand and the business's chosen focus.
- Deliberately avoids "metallic epoxy" (the swirled/marbled decorative floors seen in viral social videos) because it requires artistic skill that's hard to train into hired crew — a conscious decision to stay in a simpler, more standardized, more scalable service line ("garages are easy... you're just doing the same thing").
- No trade-specific experience required: Mike's background is tile installation, which he says has virtually no overlap with concrete coatings — he got the idea after a friend (Corey) FaceTimed him from a job site at an existing concrete-coatings company in Illinois and it looked simple enough to learn.
- Materials/terminology detail: NOT actually epoxy in his business — base coat is polyurea, top coat is polyaspartic (these are the "resinous coatings" used); "epoxy" is used only because that's the consumer-recognizable term. Flakes are broadcast by hand ("just chuck it out there... fling it out") until they fully cover the wet base coat, then scraped/flattened and sealed with the top coat.

## Concrete numbers
- Total startup cost: ~$22,000-$25,000 as he actually spent it (including a $1,000 professional logo package and a 20-inch propane-powered grinder) — but he explicitly says it can be trimmed to ~$20,000 or less using an electric grinder + generator instead of propane, and potentially close to $15,000 if minimized further.
- Equipment needed: heavy-duty grinder (20-inch propane grinder used; ~570 lbs, requires two people to move), a high-powered vacuum (paired with the grinder — vacuum has to be powerful enough that the propane-powered grinder doesn't need to draw on the customer's electrical service), spike shoes, rollers, scrapers, a work vehicle. He bought an old U-Haul box truck (with a ramp, needed for the heavy grinder) for $10,000; still using it.
- Grinder rental alternative for testing the business before buying: ~$300/day or ~$1,000/week at his local rental supplier — explicitly floated as a way to prove the model for under $1,000 total before committing to purchasing equipment.
- Materials cost: strict hard cost for base coat + flake + top coat = $1.56/sq ft; plus ~$0.50/sq ft for misc. install materials (gloves, rollers, shipping) = $2.06/sq ft total strict materials cost.
- Fixed monthly overhead: ~$7,300/month (advertising, shop rent, insurance, vehicle gas — everything except labor).
- Labor cost model recommended: pay a lead/"operator" ~$35/hour and a helper ~$25/hour (a $25/hour helper alone is described as easy to find, but a $35/hour lead is worth it to retain quality); crews are W-2 employees, paid weekly, guaranteed a flat 40 hours/week regardless of actual hours worked (helps retention/morale). Typical job = one 10-hour day for a 2-person crew (a "big job" takes 2 days).
- Pricing: $8/sq ft flat rate for all coating work; extras (adding steps, vertical stem walls) cost more. Optional add-on: charge an extra ~$1/sq ft to have someone move all the customer's stored garage items out before the job and back in after — they pay local church-connected college-age guys ~$500 per job to handle this (roughly $100-200+/hour for ~30-60 minutes of work); this add-on is rarely requested since most customers view garage cleanout as a fun excuse to reorganize rather than an obstacle.
- At $8/sq ft, cost breakdown for a ~750 sq ft average job (a large 2-car garage): ~$1,545 materials (750 × $2.06) + ~$600 labor (2 people × 10 hrs × avg ~$30/hr) ≈ $2,145 hard cost against ~$6,000 revenue — cited profit per job: $3,000-$4,000/day for one crew on one job.
- Gross margin targeting: ~50% target margin is the stated goal for a "good local service trades business"; achieved via a "just double your hard cost" pricing heuristic (materials + labor + overhead per sq ft, roughly $3.90/sq ft all-in, priced at $8/sq ft).
- Revenue ramp: February (test month, 3 jobs via word of mouth only) ≈ $10,000-$12,000; March (started running paid ads, ~1 job/week) ≈ $15,000; April (first full month "booked every day," ran nonstop from April 1) = $60,000 revenue at 50%+ profit margin; May (projected) = $69,000. Team goal for their first calendar year of real operation: $1,000,000 revenue, aided by launching a second crew and a planned expansion to a second city (Des Moines, ~2 hours away) the following spring.
- Marketing spend and lead economics: running Meta (Facebook/Instagram) ads across a 50-mile radius; spend scaled from ~$200/day to $400/day (doubled to support launching a second crew). Best-performing ad (a "how much does it cost?" hook, delivered by the brother) generates leads at ~$30/lead; other ad variants run as high as ~$100/lead — both considered acceptable given deal sizes and close rate. Roughly 80-90%+ of all leads come from Meta ads (very little from Google Ads, and they explicitly do NOT use Thumbtack, Angie's List, or similar lead-gen marketplaces, citing misleading pricing expectations set by those platforms for customers). Reported close rate: ~20% of leads become paying jobs. A single $200 personal referral (to a gym acquaintance) turned into a $30,000 warehouse job when the referred person's mother turned out to own a 3,600 sq ft warehouse needing coating.
- Customer down payment: 10% non-refundable deposit to hold a schedule slot; booked out about a month in advance at time of interview.
- Target market/served area: population base of only ~77,000 (Iowa City + Cedar Rapids + Quad Cities within a 50-mile ad radius) — used as evidence that this business doesn't require a huge metro population to reach six figures, let alone a market the size of a DFW suburb.
- Customer profile: overwhelmingly older males (Facebook ad targeting set at 55+ males), owners of 2-4 car garages/shops (no one-car garage jobs yet); framed explicitly as a luxury/discretionary purchase for higher-income households ("nobody needs their concrete coated"), which the business argues makes it comparatively recession-resistant, since it self-selects for customers who keep discretionary spending even in downturns — as long as the operator prices to serve that segment and doesn't race to the bottom on price.

## Steps/process to start and grow this business
1. Learn the trade primarily via YouTube videos and asking AI tools — no formal training required; treat your own garage as a "test" job (a permanent reminder/monument of your first-ever job, even if imperfect).
2. File an LLC and set up a basic website early, then "figure out the rest as you go" rather than waiting for a complete plan — deliberately embraced a "fly the plane as you build it" approach rather than over-planning before starting.
3. Start with word-of-mouth/personal network outreach only (friends, church, gym) before spending on ads — first 3 jobs came purely from personal announcements, no paid marketing or even social posts.
4. Learn/run your own Facebook and Instagram ad campaigns rather than hiring an agency — the reasoning given is that an agency would just be using the same AI tools (e.g., Claude) to generate ad copy/headlines that you can use yourself for free, saving "thousands of dollars a month." Practical method: study what ads catch your own attention while scrolling, replicate that style with a short (~20-30 second) vertical video showing your actual work process (not a static photo), then feed context about your business/location/target market to an AI tool (Claude, in their case) and ask for headline options; also feed live ad-performance screenshots (from Meta Ads Manager) to Claude and ask for budget-reallocation recommendations across ad variants.
5. Answer the phone immediately, every time — described repeatedly as the single highest-leverage behavior in a blue-collar service business, since the bar set by most competitors is very low (many don't answer or return calls promptly). A brother/partner is dedicated specifically to answering the lead-response phone at all hours.
6. State pricing upfront in ads and in the very first conversation, rather than requiring an in-person visit to quote — citing the book "They Ask, You Answer" (Marcus Sheridan) as the source of this principle. Because garage coating pricing is close to a simple linear function of square footage, a rough price can be given immediately by phone, which both builds trust and pre-qualifies leads (people asking directly about price and getting a real number either self-select out immediately if it's a mismatch, or convert with high intent).
7. Use lead-form friction deliberately: an early no-friction "instant form" produced angry, confused, low-intent leads; adding one required qualifying question ("what are you interested in — interior, garage, exterior, other?") before submission meaningfully raised lead quality (at the cost of a somewhat higher cost-per-lead).
8. Close deals in person where possible ("speed to lead" — offering to come out same-day/same-afternoon), with the closer (his brother, a former car salesman) doing final objection-handling live; hold firm on pricing rather than discounting under pressure, reasoning that discounting one job just to fill a calendar slot undercuts a fixed weekly capacity that another full-price customer would otherwise fill (a "your supply is fixed" framing).
9. Reduce risk before fully committing: it's possible to rent equipment (~$300/day) rather than buy, do a handful of test jobs to prove the model and recoup costs, and only then invest in owned equipment.
10. Scale via reinvestment rather than early cosmetic spend — deliberately delayed vehicle wraps/branding to instead reinvest cash into launching a second crew once demand supported it.
11. Standardize pricing to smooth over variance in job difficulty (e.g., don't charge extra for unusually rough/pitted concrete needing more prep time) — the averaging works out across the full job mix.
12. Be prepared to eat a mistake and redo work rather than dispute it — an early job failed (floor started bubbling/peeling) due to using an unfamiliar product against supplier advice; framed the loss as "tuition" rather than a business-ending mistake, and the salesperson (brother) proactively told the customer "we'll take care of you" before even knowing the fix.
13. Consider partnering only cautiously: Mike ended up with two additional partners (two rival aspiring competitors who cold-approached him, already had industry experience, and were talked into joining rather than launching a competing shop) — he explicitly does NOT recommend partnering with relative strangers as a default strategy, calling it something that "worked out" for him more by luck, and instead recommends building direct relationships/education (e.g., contacting an existing operator like himself) before rushing to scale.

## Caveats / risks
- State licensing rules vary — Mike notes some states (e.g., California) require prior contractor experience/licensing before you can legally start this business, whereas Iowa has no such barrier; check local requirements before assuming this is a low-barrier business everywhere.
- Product/material choice is a genuinely contentious topic in the industry (epoxy vs. polyurea vs. polyaspartic base coats) with strong, conflicting opinions from different practitioners — Mike frames this as mostly noise: he chose polyurea specifically because it lets a job be completed in one day instead of two (a business-economics decision, not because it's inherently superior), and cautions against getting paralyzed by dogmatic material debates.
- Mistakes are costly to redo (wasted materials + time), so while "just start and learn as you go" is the stated ethos, rushing without any care/education is explicitly flagged as risky — take your time on execution even without years of formal experience.
- Lead flow is described as highly variable/random day to day (e.g., "sometimes 10 leads come in in a day, sometimes zero") — plan for volatility in top-of-funnel volume even with consistent ad spend.
- Winter/weather is a real constraint — Iowa winters slow the business (can't coat outdoors properly in certain conditions, rain/humidity can also cause on-site delays), so seasonal revenue planning and interior-work incentives (discounting slower months) are used to smooth demand.
- The business intentionally avoids competing on price against lower-cost competitors, accepting a smaller pool of price-sensitive customers in exchange for a wealthier, stickier, lower-hassle clientele — a explicit strategic tradeoff, not a mistake, but one that requires discipline to maintain under pricing pressure.
