# He Tried an Idea From My Video and Made $46k in One Month
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vF7IT30L4jY | Published: 2025-12-07

Interview with Hunter Schenewark (417striping.com, X: @HSchenewark), who started a parking lot line striping / seal coating / crack filling business in Rogersville, MO (pop. ~4,300, near Springfield, MO) with zero industry experience, inspired by an earlier TKO episode with a striper named Cinco (Waco, TX).

## Core business: parking lot line striping, seal coating, and crack filling
- **Line striping**: repainting parking lot lines, handicap spots, directional arrows, stencils.
- **Seal coating**: applying a thin resurfacing layer (squeegee or sprayer) of asphalt-based sealant over old/faded/cracked asphalt to refresh and protect it (parking lots and driveways); needs redoing roughly every 3-5 years.
- **Crack filling**: cleaning weeds/debris out of cracks (using a weed torch to heat/help sealing) then filling with rubberized tar sealant — done in tandem with seal coating.
- Revenue: Month 1 (Sept 27-Oct 31): 4 jobs, $22,874. Month 2 (November): 7 jobs, $46,825 — nearly double the jobs and higher average job size. Two-month total: ~$50,000 in sales, ~$20,000 net profit as actually run (subcontracting the first jobs cost him some margin; would have been ~$30,000 net if done differently).
- Business pivoted from an intended striping-only focus to primarily seal coating once he discovered seal coating jobs are roughly **10x the ticket size** of striping jobs (e.g., $1,500-2,000 striping job vs. $10,000-20,000 seal coating job for the same church).

## How he found his first customers (concrete, step-by-step)
1. Joined local and industry Facebook groups (line striping/seal coating groups) months before starting, to learn about equipment and industry norms — did this passively for a few months before buying equipment.
2. Bought his first striping machine in July; didn't actually start doing jobs until September.
3. **First job (unpaid, practice)**: posted in his town's private Facebook resident group ("Rogersville Residents," ~11,000 members) offering free striping to a local nonprofit, framed as "giving back" rather than disclosing he was inexperienced — ran it as a poll where community members voted which local business/org most needed it (got ~200 votes across ~7 comments). Winner was a nonprofit ("Safe Haven"); striped 5 parking lines for free, took ~2 hours (should have taken ~10 minutes) partly because it was done in the dark and it was his literal first attempt (had practiced beforehand by spraying water with the empty machine in a parking lot to get a feel for the equipment).
4. Lesson learned: he tried to simultaneously use the free-job poll to pitch paying work to the other businesses that got votes but didn't win — got some quotes but zero sales, because he was presenting himself as an established business rather than being upfront that this was new to him; he believes disclosing inexperience openly ("full disclosure, I'm new, that's why it's free, if it looks bad I'll fix it") would have earned him more grace and actually converted better.
5. Tried door-to-door canvassing in Springfield for two afternoons — called it "terrible," struggled to reach actual decision-makers (usually only reached low-wage front-of-store employees, not owners) — doesn't recommend it and says it's not required to succeed in this business.
6. **The channel that actually worked — cold email to churches**: paid someone on Upwork $5-6 to compile a list of all churches in Springfield (300+ churches; city population ~150,000-175,000). Sent one single cold email (not a drip campaign) to each — roughly two-thirds went to generic "info@churchname" catch-all addresses (kept them intentionally, reasoning that at this small volume it's still "worth a shot" even though catch-alls are normally advised against for larger cold-email campaigns). Did not scrub/validate the email list, and sent no follow-ups (acknowledged that follow-up would likely have significantly increased results — "the playbook's working" but leaving obvious ROI on the table).
7. Result: roughly 40% total response rate (about half of responses were meaningful, i.e., ~12% converted to an actual quote request out of all emails sent, discounting polite "not now" replies) — of ~20 quotes given, closed ~9 jobs (8 churches, 1 personal connection who owned 3 Taco Bell franchise locations) for a ~40-50% close rate. Cost of the church contact list: ~$6 total for 300 leads.
8. Also purchased email lists for property managers and commercial real estate agents but hadn't yet tried emailing them by the time of this interview.
9. Replicated the identical church cold-email playbook in a second, unrelated market (near Dallas-Fort Worth, TX, where his parents live) — sent ~100 emails, got 4-5 quotes; a slightly lower conversion rate than the original Missouri market but still validated the channel works in a new geography.

## Why churches specifically as the target niche
- Chosen partly because most have sizable parking lots (~200 stalls average observed) — avoids small, low-value jobs like the free 5-line nonprofit job (a $500 minimum charge job isn't worth it for either side on a tiny lot).
- Churches maintain their lots on a recurring multi-year cycle and typically have budget set aside for it even while being cost-conscious, but purchases go through a board-approval process — creating a **longer sales cycle** (this is why October revenue was lower than November: deals were still working through church board approval).
- Advice for someone wanting to focus purely on line striping (not seal coating): skip churches and property managers, and instead target **construction companies** directly (new parking lots need to be striped as a matter of course) — noted this may be region-specific.
- Regional caveat: seal coating demand is much lower in warmer climates (e.g., Texas) where UV/sun fades paint lines but asphalt doesn't develop the same cold-climate cracking, making striping more of a standalone service there versus in Missouri.

## Pricing (from his own church jobs)
- First real paid job: $450 per line, $20 for a blue-and-white handicap stencil, $10 for a directional arrow/parking stencil.
- Pricing informed by: industry Facebook groups (where operators, including the earlier interviewee "Cinco," shared pricing benchmarks) and directly asking AI tools (ChatGPT, Grok) what to charge.
- Average striping-only job: $1,000-2,000. Seal coating jobs average far higher (cited $10,000-20,000 range for larger church lots) — blended average job size across his book was roughly $7,000.

## Equipment and startup costs
- Striping machine (used): $2,300 (a new/upgraded version would run ~$10,000; a professional rental setup he tried cost $25,000 for a weekend).
- Stencils (arrows, letters, "no parking," etc.): $800, purchased from 1-800-Stencil (chosen purely on price).
- Business insurance: ~$230 initial, then ~$93/month ongoing (also ~$30/month added for a personal vehicle policy since his truck isn't yet a dedicated commercial vehicle).
- Total startup cost: about $3,000.
- Ongoing software overhead: $29/month for "Quote IQ" (quoting/invoicing tool).
- Seal coating equipment is comparatively simpler and cheaper to run than striping equipment: a basic new seal coating setup (drum, sprayer, trailer) costs roughly $3,000-4,000 (up to ~$10,000 fully outfitted), versus striping machines which he found mechanically finicky and prone to breakdowns (bought a second striping machine specifically as a backup after equipment failures threatened to leave a client's lot unfinished before a Sunday service).
- Some low-budget operators reportedly do seal coating out of a plastic drum with a squeegee (very low equipment bar to enter the business).

## Margins and labor
- Striping-only jobs (early on, solo, no employees): paint/material costs ~20% of job revenue, pocketing ~75-80% as profit.
- Seal coating jobs: materials cost more, but even after paying labor, margins were still 30-40%.
- Hired part-time/weekend labor sourced entirely from his church congregation (including teenagers) — paid $15/hour for teenagers, $25/hour standard rate, bumped to $30-35/hour for late-night jobs (some ran past midnight). All hourly, not per-job. Plans to hire full-time crews starting in spring.
- Retrospective lesson: wishes he'd hired skilled help sooner — his hires turned out to do more precise/detail-oriented work (chalk lines, measuring, angles) than he did himself, and he realized his highest-value role was sales/oversight, not doing the physical labor.

## Marketing/web presence (or lack thereof)
- Paid an overseas freelancer $200 to build a basic website with AI-generated images and no real content — explicitly treated as a low priority ("what's better than a perfect website? $50,000 in sales in two months").
- Never set up a functioning Google Business Profile (account got suspended by Google for unclear reasons, still unresolved — considered renaming the company to work around it).
- No paid ads used to date; 100% of paying jobs came from cold email (church list) or one personal referral connection (Taco Bell franchisee).
- Plans to build up online/Google presence over the winter off-season ahead of spring, since he expects organic Google visibility to become a bigger lead source, and considers this niche generally under-marketed/easy to rank in given how few players focus on it as a Google-findable local service.
- Some national/regional franchise competitors exist (e.g., "We Do Lines," "1-800 Striping") but he hasn't felt direct competitive pressure yet, partly because small business/church customers prefer a single vendor who offers both striping and sealing rather than a striping-only specialist.

## Practical lessons / mistakes acknowledged
- **In-person presence increases close rate significantly** for this kind of trust-dependent, older-demographic B2B buyer (churches specifically) — cold-emailing then meeting in person outperformed trying to close remotely/quickly, contrary to his initial thesis that "fastest quote wins." He also noticed his close rate improved further after upgrading from an old, "beat-up minivan" (loading equipment with rear seats down) to a truck — a more professional-looking vehicle reportedly increased buyer confidence.
- **Losing early quotes from being too narrowly focused**: several early prospects wanted a bundled striping + seal coating quote; when he initially said "I only do striping," he lost those jobs to competitors who covered both, which pushed him to add seal coating (and eventually make it his primary revenue driver).
- **Fire safety incident**: while crack-filling with a weed torch to clear debris from cracks, a spark ignited nearby grass/leaves and the fire spread quickly (was ~20 feet from a house and a church) — put out using sand from his project supplies plus a fire extinguisher he happened to be carrying for OSHA compliance. Flagged explicitly as a real safety risk of this specific technique (open flame near dry vegetation).
- Reflecting on what he'd do differently: buy new (not used) equipment from the start to reduce mechanical downtime once the business model is validated, and hire skilled labor sooner rather than trying to do detailed work himself.
- Chose this business over alternatives he considered (residential window installation, water well drilling) specifically because it's B2B, "construction-adjacent," has a low barrier to entry/equipment cost relative to something like well drilling (which he estimated at "a couple hundred thousand dollars" to get into), and is an underserved niche that's easy to rank for organically on Google.

## Caveats
- Results are specific to this operator's market and timing; his own comparison of otherwise-identical cold email campaigns in two different markets (Missouri vs. Texas) already showed a meaningfully different response rate, and he explicitly notes his home market (Texas, near DFW) doesn't need seal coating the way Missouri does due to climate differences.
- He acknowledges leaving significant performance on the table by not following up on unanswered cold emails or scrubbing his purchased email lists — implying the ~12% conversion figures understate what's achievable with better execution of the same channel.
- Numbers are self-reported by the operator in an interview, not independently audited.
