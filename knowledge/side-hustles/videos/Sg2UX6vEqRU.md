# Trailer Rentals, Supplements, and Vending Machine Saturation!
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sg2UX6vEqRU | Published: 2024-04-16

Live call-in Q&A format (Episode 9). Chris takes calls from business owners and answers pre-submitted questions. Multiple distinct segments below.

## Segment: Supplement retail store dealing with a racism-driven boycott
- Caller's business: 2 supplement/vitamin/pre-workout retail stores with in-store smoothie bars, one in North Texas (opened 2014), one in Southern Oklahoma (opened 2019), ~40 miles apart, each serving towns of ~30,000 people.
- Revenue: Texas location ~$30k/month, Oklahoma ~$18-21k/month. Gross margins 25-30%. Net profit: Texas ~$7-8k/month, Oklahoma ~$3k/month.
- COVID caused revenue to roughly double at peak (Texas ~$55-60k/month, Oklahoma ~$30k/month) before declining post-COVID.
- Problem: over the past year, a local group began spreading a boycott campaign against the owner (who has a Middle Eastern-descent name, born/raised in Texas) tied to recent geopolitical conflict; spread via word of mouth at a local gym and a competing local business owner. Membership/loyalty program cancellations tracked with the campaign; sales declined over ~4-5 months then plateaued (flat for the last 5-6 months at the time of the call).
- No new direct competitors have opened within 30 miles in 5 years (a nearby GNC is poorly staffed/located and isn't real competition).
- Advice given: reach out to local news/media to tell his story publicly, specifically testing this first at the smaller/lower-risk Oklahoma location (month-to-month-adjacent lease situation there); research reporters in advance to find ones likely to be sympathetic; make a public video standing in front of the store citing concrete facts (no new competition, growing fitness demand) and pleading his case; accept that some backlash/hate is likely but argued that controversy and haters help a message spread further ("nothing goes viral without haters"); framed as an asymmetric bet given a month-to-month lease -- worst case sales stay flat and he can relocate/exit (his original long-term plan was to exit retail by age 35 anyway), best case a community "groundswell" of support materializes.

## Segment: Starting a dump-trailer rental business with adult sons
- Caller wants to buy dump trailers (~$10,000 each) and set his two adult sons up running a trailer-rental business near Corpus Christi/Texas Hill Country, with sons paying him back over time (proposed structure: pay back trailer cost via ~$500-1,000/month until paid off, then ~$250/month "royalty" for 5-10 years, without giving sons formal equity) and eventual expansion to their own trailers.
- Sons' roles: one is more of a salesperson/extrovert, the other more introverted but knowledgeable about construction; they have existing connections with home builders, construction, and fencing companies in the area.
- Market pricing found locally: dump trailer rentals go for $150-$200/day; caller planned to start at $100/day to build reputation, with multi-day discounts.
- Advice given: do NOT buy any trailers yet -- first validate demand by starting all planned marketing channels (a simple landing page via Carrd, Facebook Marketplace listings, "rent me" signage messaging) before spending money; when interested renters respond, tell them it's "not available yet," collect their name/number, and follow up once a trailer is actually acquired.
- If demand is validated, start with just ONE trailer rather than three (hedges downside risk in case of an economic/construction downturn) -- cited a parallel example: a pet cremation business considered buying a $100,000 crematorium but found competitors only used theirs ~10% of the time, so they rented one hourly instead of buying, minimizing capital risk.
- Recommended formalizing the payback arrangement with a simple written contract specifying obligated payments and a repossession right (with an Apple AirTag/tracker on the trailer to locate it if repossession is needed), with some built-in flexibility for slow seasons.
- Suggested Chris's own preferred structure: charge the sons the market/near-market rental rate if they need to actually rent a trailer to fulfill early bookings before owning one -- minimizes financial risk while still testing demand.

## Segment: Laid-off product manager weighing a "dads who cook" content side hustle vs. buying an existing business
- Caller: laid off product manager (digital health background, bootstrapped a company to a few million in revenue previously) with ~5 months of financial runway, living in high-cost LA area, building a "dads who cook" community/newsletter side project (free newsletter + $20/month membership product) currently at ~$0 MRR; cohort classes previously priced at $30 then $75 (discounted from $150) had weak conversion past $75.
- Assessment given: the side hustle isn't a viable path to replace lost income in the near term because (a) it requires inventing a new content-business model without existing precedent specifically for "dads who cook" (some loose precedent exists in adjacent fitness-for-older-men content spaces), (b) caller has no existing audience to convert, and (c) growing a newsletter/community to $5-10k/month MRR is described as "a slow linear grind." Advice: keep the newsletter running as a creative outlet but do not invest marketing spend or split focus into growing it; searching for a business to buy is "at least one full-time job" on its own.
- Given the tough product-manager job market (described as "bleak," mass layoffs), and the caller's ~$100k of free cash plus willingness to use SBA loans and seller financing, the advice was to pursue acquiring an existing small business rather than building from scratch, prioritizing an industry where the caller has direct relevant experience (his has been healthcare, e.g., medical billing/coding companies) since domain experience reduces acquisition risk (contrast: a product manager buying a restaurant with zero industry experience = high risk).
- Specific example given as a comparable success story: Chris's business partner Nick bought a medical billing company in Boise (relocating himself for the deal) for "a couple million," did not personally operate it (installed an operator with minority equity), and sold it two years later for $5-6 million with mostly efficiency changes.
- Cited opportunity within medical billing: buying an existing medical billing company and cutting costs by outsourcing labor to lower-cost regions (India, Philippines) or automating with AI.
- Tactical sourcing advice for finding small, often-unlisted acquisition targets: identify a specific niche of ~20-30 target businesses, hire a "skip tracer" (a role commonly found on Upwork; essentially someone with access to people-search/contact-lookup tools) to find owner contact info, then reach out via call/text/email; look for signs of complacency (outdated websites, apparent underpricing, businesses that seem to have been around a long time without much marketing) as the "easiest and biggest source of upside," especially in "boomer-owned" businesses.
- General framing: home service businesses were suggested as lower-risk/lower-skill acquisition targets than manufacturing or restaurants (a home service skillset can often be learned via a weekend of YouTube videos); willingness to relocate for a good deal is treated as a major advantage ("beggars can't be choosers").
- Advice against sinking money into paid ads for a $20/month digital product from a standing start ("that's a whole nut to crack ... you're going to have to put a bunch of money into it just to break even") -- the alternative funnel model referenced (attributed to Russell Brunson's playbook) is a cheap ~$49 front-end offer priced to break even on ad spend, used to generate leads for a higher-ticket back-end offer.

## Segment: Selling an AI sales chatbot to small "blue-collar" businesses (e.g., shed dealers)
- Caller: software engineer with an AI chatbot/sales-automation product, originally targeting complex white-collar integrations, pivoted after showing it to a shed-dealer business owner (~$3M/year revenue) who reacted enthusiastically ("like showing a caveman fire").
- Product: an omnichannel AI chatbot (website chat, SMS/text via a service like Twilio, phone, Facebook Messenger) trained on a business's FAQ data to auto-answer routine inbound sales questions (e.g., "will this shed fit my property," delivery/sizing questions) and filter for the ~2-3/week high-quality, ready-to-buy leads.
- Proposed pricing: $600-700/month recurring, justified by comparing to the cost of hiring a human employee or VA to do the same triage work (which the target customer already struggles to hire/retain reliably, even at a decent wage).
- Advice given: chatbots are a "crowded" category in tech circles, but irrelevant if the target customer (small blue-collar business owners) has never heard the word "chatbot" -- the market awareness gap itself is the opportunity.
- Go-to-market plan suggested: get the first customer (the shed dealer) live even at a discounted/negotiated price in exchange for detailed access to his pain points, build the initial landing page/branding narrowly targeted at the shed industry specifically (not a generic name -- but keep the underlying product name generic so it can be repositioned to other verticals later), then scrape a list of shed businesses nationally/statewide and cold-outreach them via text starting with an ordinary customer-style question ("are you open this weekend?") to open a conversation before pitching the product.
- Suggested target conversion benchmark ("the 1% rule"): if you can convert roughly 1% of a large outbound list (e.g., 100 out of 10,000 shed businesses), consider that a scalable business worth investing further optimization into (with the expectation that optimization can lift conversion to 2-5%). Once one vertical (e.g., sheds) is proven and the first real customer's data has trained the underlying model, expanding to additional verticals becomes progressively easier/cheaper by reusing the same underlying product with new front-end branding.
- Caveat raised: caller had only a verbal positive reaction from the pilot customer, not a signed/paid commitment yet -- advice stressed getting an actual paying customer (even at a steep discount) before scaling outreach, and being wary of extrapolating a scalable business from a single anecdotal positive conversation without validating against 10+ more prospects in the same niche.

## Pre-submitted question: Buying a home in a very small (<40 home) community with none listed for sale
- Advice: statistically, if average homeowner tenure is ~3-5 years and there are ~35 homes, expect roughly one home to turn over every ~2 months on average -- so waiting is one option.
- Faster approach recommended: physically knock on all ~40 doors in one evening (a Tuesday/Thursday, 6-9pm), introduce yourself, express genuine interest in living there, leave a memorable impression (e.g., a distinctive item of clothing) and a card/flyer, collect contact info, and follow up every ~6 months.

## Pre-submitted question: Repurposing a patented tufa stone quarry to produce material for the cement industry (Pelon)
- Caller's quarry could potentially be reprocessed into a cement-industry input material; initial testing costs $3,000-$10,000 to confirm feasibility.
- Advice: framed as a high-upside, low-cost "asymmetric bet" -- rather than self-funding the test, find outside investors willing to put in that amount in exchange for a percentage of the upside (analogized to the real case of a UK man who raised outside money to excavate a landfill for a lost hard drive containing ~$130 million in Bitcoin, since he couldn't personally afford the $1-5 million excavation cost).

## Pre-submitted question: Is the vending machine business oversaturated?
- Chris's position: no business is inherently "oversaturated" as a general claim; perceived saturation is usually just an artifact of someone's narrow personal social-media bubble (example given: virtual assistant agencies "feel" saturated on Twitter because so many people in that niche online community are launching them, but that doesn't reflect true market-wide supply vs. demand).
- The one case where "oversaturated" can be stated with more confidence: when a specific local market has a provably higher number of providers than comparable demographically-matched markets (e.g., twice as many plumbers in one town vs. equivalent towns) -- but even then, if all those competitors are still in business, it usually signals higher local demand or that all the incumbents are simply not maximizing their own potential (unfairly priced, poor marketing, bad service), leaving room for a better-run competitor.
- Framed conclusion: rather than asking "is this market saturated," ask whether you personally want to be in the business, have any unfair advantage (e.g., unique sourcing/placement access for vending machines), and have genuine passion for it.
