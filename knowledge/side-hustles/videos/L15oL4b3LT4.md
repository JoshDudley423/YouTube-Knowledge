# He Turns Abandoned Driving Ranges Into Cash Machines
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L15oL4b3LT4 | Published: 2026-03-19

Interview with the founders of Golf Ranch / "Driving Range Guys" (golfranch.com), whose business model is buying outdated, overgrown/abandoned or underperforming driving ranges and turning them into modern membership-driven venues.

**The core idea:** old mom-and-pop driving ranges are an overlooked, low-bar industry. Buy an existing (ideally already-loved but neglected) driving range, make targeted upgrades based on customer feedback, and convert it to a membership/experience-driven model rather than the traditional pay-per-bucket model.

**Growth numbers cited:**
- One flagship location went from ~3.5 million balls hit per year to **12 million balls/year** (~3.4x) after the renovation/repositioning.
- **8,000 members across 4 locations**; goal is **100,000 members and 50 locations, one per US state**.
- One location: **10+ million balls hit per year**, described as the 2nd busiest driving range in the country.
- LTV:CAC ratio is about **10:1** (average customer LTV is "north of a couple hundred dollars"); one-year CAC is called "fantastic," and they're optimistic (not yet proven) about 5-year CAC/LTV.
- One flagship site runs at ~85% capacity.

**What they changed vs. the old driving-range status quo (identified via customer surveys):**
1. Golf ball quality — sort balls before returning them to hoppers (most old ranges just throw balls back in regardless of condition); continuously replace worn stock (one location goes through **120,000 new balls/year**, sourced at volume-discount pricing of **$0.50-$0.70 per ball**).
2. Mat/turf quality — replaced worn, hole-ridden mats by testing many options bought on Amazon and sourcing from smaller specialty distributors.
3. Weather protection — added covered tee lines (protects against Texas heat and hail), extending usable hours that would otherwise be dead/lost revenue.
4. Extended hours — open until midnight in summer (most driving ranges close at sundown); added lights; this created new use cases like first dates.
5. Ambience — string lights, music (breaking the "stuffy" golf-culture norm), relaxed dress code, fans and heaters, patio seating for non-playing family members, putting greens and other amenities.

**Business/pricing model — "Range Pass" membership:**
- $20/month membership gets **40% off per-bucket pricing**; month-to-month, cancel anytime (deliberately flexible given golf's seasonality).
- ~40% of visitors at the flagship location are members; membership skews toward off-peak (morning) visitors, converting typically "dead" morning hours into paying/loyal traffic.
- Membership wasn't the original plan — it emerged because high-frequency customers were already asking for a loyalty/commitment option ("stumbled into membership").
- Deliberate Costco-style model: membership dues cover overhead; retail/bucket purchases plus loss-leader offerings (subsidized food/drink, subsidized golf lessons) drive the profit and retention, rather than treating memberships purely as float.
- Instructors are treated as key salespeople/relationship-holders (the "face of the business") — actively encouraged to upsell customers into Range Pass memberships and events, described as more effective at building loyalty/rapport than front-desk staff.

**Revenue mix:** food/beverage is intentionally kept to only **~15% of total revenue** — simple menu (hot dogs, burgers off a flat-top, buckets of beer/seltzer/soft drinks) as an amenity, explicitly avoiding Topgolf's food-and-beverage-forward, "hospitality first, golf second" model.

**Why Topgolf-style entertainment golf struggles on repeat visits (per the founders):** its hourly-bay-rental model creates a strong first-5-minutes experience but a poor last-5-minutes experience (rushed out, surprise bill). Golf Ranch instead lets people stay as long as they want rather than renting fixed time blocks — cited as a major driver of satisfaction/retention.

**Technology — Toptracer Range (licensed from Topgolf):**
- Topgolf's original strategy was to license this ball-tracking tech directly to mom-and-pop driving range owners, but most owner-operators (often in their 70s) wouldn't pay for/adopt it.
- Licensing requires a significant upfront build-out cost (wiring, on-site "data center" unit) rolled into a per-bay licensing rate, plus a multi-year lease/payment commitment. Upfront investment historically cited around **$200,000** for a build-out (not confirmed precisely by the guest).
- Ranges that did adopt it early saw meaningful traffic/demand increases that repaid the investment.
- Insight that shaped this business: since owners themselves largely wouldn't invest in the tech, the opportunity was to buy the ranges outright and install the tech directly rather than trying to sell it to reluctant existing owners.
- Add-on upsell product: "Toptracer Coach," letting golf instructors store student data/drills so students get personalized feedback tied to swing data; also offers a customer mobile app to track performance data — a real differentiator vs. traditional ranges with zero performance tracking.

**Customer acquisition:** paid ads/SEO and search are described as a huge channel — "golf lessons near me" is called a high-intent, highly qualified search term. A recurring acquisition offer used across locations: a **free first bucket** ("come try it, stay as long as you want, if you don't love it that's on us").

**Site selection criteria for expansion:** prefer buying existing driving ranges that already have community affection but need a facelift, rather than building new from raw land (raw-land builds explicitly deprioritized right now). Also evaluates local golf demand (number of public courses/practice facilities in the area) and demographics — the business specifically targets **families** (parents introducing kids to golf), not just wealthy/exclusive-neighborhood golfers, which the founders say expanded their addressable market beyond what they originally assumed (their stated biggest early mistake: assuming the only customer was "serious golfers").

**Costs:** land is the largest and most "exorbitant" cost; water is a bigger and more surprising ongoing cost than expected (an example irrigation build-out for a full outfield was quoted at ~$1 million, so they instead invested only in visible/high-traffic turf areas, e.g. "the first 30 yards" of a putting green, leaving the rest natural/dead grass since customers don't walk on it and can't see it at night). Labor, infrastructure build-out, and ball replenishment are ongoing significant costs. Ball-picking uses a mix of manual crews and an automated robotic ("Roomba"-style) picker; rain can halt picking, so they keep ball inventory buffers.

**Caveats/risks noted:**
- Golf is a seasonal business — capacity/revenue fluctuates with weather/season; the company tries to expand "capacity" by extending operating hours and creating rainy-day revenue options rather than just adding physical locations.
- A brand-new/abandoned-range acquisition (example cited: an upcoming Grand Prairie location with a history of bad Google reviews and broken equipment) starts from a worse position than an already-loved range — described as starting "from minus one or two," not zero.
- Deliberately avoiding rapid franchise-style scaling that risks diluting service quality/consistency — prioritizing "growing the right way" over speed.
- Founders stress the model is "simple" (buy range, invest in the specific things golfers said mattered) but caution "simple doesn't mean easy" — most existing driving range owners haven't updated even basic marketing (some websites unchanged since the 1990s, not mobile-optimized), which the founders see as a major exploitable gap for younger/digitally savvy operators.
- General business-improvement advice from the founders: proactively survey customers and personally call top customers to ask what's missing, rather than giving up on feedback loops after low response rates or writing off a channel (e.g., Facebook ads) after one weak attempt.
