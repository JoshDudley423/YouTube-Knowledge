# How to Get a Yes From Anyone (and Change Your Life)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSahqscHOsk | Published: 2025-05-16

Guest: Stu Heinecke, author of "How to Get a Meeting with Anyone" (new edition out June 3), pioneer of "contact marketing" / "contact campaigns."

This isn't a specific side-hustle idea video but a tactical playbook on creative outreach ("contact marketing") that can be applied to land clients, partnerships, distribution deals, or key meetings for any business.

Core concept: contact marketing targets a small, high-value list (often ~100 people or fewer — key clients, mentors, partners) with unique, personalized, hard-to-ignore outreach, rather than mass/cold outreach at scale. Response rates in the 100% range are achievable and "a feature of contact marketing," not a fluke; one campaign even exceeded 100% response by going viral among recipients (reached 3-4x the original audience).

Case studies with numbers:
- A dental startup selling a tongue scraper (via YouTube) wanted Walmart's 6,500 stores. After normal channels stalled, they ran a $28 targeted Facebook ad aimed specifically at Walmart's Bentonville HQ (targeted by location/demographics), using a tongue-in-cheek dentist mascot. Walmart's legal department contacted them within 48 hours to ask them to take it down; this led to a meeting with Walmart's dental buyer and a $2 million order. The founder said this also roughly 10x'd the company's valuation (from ~$2M to ~$20M) — framed as a "69,500,000% ROI" from a $28 ad.
- Chris Koerner's own story (referenced): bought one of everything from Buc-ee's convenience store chain (~50 locations, Southeast US) for $1,500, launched an unofficial resale business, cold-emailed 20 reporters, it went viral, Buc-ee's legal department reached out (a sign of getting the "attention of higher-ups"). Five years later, that business does "millions of dollars a year" and is the industry leader in reselling Buc-ee's products online, despite no official partnership.
- A commercial realtor noticed (via public tax records) that Chris had signed a new retail lease; he laminated/framed a small local newspaper article about the new store opening with a personal note, hand-delivered it. Cost: ~$10 in materials, ~30 minutes of time. Estimated value of the resulting relationship: a future 5-year lease worth $36,000-$180,000/month scenario at ~5% commission = roughly $9,000 in commission from that one gesture — a huge ROI on minimal cost/effort. Chris found it so memorable he shared it with his wife and friends unprompted (turning "100% response rate" into "400%" via organic sharing).
- Dan Waldschmitt (turnaround specialist): scans daily business news for companies that just missed earnings estimates (candidates for a corporate turnaround engagement), then sends the CEO a custom, non-sharpened replica movie-prop-quality sword (~$1,000 to make, made by a Gladiator prop maker), engraved with the CEO's name and a note ("Business is war... if you ever need a few extra hands in battle, we've got your back"), packed in a felt-lined wooden box with a handwritten note. Reports "100% response rate" among CEOs who engage, with resulting engagements "worth a million and up."
- A Venmo/PayPal micro-payment tactic: sending someone a small payment ($1-50) with a message in the transaction "comments" field to get their attention cheaply, since almost no one reads comments unless told to look. Cited as an underused, low-cost outreach channel. (Caveat noted: the recipient must follow through professionally on the resulting opportunity — a case is described where the sender got the initial meeting but then handled the follow-up poorly and lost the opportunity.)
- Cold-emailing John McAfee: differentiated a generic cold email by offering to pitch entirely in person, at McAfee's house, on McAfee's schedule — bought a plane ticket only after McAfee said yes.
- Sponsoring a stranger's kid's Little League team (after researching a target buyer's personal life) as a relationship-building/rapport-building move before a business ask — cited example: sponsoring for ~$300 could later support referencing it to open a $3M relationship.
- "Donut box" example: instead of literally bringing donuts to an office (common, not memorable), one practitioner (Dale Dupree, referred to as "Dale Dri" in transcript) delivers an empty donut box with grease stains and a note explaining he ate them all while waiting so long for a meeting — memorable because it's unexpected.

Stu Heinecke's signature tactic — personalized cartoons: mails custom cartoons (drawn by top-tier professional cartoonists, e.g., from Playboy/The New Yorker) personalized to the recipient (the joke is always about the recipient, who is kept "out of frame" so it applies cleanly, and the humor always ultimately compliments them). Example: sent Mark Cuban a custom cartoon referencing his Mavericks tickets on an 18x24" foam board; it's now framed on Cuban's office wall. Also uses a "Heinecke window" — a die-cut envelope/mailer showing only part of the cartoon through a window to create a hook, with the full payoff visible only once opened.

AI angle discussed: AI can help generate cartoon/outreach content quickly (demoed generating a custom cartoon image in ~30 seconds from a prompt) and can do "deep research" on a target person (e.g., searching an old/inactive social handle to uncover a hobby or passion no one else references) to craft a hyper-personalized angle competitors won't have used. However, both guests argue AI-generated creative content (cartoons specifically) is currently visibly inferior to skilled human work, and as AI-driven cold outreach (email/calling agents) proliferates and drives response rates down toward ~0.1%, in-person and uniquely human outreach approaches will become comparatively more effective, not less.

Practical guidance: identify your "top 100" (or fewer) highest-leverage contacts rather than optimizing for mass-outreach volume; a targeted campaign to ~20 highly influential people can beat a 10,000-person cold email blast on both percentage and raw response counts. Follow-up matters: a mediocre initial response rate (e.g., 30%) from a genuinely differentiated outreach method, followed by real phone/email follow-up referencing the specific stunt, still vastly outperforms typical cold outreach. Job seekers and low-key sellers are encouraged to embrace standing out (e.g., an old comparison to bringing donuts with your resume) rather than avoid it out of fear of looking foolish.
