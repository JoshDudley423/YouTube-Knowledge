# You'll Never Believe How Profitable This Business Is...
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xQQJOQjZSNs | Published: 2024-11-05

Interview with Shannon Houchin, founder of Roadside Republic (roadsiderepublic.com), on the roadside produce (mainly peach) stand business, which she scaled to over 100 locations in her first summer in South Carolina and now runs in the Dallas-Fort Worth (DFW) metro.

## The business idea
- Roadside produce stands (primarily peaches, also watermelon, cantaloupe, other seasonal produce) set up as pop-up tents, typically in the parking lots of independent gas stations/convenience stores, along high-traffic roads.
- Origin: Shannon apprenticed a summer under a mentor ("Rob," a former colleague from her family's casino-gaming software company) who had grown a small peach-stand operation to 5 stands over 5-6 years, profiting ~$100,000-$150,000/season. She scaled it to 100 stands in her very first summer (2013), generating $2M+ in gross cash sales selling only peaches.

## Startup costs and unit economics
- Startup cost per stand: ~$200 (a 10x10 tent, a couple of folding/Lifetime tables, chairs — "not fancy").
- Originally cash-only; now accepts cashless payment apps and credit/debit cards as well (shifted post-COVID).
- Margins: roughly 70% gross margin cited as typical for the business.
- Peach case economics (25-lb "half bushel" case): cost varies heavily by region — west of the Mississippi (where she now operates, in Texas) she typically pays $20-$32/case; east of the Mississippi (where she started, South Carolina), the same case cost only $12-$15 due to proximity to growing regions. In a bad harvest year (a 2023 Georgia freeze destroyed ~90% of that state's crop), she paid as much as $48/case and still turned a profit.
- Pricing model: switched away from selling by the pound (which invites direct comparison to grocery store pricing) to selling by a "peck" basket — deliberately breaking the consumer's mental pricing pattern. A "peck" here = about 4 lbs, sold for $15. One 25-lb case yields about 8 baskets, so a $28-$32 case generates roughly $120 gross revenue (about 4x cost when harvest/pricing is favorable).
- Stand-level profitability targets (her framework, "Roadside Riches," includes a "peach calculator" tool): a good stand should net a minimum of $1,000/week profit; average target is $1,500/week profit; a really good stand nets $2,500-$3,500/week profit. On strong weekends, a single stand can gross $5,000+ in sales over 2-3 days.
- Labor cost: payroll runs about $700/week per staffed stand. Workers (many high school/college students) are guaranteed $20/hour effective pay ($15/hour base plus a pooled tip reserve), and typically end up earning $25-$35/hour once tips are included.
- Also monetizes as a broker/trainer: teaches others the model for a flat coaching fee and can broker produce sourcing to them directly, leveraging her established relationships with orchards nationwide.

## Location strategy — the "convenience store unlock"
- Key insight (learned from casino-industry experience with convenience stores): independent, local convenience stores/gas stations pull customers from only a 3-5 mile radius. This means stands placed at convenience stores just 3+ miles apart do not cannibalize each other's sales — enabling near-unlimited density of stands across a metro area without competing against yourself.
- Selection criteria evolved from "eyeballing" busy locations to using Department of Transportation traffic-count data to proactively identify high-traffic sites (mentions specific targets like a location with 40,000-50,000 cars/day).
- Visibility is described as the single most important success factor — a simple, symbolic image (a peach, a watermelon) visible from the road triggers an ingrained "collective memory" association (freshness, nostalgia, local, quality) that primes consumers to stop before they even see the actual product — described as a rare quality among businesses.
- Signage should be simple/symbolic rather than text-heavy.
- Deal terms with gas stations: informal handshake deals (no formal lease), typically a flat daily rate around $25/day per parking space used; can exit easily if a location underperforms (she gives locations about 3 weeks to prove viability before pulling the stand and relocating).
- Location evolution over the years: started with large (10x21 to 20x40) semi-permanent industrial tents (from a supplier called King Canopy) set up for the entire Memorial Day-to-Labor Day season in higher-traffic exurban/rural spots; later moved into city-limit partnerships (e.g., a long-running spot near TCU in Fort Worth) and eventually consolidated into a fixed retail/farmers-market space (a stall inside an existing flea market, "Pulga") when road construction killed traffic at a prior spot.

## Sourcing and product quality (why roadside peaches taste better)
- The produce sold is intentionally "number twos" — fruit not destined for grocery stores because it has cosmetic blemishes, not because it's lower quality in taste. "Number ones" (visually perfect fruit) go to grocery chains and get picked earlier, then placed in deep cold storage for shipping/shelf life.
- Since stone fruit (like peaches) continues ripening after picking, extended cold storage halts/damages that ripening process — this, not the variety or farm, is the main reason grocery store peaches taste bland or turn mealy/leathery/brown instead of ripening properly on the counter.
- Her produce comes fresh off the truck without long-term refrigeration, preserving natural ripening and flavor — same farms as grocery stores in many cases, different post-harvest handling.
- Peach size varies by point in the harvest season: orchards plant ~25 different peach varietals with staggered ripening windows (roughly every 2 weeks) across the Memorial Day-to-Labor Day season; peaches get progressively larger as the season progresses (more sun exposure, orchards prune blossoms to concentrate growth) — so bigger peaches later in the season are simply a seasonal artifact, not a different farming approach.
- Sourcing tiers: buying directly from an orchard owner (preferred — better pricing, relationship-based flexibility, easier fulfillment in a pinch) vs. going through a produce broker/wholesaler when an orchard won't sell directly to a reseller (common when an orchard already has an exclusive wholesaler relationship). At scale (semi-truckload volumes), she had to reserve harvest allocations directly with orchard families months in advance, similar to how established brokers/resellers operate.

## Sales technique: "storytelling selling"
- Central differentiator from other struggling produce-stand operators (she notes other farmstand operators are frustrated/confused by her outsized success while they "struggle to make $7 on some tomatoes"): explicitly tell customers the story behind the product — which orchard it came from, when it was picked, the varietal name, whether it's a cling or freestone peach — to establish expertise/authority, which lets her charge a premium price without resistance ("sell your story," not just "tell your story").
- She is fully transparent that she is a reseller, not a grower, and leans into naming/describing specific orchards by name as part of the sales pitch.
- Customer service approach for inevitable bad produce: train staff to never argue with a dissatisfied customer over produce quality (which is inherently inconsistent) — simply replace the item without friction ("we don't have X-ray vision") rather than defending the product, which builds trust/repeat business.

## Business model evolution / diversification
- Adds a new business segment every year onto the core roadside-stand model to extend the otherwise short (May-September) season toward year-round revenue:
  - Online sales and shipping.
  - A subscription/delivery service ("farm boxes") using a fleet of high-school/college-age drivers.
  - Mobile "pop-up farmers markets" targeting HOAs and condo/residential associations (a tactic she started during COVID when HOAs were already bringing in food trucks) — described as extremely productive: about $2,000-$2,500 in sales within a 2-hour pop-up window, deliberately scheduled on the otherwise-slower weekdays (Monday-Wednesday).
  - Subscription delivery partnerships with high-end senior/independent-living facilities (dropping off farm boxes to all residents on a set day/week).
  - A "leftover produce" channel using the app "Too Good To Go" — since almost no other fruit/vegetable sellers use it, her leftover listings get an outsized, high-demand waitlist of interested buyers, turning otherwise-wasted inventory into recovered revenue/goodwill.
  - Training/coaching: teaches students (originally face-to-face, now also via a paid online course she filmed with her son) how to run their own seasonal roadside stand to fund a full year of college tuition/living expenses in 3 months of summer work; also brokers produce sourcing to people she trains.
- Employs 25-200 kids (mostly high school/college age) during peak season; during weekends may run 6-8 simultaneous pop-ups with kits/tents dispatched to different locations, then torn down and returned same day.
- Competitor comparison: "The Peach Truck" (Nashville-based) reportedly runs ~60 locations partly via consignment placements inside other businesses, and works directly with Titan Farms (the largest US peach orchard, ~7,000 acres in South Carolina) using a pre-order/RSVP model with satellite warehouses in dense markets like DFW for truck-based delivery — a different logistics model than Shannon's pop-up-tent approach.

## Caveats
- Physical labor intensity is significant: peak-season work weeks can run up to 80 hours across many locations; she has intentionally shrunk her own footprint over 11 years (from 100 simultaneous stands toward a more consolidated DFW operation) partly for this reason, shifting emphasis toward teaching/brokering rather than operating dozens of stands herself.
- Profitability is meaningfully affected by geography (distance from growing regions raises landed cost) and by annual harvest quality/weather risk (e.g., the Georgia freeze that spiked costs one season).
- Not every stand works — she describes constant attrition (opening and closing stands) based on a roughly 3-week performance window before deciding to relocate.
- Access to some specific desirable orchards (e.g., a favorite East Texas orchard, Eufords) may be blocked by existing exclusive wholesaler agreements, forcing purchase through a broker rather than direct-from-farm pricing.
