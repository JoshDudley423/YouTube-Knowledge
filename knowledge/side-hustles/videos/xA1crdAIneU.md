# The Most Overlooked Way to Make Money with AI (+10 More Ideas)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xA1crdAIneU | Published: 2025-06-09

Freeform "business idea showdown" between Chris Koerner and guest Michael Girdley (girdley.com, x.com/girdley) — each pitches ideas back and forth, building on each other's, ending in a vote for favorites. Mostly rapid-fire concepts rather than tested businesses; include as brainstorm-stage ideas with the reasoning behind each, not proven case studies.

## AI-content-detection browser plugin ("Distrust")
- Idea: a browser plugin/extension that passively scans everything on your screen (web pages, inbox, video) and flags content likely to be AI-generated, e.g., with a red highlight/glow.
- Proposed price point: $20-$30/month.
- Extension idea (Koerner): create a governing/certification body (like an SSL certificate seal or ISO 9001) that charges other AI-wrapper apps and services a licensing/certification fee to be "graded and scored" for authenticity/trustworthiness — modeled on how arbitrary-seeming authority badges (e.g., a "Voted Florida's Best Dentist" sign) can meaningfully drive customer trust and referrals with zero other marketing.
- Voted a joint finalist/favorite in the episode; noted as buildable by "any vibe coder... in a weekend."

## Old-timey photo booths in bars/malls/tourist areas
- Idea: place vintage-style photo booths (the old Kodak/Polaroid-strip kiosk style) in bars, malls, or tourist areas — riding a trend/nostalgia wave against phone-camera/social-media fatigue.
- Evidence cited: a viral photo of long lines for old-timey photo booths near Times Square; personal observation of a bar in San Antonio where a wall was covered with hundreds/thousands of printed photo-booth strips from patrons; multiple standalone old-timey photo-booth retail storefronts in tourist towns like Pigeon Forge/Branson (paying an estimated $10,000+/month rent, and apparently sustaining that for years, implying profitability).
- Suggested test/validation framework: take a business model that thrives in an expensive tourist-trap location and test a lower-cost version elsewhere (a bar, a mall kiosk) at a fraction of the setup cost to see if demand holds outside the tourist context.
- Voted the other joint favorite/finalist; Girdley's rationale: very approachable to execute — "you just get a bar owner on the phone, buy the booth from China, and place it," no heavy customer acquisition problem like a plugin would face.

## Mini gold-panning / gem-mining kiosk (mall version of tourist attraction)
- Idea: shrink the classic tourist-town "gold panning/gem mining" attraction (a barrel/trough of dirt seeded with cheap gems, running water) into a small mall kiosk format (e.g., a high-traffic mall).
- Mechanics: charge ~$35-$45 per bag of dirt to pan; seed roughly 1 in 10 bags with a "big win" (a gold nugget or gift card) and publicize wins (a TV display, live announcements) to drive foot traffic and word-of-mouth.
- Cost data point: dedicated tourist-attraction mining setups from existing suppliers cost roughly $5,000-$15,000, though these are built for standalone tourist venues, not compact mall installation (would need adaptation, similar to needing just a drain and water hookup like a smoothie kiosk).
- Framed under a broader trend the hosts discuss: "pendulum swing" back toward in-person/IRL experiential spending (also cited: teens choosing to hang out at malls again after a decade of mall decline).

## Minority-stake "mini private equity" fund for small businesses
- Idea: create an investment fund that buys minority stakes (e.g., ~40%) in small/lower-middle-market businesses (target range: enterprise value ~$5-15 million, throwing off $2-3 million/year EBITDA) that are too small for institutional PE but whose owners want partial liquidity without a full sale or waiting for a family member to take over.
- Structure discussed: pay cash for a minority equity stake, structured with a preferred return, plus guardrails preventing the owner from paying themselves excessive salary; owner keeps control and upside on an eventual exit.
- Refinement suggested: focus on a specific niche (e.g., only home services/HVAC) and specific quality filters — a "Lindy" business that's been operating 20-30 years, shows consistent management, and has double-digit net margins.
- Risk flagged: adverse selection — the highly driven, "gritty" owner-operators who build the best businesses are exactly the ones least likely to want to cash out partial equity and potentially reduce their drive/hustle post-liquidity event; suggested (half-joking) mitigation: require prospective owners to score above a threshold on Angela Duckworth's "Grit Scale" test before proceeding with due diligence.

## "BizBuySell for businesses nobody thinks to sell" (special-situations marketplace)
- Idea: a marketplace specifically for small business owners who have mentally already resigned to closing their doors (rather than actively trying to sell) — many small owners (example given: a hairstylist with 75 regular clients) don't realize their business/client list/lease/equipment has any resale value at all.
- Structure: an eBay/auction-style listing site where opportunistic buyers can acquire a "starter kit" cheaply — an email list, a lease, a workshop full of equipment — rather than starting completely from scratch. Framed using deal-industry term "special situations" (distressed/unusual assets, as opposed to standard, competitively-marketed listings).
- Concrete lead-gen tactic proposed: scrape existing BizBuySell listings and filter for ones that have sat unsold for 365+ days — these owners already know their listing isn't attracting interest and may be receptive to a direct, low offer ("we both know the score here").

## AI-powered "EOS implementer" replacement
- Context: EOS (Entrepreneurial Operating System, from the book "Traction") is a business operating framework; the book/templates are cheap (~$15), but companies still pay human "implementers" (certified EOS facilitators) up to $10,000/day to run their planning sessions.
- Idea: build/train an AI (fed the entire EOS book/documentation) to act as the implementer, join a company's planning meeting via a bot bridged into Google Meet (listening/speaking via audio, treated by participants as if it were a human facilitator), and run the same structured process — priced at roughly $250/month instead of thousands per day.
- Identified technical requirement: an AI voice/audio bot that can join and participate in a live Google Meet call as a "participant," not just process a transcript afterward.
- Caveat raised: accountability — a human implementer's presence creates social pressure ("you're paying me, why aren't you doing the thing") that an AI may lack; suggested hybrid mitigation was a ratio of one human overseeing multiple AI-run sessions (e.g., 10:1). Also flagged: companies that hire implementers because they can't manage their own team dynamics may have a leadership problem an AI can't actually fix — and a leader unwilling to take feedback from an AI facilitator is itself a red flag.

## "AI-first EOS" — a new competing operating-system framework/brand
- Extension of the above: rather than build a tool that mimics EOS, create an entirely new competing framework, built AI-first from scratch, in the way Instagram (mobile-first) beat previously desktop-first products like Facebook.
- Tactic: feed an LLM the "Traction" book, extract its first-principles structure (visionary/integrator roles, etc.), and have it rewritten with AI/automation tooling (e.g., n8n, Lindy) as the operational backbone instead of human process alone.
- Distinct value proposition versus original EOS: instead of "pay $10,000/day to fix your $7M business's management," pitch is "pay $10,000/day and we'll show you the AI tools/automations that let you cut $30,000/month in payroll" (explicitly framed as helping companies replace headcount with AI tooling).

## Regional-language dubbing / localization of existing hit content ("Metastasis" model)
- Case study cited: "Metastasis," a Colombian cable-industry production that licensed the Breaking Bad script from Sony and remade the entire series scene-by-scene in Latin American Spanish with local actors on a fraction of the original budget — became hugely popular across Latin America. Also cited: The Office (UK to US remake), MrBeast paying native-language actors/dubbers (e.g., Korean) to recreate his English-language video content natively for regional channels (MrBeast Korea, etc.).
- General idea/framework: identify content that has already proven successful in one market/language and license/recreate/localize it for a different culture/language rather than creating something original from scratch — described as a repeatable, underexploited opportunity, with Spanish singled out as a high-leverage target language (alongside Chinese, English, Hindi, French) given market size.

## "Internationalization as a Service" (voice-cloned foreign-language content agency) — episode's top-voted idea
- Origin: Koerner noticed a copycat account in Canada/India (400,000 Instagram followers) republishing his exact content, verbatim-translated into Hindi, ~6 hours after each of his uploads — evidently profitable in that market despite lower ad-revenue-per-view than the US.
- Response: Koerner proactively launched "The Koerner Office Hindi" using ElevenLabs to clone his own voice, publishing his own content in Hindi himself (with his own face/likeness), to beat the copycat to market and capture that audience/monetization directly.
- Underlying market data point: Facebook has 2.1 billion daily active users; the U.S. alone represents ~220 million daily active Facebook users while India represents roughly ~400 million — a much larger audience pool even at a lower revenue-per-view rate. Facebook actively pays native creators to post Reels directly to the platform (Koerner cites earning $0.20 per 1,000 views) because it lacks enough good native content — described as an "everyone's ignoring Facebook" opportunity relative to Instagram-focused creators.
- The scalable business idea: build an agency that does this "internationalization" as a service for other creators — download/license a creator's existing content (with permission), voice-clone it with ElevenLabs into other languages, republish to Facebook/Instagram/etc. in that market, and take a 20-40% revenue share (discussed example: 50/50 split). Framed as needing minimal capital: a ~$50/month ElevenLabs account plus outreach/DM capability to sign up creators, then hiring offshore staff to run the playbook/publishing pipeline at scale.
- Framed as a "found money" business model (compared to Molson Hart's patent-infringement-lawsuit revenue-share business): zero cost/risk to the creator, pure upside, so it's an easy pitch to get creators to say yes.
- Vote outcome: tied for episode's top pick alongside the old-timey photo booth idea.

## "Mosquito Death Star" (consumer laser mosquito-zapper)
- Idea/product concept: a backyard device that uses a laser to detect and shoot down mosquitoes mid-flight, referencing a real, never-commercialized ~2010 Nathan Myhrvold (ex-Microsoft CTO, Intellectual Ventures) TED Talk demo of exactly this technology.
- Why it stalled 15 years ago: a laser strong enough to kill a mosquito from 30 feet away was also strong enough to risk blinding people, and the compute needed to reliably distinguish/target only mosquitoes (avoiding false hits) wasn't yet feasible or affordable — the hosts argue modern consumer GPU-level AI vision processing (e.g., an RTX 40-series-class chip) now makes safe, selective targeting realistic.
- Consumer appeal noted: strong novelty/viral potential ("one Instagram reel and you've sold thousands of units"), extra gamification/social ideas floated (a "kill counter" scoreboard, phone push notifications per mosquito zapped).
- Related idea (Girdley, building on the laser theme): a similar targeted laser already used commercially in China/Asia to trim tree branches away from power lines — proposed adapting the same targeting-safety logic (identify tree species/branch thickness via any smartphone-grade vision model, confirm no humans in range, then fire) to build safer consumer/commercial laser tree-trimming equipment, sold to or used within tree-trimming businesses to cut labor cost versus manual bucket-truck jobs (a comparison job cited: $3,000 for a 3-hour bucket-truck job).

## Pro-natalist venture capital fund
- Premise: US birth rate has fallen below the 2.1-per-woman replacement rate, down ~17% over the past 15 years; framed as a coming macro investment theme (cites Elon Musk and other wealthy figures publicly prioritizing this).
- Idea: raise a VC fund (standard "2 and 20" fee structure — cited example: a $100M fund generating ~$2M/year in management fees) specifically investing in "pro-natalist" technologies — anything that reduces the opportunity cost (financial and career/time cost) of having children: cheaper/easier partner-matching, conception, pregnancy/birth, child care, tutoring, schooling, and college.
- Discussion note: the hosts frame the underlying societal driver as parenting-time/expectation inflation (time spent on parenting activities has more than doubled over 50 years) rather than simply career ambition, and speculate that home robotics (automating cooking/cleaning) could eventually reverse birth-rate pressure on its own.

## Miscellaneous / non-replicable riffs (context only, not standalone business plans)
- "White foreigner as a service" in China/other markets: hiring Western-looking people to appear at store openings or as stand-up performers, leveraging cultural novelty/status bias (mentioned as a real historical phenomenon in China, not something the hosts are launching).
- A tongue-in-cheek "thought experiment" about exploiting Texas's low residency bar for county elections to take over the government of the real, extremely low-population Loving County, TX (24 residents, large oil-tax-driven surplus) — explicitly discussed as legally possible but flagged by both hosts as impractical/dangerous (personal safety and reputational risk cited) and not intended as an actual business recommendation.

## General takeaways from the format
- Both hosts explicitly frame themselves as "yes men" to any business idea in principle — the belief expressed is that almost any idea can work with the right marketing/execution, so the exercise values idea generation/riffing speed over immediate validation.
- Recurring evaluation heuristics used throughout: (1) is there already a working comparable model elsewhere (tourist-town precedent, existing viral proof) that could be tested cheaply in a new context; (2) does the model create "found money" (revenue the other party wouldn't otherwise get, making it an easy yes); (3) how approachable/executable is the idea for an average person (ease of customer acquisition and lack of dependency on hard-to-reach channels) — this was the explicit deciding factor between the top two finalist ideas.
