# Is Rucking the Next Gold Rush?
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pIiqnuQLBPw | Published: 2025-04-11

"HoldCo Bros" brainstorming episode (Chris Koerner + Nik) covering AI image-generation business ideas, a general framework for choosing which market/business to enter, and a detailed brainstorm around the rucking (weighted backpack walking) fitness trend.

## Business ideas built on AI image generation (GPT-4o image editing, at time of filming)
- Context: ChatGPT's image generation had just gained the ability to actually edit existing photos (not just generate new ones) and to render legible text/words/numbers within images — described as a recent, fast-moving capability jump (with competitors Grok/X AI and DeepSeek releasing similar features around the same time).
- **Resume-to-infographic micro-SaaS**: build a simple site (domain example floated: visualres.com) where a user uploads their resume for $5 and receives an AI-generated visual infographic version of it (same information, more attractive presentation) with unlimited revisions; capture the user's email at delivery to enable upselling into a recurring product (e.g., a $9.99/month app offering ongoing image regeneration/editing). Rationale given: resume/job-related SaaS products reliably perform well because users are in an active, spend-motivated "bettering my life" mindset. Suggested build tools: Cursor, Replit, or Lovable — described as simple to build.
- **AI-assisted family portrait service for photographers**: let clients send candid/casual family photos to their photographer throughout the year; at year-end, the photographer uses AI (with a simple survey collecting family details, favorite places, and desired photo style/theme) to merge and stylize the images into a finished family portrait, with unlimited free revisions (adjust background, remove a blemish, etc.) instead of needing a single perfect in-person photo shoot. Positioned as something existing photographers should adopt to stay relevant ("photographers are toast" without adapting these tools) rather than a pure disruption play.
- **"Lo-fi"/deliberately low-tech counter-positioning idea**: as AI-polished marketing/visual content becomes ubiquitous and cheap, the ability to stand out may shift toward *intentionally* low-production, low-tech-looking creative (referenced a viral job-posting ad that was deliberately drawn in MS Paint, and the broader trend of underproduced short-form video outperforming overproduced content) — floated as a possible "lo-fi wrapper" product/tool built specifically to produce that raw, non-polished aesthetic on demand, as a countercyclical bet against increasingly AI-perfected content.
- General framing: expect a new wave of niche "ChatGPT wrapper" apps/tools to emerge purely riding this one feature (AI image editing) the same way tools have historically been built quickly around other single new model capabilities.

## Framework for choosing which business/market to enter (not idea-specific, but a general decision-making tool)
- Proposed formula: Success = Idea + Execution, where Execution breaks down into four components — Grit, Distribution, Fulfillment, and Knowledge/Experience. To succeed, you need a genuine competitive advantage in at least one of these areas (or in the idea itself); simply working hard is "table stakes," not a differentiator, especially against established competitors.
- Counter-argument/addition from the other host: the size and timing of the underlying market/trend ("tidal wave") you choose to ride can matter more than a unique execution edge — cited example: a home-services video creator who built a following purely by mowing/pressure-washing lawns for free and filming time-lapses, with no differentiation from thousands of similar creators, but who accumulated 45 million followers over ~2.5 years simply by being an early, consistent participant in a booming content format/demand wave (with the counter-caveat that pure copying still requires sustained "grit" — showing up and producing content for years before results compound — and that a true copycat with zero execution edge, e.g., no video editing skill, would plausibly get a smaller but still meaningful slice of that opportunity, e.g., 4-20 million followers rather than 45 million).
- Practical implication discussed: entering a market later/with more existing competitors generally lowers your probability of success, requires more resources, and lengthens the learning curve versus entering early — but a sufficiently large, growing "tidal wave" market can still absorb many undifferentiated entrants.
- **"Barbell strategy" for market/skill selection** (both hosts endorse this explicitly): when choosing where to build a competitive edge, go to one of the two extremes — either a fully AI/tech-driven approach, or a fully manual/no-tech, hands-on trade (e.g., plumbing, HVAC, framing/construction) — and avoid the messy, increasingly-automatable middle. Reasoning: skilled manual trades are unlikely to be displaced by robotics/AI within the next decade, and AI adoption in a given industry (like home services) will reduce the number of competitors over time even if overall market size doesn't necessarily grow, making both extremes relatively less crowded than the middle ground.
- On learning-by-doing vs. apprenticing first: the hosts disagree by personality type. One (self-described "hustler") advocates just starting immediately, arguing that people who first seek to learn/apprentice under someone already in an industry often talk themselves out of the opportunity by absorbing that person's accumulated pessimism/blind spots (illustrated by an anecdote: a home builder dismissed a viral outreach tactic — doing free erosion-control/silt-fence repair work as an unsolicited goodwill gesture to land future jobs — as unworkable from his own narrow experience, when the tactic's actual audience is the thousands of other builders who might respond well to it). The other host argues the reverse: taking a lower-paying job in a target industry first lets you make costly early mistakes on someone else's dime rather than your own (referencing the host's own costly early mistakes learning phone-repair sourcing/logistics by trial and error). Conclusion offered: there's no universally correct answer — it depends on individual personality/risk tolerance, but either path still requires *actually learning the fundamentals of running the business*, not skipping straight to hiring an outsourced operator to run something you don't understand yourself.
- Mentioned in passing: an "entrepreneurship quiz" concept for a website/newsletter to help users self-diagnose which path fits them (start a business / buy a business / invest in a business / run a side hustle first because you're not ready to commit).

## Rucking (weighted-backpack fitness walking) business brainstorm
- Trend context: rucking (walking with a weighted backpack, historically popular in a niche way among ultramarathon runners) is becoming mainstream; GoRuck, the dominant branded player, reportedly does roughly $50 million/year in sales via a Shopify store selling ~$300 backpacks and ~$75 individual weight plates.
- Core critique/opportunity identified: GoRuck's core product is functionally "just a backpack with weights," priced at a steep premium ($400-500+ all-in for a bag plus weights) — hosts view this as an inflated-margin category ripe for a cheaper alternative, similar to how expensive niche fitness/lifestyle products (e.g., Stanley tumblers) become popular partly through momentum/social proof rather than unique product merit.
- **DIY validation anecdote**: host tested rucking cheaply himself using a ~$24 generic backpack (bought via Amazon, brand "Tazio") loaded with concrete pavers from Home Depot (~$1.78 for a 17 lb paver) — total cost roughly $30-40 out the door, versus $400-500+ for the branded equivalent, with no meaningfully worse experience (no strap/flap issues even while moving quickly).
- **Barbell-strategy productization ideas for the rucking niche specifically** (mirroring the general framework above):
  - Ultra-cheap/budget end: source a basic generic backpack from a Chinese manufacturer/Alibaba (knockoffs likely already exist), pair it with cheap dense weights (concrete/paver-style), and market it with self-aware, humorous branding (e.g., leaning into nostalgia — "feel like you're running to catch the school bus again" — rather than pretending it's premium). Explicit point made: much of the branded product's cost is likely shipping-related (importing dedicated weight plates), so sourcing the heavy weight component *locally* (e.g., via a checkout-automation "wrapper" that places a Home Depot paver order to ship to the customer's home alongside the backpack, rather than shipping heavy weights from overseas) could meaningfully cut costs.
  - Ultra-premium end: an all-natural-materials version (all-cotton construction, no synthetic microplastics, no lead/paint in weights) marketed like a durable, lifetime-warranty outdoor brand (comparison drawn to Patagonia).
  - Digital-product-only option: a $20 instructional PDF/ebook teaching people how to assemble their own DIY rucking setup cheaply (what to buy at Home Depot, workout programming tips) rather than selling any physical product at all.
  - Affiliate/dropship-style hybrid: build a simple tool/wrapper that helps a customer order a backpack (affiliate link) and the weight plates (direct from Home Depot, shipped to their home) together in one flow, monetizing via affiliate commission without holding any inventory.
  - **Activity-tracking niche app idea**: a Strava-like tracking/social app specific to rucking — pitched because mainstream fitness trackers (Garmin, etc.) don't have a dedicated activity mode for rucking (it registers as generic walking/hiking despite the elevated heart rate from the added weight), leaving a gap for niche-specific tracking, or alternatively a simple rucking workout-scheduling app.
  - **Influencer-seeding/affiliate play**: identify the most prominent rucking-focused fitness influencers, send them free branded backpacks, and set them up with affiliate links for anyone who buys through their audience — explicitly modeled on how accessory ecosystems (referenced: pickleball) have grown around a suddenly-popular activity.
  - **Audience research tactic**: use an existing newsletter/audience to informally poll however many subscribers already ruck about their single biggest pain point with the activity/gear, and build a narrow product specifically targeting that one complaint.
- General framing/caveat acknowledged directly by the hosts: rucking itself is not a novel invention (just a weighted backpack, a "rebranded" old idea) and its rising popularity may be driven substantially by social/trend momentum ("it's popular because it's popular") rather than the product itself being uniquely superior — comparable to how Stanley tumblers became a cultural phenomenon.

## Caveats
- No revenue/execution results are reported for any of the AI-image or rucking ideas discussed — this is a pure ideation/brainstorming episode, not a documented case study.
- The competitive-advantage framework and barbell strategy are the hosts' personal heuristics, presented as opinion/debate rather than empirically validated principles.
