# How to Start a $20k/Month Email Marketing Agency
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GL3MQMC0Rs4 | Published: 2024-12-15

Chris Koerner answers listener-submitted voice questions (Q&A format) covering several distinct business ideas. Despite the title, the episode covers 7 separate questions — the Klaviyo/email agency answer is only one segment.

## Claw machine business (Crystal, New Zealand)
- Context: Crystal has access to a claw machine and can stock it with anything (from stuffed animals to luxury items), and asked how to turn it into "hype."
- Key fact revealed: most modern claw machines are deliberately programmed to give a strong/winnable grip only after roughly 10-15 unsuccessful plays — meaning average win probability across all plays is only about 5-30%, but a player who watches 10-15 failed attempts first and then plays has a meaningfully higher chance (Chris estimates 30-60%) since the machine is now "due" for a winnable grip.
- Recommended approach: don't put single high-value luxury items in (too obvious/costly if lost); instead fill the machine with "mystery" items (sealed packs — sports cards, Pokémon cards) with a sign advertising that one specific pack in the machine contains a known, verifiably valuable item (e.g., a real $1,000+ rare holographic card), and physically insert that real card into one sealed pack yourself so the claim is literally true (never advertise a prize that isn't actually in there).
- Worked numeric example given: buy 100 packs of cards; one contains a genuine $1,000+ rare card; if average per-pack cost is ~$3-5 and win probability is calibrated (e.g., via testing) at ~10%, charging ~$2/play, it would take ~1,000 plays to exhaust the 100 packs, generating ~$2,000 in revenue against ~$1,300 in total cost (packs + the rare card) — roughly 40% profit margin in his illustrative math.
- Process to determine real win probability before launching: build a spreadsheet, have several different people (kids, spouse, self) play repeatedly with different item types (a ball may be easier/harder to grab than a stack of cards) and log actual win rates; use that measured probability x cost-to-play to calculate what prize value you can afford to offer.
- Suggested extra tactic: add a motion sensor that triggers a sound/attention-grabber when someone walks by, to draw notice to a high-value prize claim; continuously rotate/test inventory (weekly or monthly) since what appeals to kids vs. teens vs. adults will differ by venue.
- General framing: this is a probability/math-driven business, directly comparable to a hole-in-one golf challenge business Chris covered previously (a floating green in New Zealand paying $10,000 for a hole-in-one, with roughly 1-in-12,500 odds, paying out about every 2 weeks, grossing ~$600K/year and netting roughly a third of that) — profitability comes from knowing the real odds and pricing/payout against them, not guesswork.

## Recovering from burnout / business failure (Robert)
- Context: Robert's niche visual-effects studio business (film/TV post-production work, clients included Netflix/HBO) collapsed when the VFX industry contracted; he's struggling with burnout and rebuilding identity/motivation.
- Chris's framework (mindset, not a business tactic): (1) intentionally cultivate a "chip on your shoulder" from setbacks/betrayals and use it as fuel rather than letting it be purely painful; (2) take inventory of what you naturally think about when you don't have to think about anything (open a notes app and log recurring interests) as a way to identify genuine passions vs. merely tolerated work; (3) recognizes burnout often signals the prior work wasn't a true passion, and that "not doing your passion is not scalable" long-term; (4) if you love business/entrepreneurship itself (rather than one specific craft), that itself is a viable "passion" that opens many possible business options.

## Mobile oil-change business (Scott)
- Business: on-site oil changes (bring the oil, filter, do the full job, remove old oil) — cited as a genuinely growing category with real agency clients scaling in it.
- Pricing approaches given:
  1. Hourly-rate-backed pricing: decide your target hourly rate (e.g., $50/hr including drive time), add your per-job cost (e.g., $25 for oil/filter), and price accordingly (~$75/job in that example).
  2. Market-based pricing: call other mobile oil-change competitors (even in different cities/states) to benchmark price, since a mobile/convenience service commands a premium over a fixed shop regardless of the provider's actual lower overhead (compared explicitly to food trucks charging more than the food quality justifies purely for convenience) — explicitly warns against underpricing just because your overhead (a $40K van with a $500/month payment) is lower than a shop's.
  3. Cost-based pricing: separate fixed costs (van payment, insurance) from variable costs (gas, oil, materials) and price to cover both plus margin.
- Scaling note: once employing others to do the actual oil changes, expect a lower personal hourly return per job (e.g., ~$25/hr instead of $50/hr) but higher total hourly income if running multiple trucks simultaneously (e.g., 3 trucks at $25/hr net = 50% more effective hourly income than working solo at $50/hr).
- Marketing tip: Chris states from direct experience that mobile oil change and mobile brake repair/mobile mechanic services perform very well specifically in local Facebook groups.

## Perfume vending machine investment decision (Rehan, Toronto)
- Context: Rehan has ~$20,000 CAD (~$17,700 USD... [Chris quoted the figure loosely]) in total savings, 3 months of runway, a family of 3 kids, was laid off, and is considering investing a large chunk of it in a perfume vending machine (from Chris's own company).
- Chris's direct advice: do NOT buy one under these circumstances, despite being the seller — deems it too large a share of very limited total savings for a slow-ramping asset, given no other income.
- Realistic ramp-up numbers given for a perfume vending machine: ~$500-$1,000 revenue in month 1, ~$700-$1,200 in month 2, meaning ~3-4 months just to recoup the machine's cost, and only ~$1,000-$2,000/month profit thereafter — not enough to support a family in Canada as a sole income source.
- Recommended alternative: get a stable job to cover living expenses first, then build a side/service business (online or in-person) nights and weekends until it can support replacing the job — rather than betting a large share of remaining savings on one illiquid, slow-to-ramp asset.

## Business "accountability partner" service (Sean)
- Idea: a paid accountability-coaching service for business owners/entrepreneurs (checking in on whether they made their calls, sent emails, filed their LLC, etc.), modeled on existing $400-$900/month health/fitness accountability coaching services.
- Chris's skepticism: he treats "nobody else appears to be doing this" as a mild red flag rather than a sign of white space — prefers ideas with prior iterations to learn from (cites Google, Apple, Facebook as famous non-first-movers, while acknowledging they're outliers).
- Pricing-tier concern: the $400-$900/month range is described as an awkward "messy middle" — expensive high-ticket business/life coaches ($5K-$50K) succeed partly by using price itself as forcing-function motivation, while cheap ($9-$50/month) automation/app tools serve the low end; the middle tier is hard to justify and hard to staff profitably (a human accountability coach costing $300-400/month to hire wouldn't leave the business owner much margin).
- Alternative pivot suggested: build this as an AI-powered app/"ChatGPT wrapper" instead of a human-coach service, potentially with a financial-commitment mechanic — e.g., the user deposits money (e.g., $1,000) upfront, and if they fail to complete stated goals within a set period, the app either donates the money to charity or keeps it as its monetization method (making the app itself potentially free otherwise, profiting from users' failures to hit their stated goals).
- General instruction: since Sean already has a landing page live, just launch and test for real signups/churn rather than only theorizing.

## Klaviyo/email marketing agency for e-commerce (Jack, Austin) — the titular idea
- Business: build a service business managing Klaviyo (a publicly traded, Shopify-centric email marketing automation platform; comparable/competing tools mentioned: Sendlane) for e-commerce store owners on a monthly retainer.
- Chris still considers this a solid, durable opportunity a year after first recommending it in a December 2023 newsletter ("Your Exact Path to $20K per Month in 3 Months") — his framework is that most real business opportunities persist over time rather than disappearing.
- Two core Klaviyo feature types to learn: **Flows** (automations triggered by user behavior, e.g., following up with someone who signed up but never purchased) and **Campaigns** (one-off broadcast emails to a list/segment).
- Recommended launch sequence:
  1. Create a free Klaviyo "dummy" account and learn the tool via its tutorials (calls it fun and intuitive software, not difficult to learn).
  2. Find a friend with an existing e-commerce/Shopify store and implement Klaviyo for them for free as a test case; use Klaviyo's built-in attribution reporting to document real revenue generated.
  3. Use that case study to approach strangers in relevant Facebook groups of Shopify store owners — target businesses already using Klaviyo but likely underutilizing it (estimated 80%+ of Klaviyo users only scratch the surface of its capability) rather than trying to convince non-users to adopt a new tool, since that's a much easier sell.
  4. Do another round or two for strangers for free, document results, then start charging (any starting price — e.g., $200-$500/month), raise prices over time, and ask happy clients for referrals.
  5. For more advanced list-building: use BuiltWith (a tool that identifies which technology/platforms a website runs on) to scrape a list of stores using Shopify.
- Cold-emailing larger Shopify Plus stores directly is explicitly discouraged as oversaturated/low-response; the recommended channel is Facebook groups of store owners rather than cold outbound.

## Bike-powered smoothie business (Anonymous, rural area)
- Inspiration cited: a Dubai concept — a shipping-container kiosk with 2-3 stationary bikes out front, each rigged so pedaling powers a blender mounted on the handlebars; customer puts fresh fruit in and blends their own smoothie using their own pedaling energy.
- Why it works economically: cuts labor in half (normally 2 staff — one on payment, one making smoothies — but the bike setup only requires one, since the customer does the blending), while simultaneously supporting a premium price because it's sold as an experience, not just a beverage; also inherently generates free social-media marketing (customers filming/posting themselves using a novel bike-blender, ideally branded).
- Chris's staged rollout recommendation (don't start with the $14,000 camper/trailer the asker already found): 
  1. Start at a local farmers market with a basic stationary-bike setup — typical cost to post up with a 10x10 tent at a farmers market is $50-$250 (sometimes free for local residents).
  2. Use farmers-market runs for direct face-to-face customer feedback (bike too flimsy, too hard to pedal, insufficient torque, fruit freshness/quality issues, smoothie taste) at low financial risk (~$100 downside per test).
  3. Feed collected feedback into ChatGPT to help synthesize what to change.
  4. Once validated, move up to investing in the bigger $14,000 camper/trailer setup and take the business to festivals for a different, larger customer base and more feedback.
  5. Progress further to locations where food trucks typically operate, and eventually consider a fixed permanent location if demand continues to justify it.

## General principle repeated across answers
- "Test everything except drugs": Chris's recurring advice across nearly every idea in this episode is to run a small, cheap, real-world test (farmers market, free pilot client, small live financial stake) before committing significant capital or scaling, and to use direct customer feedback to iterate rather than theorizing.
