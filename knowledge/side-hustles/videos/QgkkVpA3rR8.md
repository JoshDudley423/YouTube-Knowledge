# He Makes $4K/Day With This Weird Middleman Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QgkkVpA3rR8 | Published: 2026-04-28

Interview with William Lindholm (Daymaker, daymaker.com; LinkedIn/IG: William Lindholm; contact@daymaker.com), founder of a "cold caking" business — sending physical cakes to prospects/companies as an outbound sales tool to book meetings.

## The idea
- "Cold caking": send a physical cake (instead of/alongside a cold email or call) to a target prospect or company to cut through digital outreach noise, get attention, and book a sales meeting. Framed as a modern, AI-noise-resistant twist on corporate/physical gifting.
- Started as an unrelated pivot: originally a birthday-cake delivery automation service for companies (to celebrate employee birthdays/milestones) that began in Norway and initially failed to gain traction when transplanted to San Francisco; the founder pivoted the same cake-sourcing/delivery infrastructure into B2B sales outreach ("cold caking") instead, and that pivot is what took off.
- Secondary/emerging use cases beyond outbound prospecting: "retention cakes" (thank-you gifts to existing long-term customers to boost referrals/loyalty), agency lead-gen (attaching a QR code on the cake linking to a personalized audit, e.g., an SEO or AI audit of the recipient's business), and real estate (sending a cake to a listing agent shortly before their listing is about to expire, to win the relisting).

## Numbers
- Company age at time of interview: ~6 months total; the pivot into cold-caking/sales happened ~6 weeks before the interview.
- First 2.5 months in the US (before pivoting to cold-caking): $0 in sales; founder nearly shut the company down.
- After pivoting and going viral (sending "cold cakes" to VCs like a16z and Sequoia, including one with a friend's pitch deck baked onto a 100-piece cake that got ~300,000 views on X and hit European tech news): revenue reached $120,000 in a single month, roughly 6 weeks post-pivot, with $30,000-40,000 of that as profit (~25-33% margin).
- Passive side revenue: the original Norway birthday-cake business still runs on autopilot, invoicing ~$12,000/month, run largely by the founder's mother doing customer service.
- Norway origin numbers: from a single cold-calling session at a sales competition, booked 17 meetings in ~4 hours (out of a 5-hour, 5-round elimination cold-calling contest with ~50 competitors); grew the original birthday-cake B2B product to $150,000 ARR in Norway before it stalled and the US pivot happened.
- Conversion data claimed: standard cold calling converts at roughly 2-3% on average (varies wildly by product — could be ~30% for something like t-shirts, ~0.3% for $30K/month software); cakes sent by Daymaker have converted into booked meetings at ~35%.
- Example large order: a dental-industry software company (archie.com) ordered 1,000 cakes in a single week, sent to dental offices across San Francisco, Florida, and Houston — this order followed a similarly large (and reportedly very profitable, "tens of thousands of dollars") cake/pizza campaign the company had run previously, which served as their internal pilot test.
- Cake pricing at volume: roughly $50-70 per cake at large-order volume (per the archie.com-scale deal).
- At time of interview: 45 inbound leads in the pipeline (requests for cakes in Australia, Canada, Spain, Mexico) that the small team can't yet fulfill, prompting a fundraising/hiring push rather than staying purely bootstrapped.
- Some customers requesting other physical gifts beyond cakes (e.g., ping pong tables) and asking for recurring integrations with their CRM (HubSpot, Salesforce).

## Process / how to do it
1. Identify a target list of prospects/companies for a given campaign (VC firms, dental offices, SEO agency clients, etc.).
2. Send an initial outreach email or text a few days ahead confirming the recipient's shipping address ("Hey, in 2 days we're sending you a cake — is this the right address?").
3. Ship the physical cake (locally sourced via a bakery network — typically kept within ~30 miles of the recipient for freshness/logistics; for special/remote cases, cakes have been flown in on a commercial flight to reach a hard-to-source location).
4. Follow up by email or phone after delivery to convert the attention into a booked meeting.
5. For agency/consulting use cases: attach a QR code directly on the cake linking to a personalized audit (e.g., a Loom-recorded SEO audit of the recipient's website, or an "AI audit" pitch) as the call-to-action, rather than a generic pitch.
6. Sourcing/logistics is run almost entirely by an AI agent workflow (see Tools section) rather than manual bakery outreach — this is what let the company scale from 1 bakery partner to ~15 bakery partners in a single month.
7. Quality control: the company deliberately partners only with higher-end bakeries (e.g., "organic French patisseries") rather than the cheapest options, because cake quality is seen as a direct reflection of the sending company's brand/quality — cheap bakeries are turned away even if available.
8. Business-model guidance for someone wanting to copy the underlying principle without building a cake company: physical gifting/high-effort outreach only makes sense economically when (a) there's a marketing/virality angle to the stunt, and (b) the average contract value or LTV of what you're selling is high enough to justify a real physical-gift cost (e.g., B2B software, agency services, real estate commissions — not low-margin products like t-shirts).
9. Concrete idea suggested during the interview: real estate agents could pull a daily list of soon-to-expire property listings and send a cake to the listing agent about a week before expiration to win the relisting — rationale: even a $500K home relisting nets $10-20K commission, making an ~$80 cake trivially worth it even at a 10% conversion rate.
10. General "attention is everything" philosophy voiced by the founder: doing unusual/outsized stunts and posting about them publicly (e.g., a giant unicorn cake, or an unrelated stunt — renting a llama and hiring a jazz band for a founder dinner in Oslo) tends to go viral and generates free PR, which in turn creates inbound demand from other companies who saw the stunt work.

## Tools/platforms/resources named
- Claude Cowork — used to run a "super prompt" AI agent that autonomously manages bakery sourcing and negotiation (checks email every 2 hours; if no bakery is yet secured for a target location, it sources ~20 new bakery candidates, evaluates them via reviews, and negotiates pricing/discounts by email).
- Superhuman (via MCP integration) — the email system the AI agent uses to send/receive/negotiate bakery outreach emails.
- The agent's negotiation flow: sends bakeries an opening email like "we want to buy 200 cakes in 2 weeks, can we make it happen?", then asks for a volume discount; if the bakery's offered discount is good, the agent escalates it to the founder's personal email for a manual thumbs-up/thumbs-down approval, and if thumbs-down, it automatically continues renegotiating with the bakery.
- Founders Inc (an accelerator/community) — where the founder was based in SF when the VC "cold caking" stunt happened.
- playmakersai.com — Chris Koerner's own separate AI-consulting community (mentioned by Koerner, not Daymaker) for people wanting to start AI implementation/consulting agencies and use similar acquisition tactics.

## Caveats / risks
- Cultural fit matters: the original Norway version of the idea (automated employee birthday cakes) failed to gain traction in the US specifically because, per the founder, US company budgets prioritize sales and engineering almost exclusively — employee-morale spending on birthdays wasn't seen as a high enough priority/pain point to buy, despite going semi-viral in Norwegian press with zero resulting inbound interest.
- Only offers with genuine pain/urgency (or a clear revenue-generating angle, like booking a sales meeting) convert into actual paying interest — media virality and public attention alone did not translate into inbound sales in the original birthday-cake product, a pattern the founder saw repeat in both Norway and the US.
- The tactic is explicitly framed as time-limited/self-cannibalizing at scale: the founder predicts cakes will become overused ("everyone's going to get five cakes a day") and lose effectiveness as the novelty fades, which is why the company is branded "Daymaker" rather than a cake-specific name, and why they're already testing other gift formats (e.g., ping pong tables) per customer request.
- No hard data yet on downstream close rates: the founder acknowledges he doesn't have confirmed data on whether cake-sourced meetings convert to closed deals at a higher rate than cold-called meetings — this remains an assumption/hypothesis, not proven with numbers, though the founder plans to start collecting and publishing that data.
- The cake itself does not close the sale — it only gets the meeting/attention; the actual sales execution afterward is on the customer, not something Daymaker's service provides.
- The company was not profitable/self-sustaining as of the interview by the founder's own admission ("we're cooked"/"we're baked") — actively raising money to keep up with a backlog of ~45 unfulfilled inbound leads rather than bootstrapping off the existing ~$120K/month with $30-40K profit, despite acknowledging that with net-30 payment terms to bakeries and upfront customer payment, the business could plausibly self-fund/bootstrap instead.
- The founder notes this is a copyable model — competitors have already reached out saying they intend to compete directly, though he considers execution (not the idea) the actual moat, and floats potential partnership/franchise-style splits with copycats rather than treating it as a defensible secret.
