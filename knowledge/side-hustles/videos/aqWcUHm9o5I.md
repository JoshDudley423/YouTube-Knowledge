# Turning $20K into $4.7M with an AirBnB
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aqWcUHm9o5I | Published: 2024-08-16

Guest: Isaac French, developer of Live Oak Lake, a 7-cabin "micro-resort" short-term rental property near Waco, TX, sold to private equity. Co-hosted with Nik (Nikonomics).

Business idea: build a unique, design-forward, story-driven "micro-resort" (a small cluster of architecturally distinctive cabins/units) as a short-term rental destination, rather than a commodity Airbnb listing, then monetize both direct bookings (via a built social media following) and eventual sale of the whole turnkey property + brand + audience to an institutional buyer.

Key numbers and timeline:
- Started in 2021 with about $20,000 in personal savings. Found a 5-acre property (mature live oak trees, an existing small pond) near Waco via Zillow, got it under contract same day.
- Raised the rest of the capital from 4-5 family members, initially seeking a hard-money loan (to repay after refinancing), but family insisted on equity instead — gave up 40-45% equity in the project as a result.
- Also built and sold a separate $750,000 spec home during the same period in 4.5 months, netting about $200,000 profit, which he rolled into the resort project to cover a significant cost overrun (was 35-40% over the original budget, itself likely underestimated).
- Designed and built the 7-cabin "modern Scandinavian" resort himself with no architects or outside consultants (leveraged prior hands-on general-contracting/construction experience), in 9.5 months, during which he fell off a ladder and broke his pelvis (multiple surgeries, permanent stainless-steel hardware).
- Opened January 2022, launched on Airbnb 2 weeks later. Despite immediate strong bookings/reviews, Airbnb suspended the listing entirely (grayed out, no clear explanation) for 6 days — described as a mistake on Airbnb's part; customer support was unhelpful throughout.
- During the suspension, pivoted hard toward building direct bookings: paid a Dallas-based travel Instagram influencer account (~200,000 followers) $900 to run a 2-night stay giveaway. Result: roughly 5,000 new Instagram followers and $40,000 in direct bookings within about a week — cited as the single most pivotal moment of the whole venture, since it revealed the leverage of building a following/brand around the property instead of relying on Airbnb.
- Over the following ~12 months, grew to about 100,000 Instagram followers, roughly half from continuing similar influencer giveaways; total giveaway spend was about $50,000, which drove roughly $800,000 in direct bookings.
- First full year results: ~80% of all occupancy was direct (non-OTA) bookings; overall occupancy across all 7 cabins hit 94-95% for the full 365 days; about $1 million in topline revenue; about $550,000 NOI.
- Refinanced the property about 6 months after opening — the post-build appraisal came in roughly double the pre-build value, allowing them to pull out all invested cash plus an additional $400,000.
- 18 months after opening, sold the entire turnkey property (real estate + brand + ~150,000-follower Instagram following + a small "mist"/other assets) to a private equity buyer for $7 million (built for $2.3M total, i.e., roughly $1M/key vs. $330K/key build cost) — net profit of $4.7 million. The buyer explicitly valued the built social following alone at around $2 million, citing plans to either expand the brand or leverage the following to launch additional properties.

Strategic philosophy/contrarian points made:
- Deliberately avoided high-barrier-to-entry/highly regulated markets (the conventional STR investing wisdom, meant to block copycats) — chose an unincorporated part of the county specifically because it required no permitting, prioritizing speed to market and proximity to a large population base (the "Texas Triangle," 22M+ people) over regulatory moats.
- His alternative "moat" thesis: build something so genuinely unique and "word-of-mouth worthy" that competitors building nearby don't matter, because you already have first-mover brand recognition and a differentiated, non-commodity experience — argues word-of-mouth is the best marketing channel, and social media/influencers merely amplify it.
- Deliberately excluded TVs from every cabin (despite high-speed WiFi being available) as part of an intentional "digital detox"/nature-immersion design choice, explicitly against conventional hospitality wisdom; credits countless small intentional design decisions like this (from entry gate to welcome gift to trail signage) for creating an experience people returned to repeatedly (some guests returned 3-4 times within 12 months).
- Frames the broader industry opportunity as an early-stage secular shift in travel/hospitality demand toward "experiences over possessions," arguing large legacy hospitality brands (Hilton, Hyatt, Four Seasons) structurally cannot replicate small-scale, highly bespoke micro-resort experiences, evidenced by big-money acquisitions of smaller unique-stay brands (cites Graduate Hotels selling for ~$200M for the brand alone across 50 properties with no real estate included, and Auto Camp's institutional exits).
- Believes Instagram (not Twitter/YouTube) will be the primary booking-discovery channel for unique-stay properties specifically, due to its visual, story-driven format being well-suited to selling an experience rather than a commodity room.

Business ideas/gaps in the market Isaac flagged as opportunities for others to pursue:
1. A polished, design-forward direct-booking website/engine ("Shopify for short-term rentals" / "the Amazon-to-Airbnb's-Shopify gap") — existing property management system (PMS) providers (named: Hostaway, Guesty, Hospitable, OwnerRez) all include a direct-booking widget, but he describes them universally as poorly designed ("trash," "Home Depot Orange"), a significant pain point repeatedly raised within his ~55-person community of active short-term rental builders/entrepreneurs. Suggests monetization via a percentage of bookings plus a monthly SaaS fee; floats potential expansion into revenue management/dynamic pricing and smart-home automation. Named examples of well-designed but non-licensable in-house booking sites: Bolt Farm Treehouse (boltfarmtreehouse.com) and Wanderer (wanderer.com). Named a UK-based agency already doing custom direct-booking site builds for STR operators: Mark Simpson's agency at boostly.co.uk (noted as functional/good enough for most properties but not high-end design-focused). Also named W.co (Austin-based startup) as a company solving a related problem: booking/payment integration for on-site guest experiences (kayak guides, local activity vendors) plugged into a property's site.
2. An influencer-matchmaking/talent agency specifically pairing short-term rental (or other) properties/brands with under-monetized niche influencers for sponsored content and giveaways — floated collaboratively during the interview; premise is that mid-tier influencers (example cited: a lawn-care-niche Instagram influencer with 40,000 followers earning ~$3,000 for a single sponsored post) are still significantly underpriced relative to their value, the market is highly fragmented, and it's genuinely hard for brands to vet and coordinate influencer partnerships at scale — an agency could vet creators and businesses and pair them for a cut, or even build/incubate its own influencer accounts in the niche.
3. A "social media retreat compound" — a destination property designed explicitly around hosting meetups/retreats between a well-known online creator/influencer and their audience (e.g., a weekend with a specific YouTuber), engineered around highly shareable photo/video moments, elevated food presentation, and potentially an educational component.

Other notes:
- Isaac explicitly says there was no market research or comparable case study before building Live Oak Lake — decision was conviction-driven ("a dream that was crystallized into a hardcore conviction"), acknowledging this was a large, undiversified bet.
- Attributes part of his entrepreneurial capability to growing up in an intentional agrarian Christian community (Homestead Heritage) with no TVs, video games, or unrestricted screens/phones as a child, heavy emphasis on traditional skills (woodworking, blacksmithing, growing food) and early entrepreneurial experience (e.g., saving to buy his first flock of ducks at age 5) — argues removing passive entertainment consumption created space for creativity and a strong personal-responsibility/work-ethic foundation transferable to running a business like this.
- General caveat raised in the discussion: heavy startup capital availability (e.g., large VC rounds) can remove the forcing function of creative problem-solving under real constraint — the Airbnb suspension crisis is framed as a costly-but-valuable forced pivot that wouldn't have happened absent that specific setback.
