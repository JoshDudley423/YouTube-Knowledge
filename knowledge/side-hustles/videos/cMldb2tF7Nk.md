# How to Find More Home Service Customers with Google Street View
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cMldb2tF7Nk | Published: 2024-03-09

Live call-in Q&A format ("Koerner's Corner") -- Chris Koerner gives advice to 5 different callers pitching/building specific businesses.

## 1. Virtual receptionist for home service businesses (caller: Corey/Andy)
- Model: a "Ruby Receptionist"-style virtual answering service, but narrowly targeted at home service businesses.
- General pattern Koerner recommends: watch big generalist platforms (Ruby, Upwork) that took ~a decade to become ubiquitous, then "unbundle" them -- offer the same core service to one narrow sub-segment of their customer base.
- Caller's mistake: "home service" is too broad -- ~20 million home service businesses exist in the US (out of 32-33 million total US businesses; 50-60% of all US businesses are home-service). Advice: pick a much narrower niche (e.g., gutter cleaning specifically), but the *business name* can stay broad while the *offer/pitch* is niche.
- Customer acquisition method recommended: ringless voicemail drops (tool named: SlyBroadcast / "sbc.com" -- described as used personally, not sponsored). Process: draft 5 different voicemail scripts (either 5 industries with the same script, or 5 scripts within one industry), send to ~1,000 total contacts (200 each), track response.
- Script advice: use humor, an aggressive/attention-grabbing hook ("hey, you didn't answer your phone"), dynamic non-monotone voice -- treat the voicemail hook like a YouTube video or tweet hook. Note: many recipients will just read the voicemail transcription rather than listen, so the message needs to work as text too.
- Also mentioned: mass text as a channel (not universally applicable per industry); tool named: Podium (referred to as "porterhouse doapp" -- likely mis-transcribed "Podium" -- for compliant SMS outreach requiring a "MacBook").
- Explicitly advised against: paid ads, cold email. Cold calling "works but is not efficient" compared to ringless voicemail.

## 2. Computer-vision SaaS using Google Street View / satellite imagery to find home-service leads (caller: David)
- Product: software that runs object detection over Google Maps/Apple Maps satellite and street-view imagery to find sales leads for home-service companies -- e.g., detect dead/leaning trees, roofs with tarps on them, pools, solar panels, etc., then generate mailer lists.
- Current traction/pricing at time of call: 1-2 paying customers at ~$200/month flat fee, plus a per-lead call-tracking charge of $20-30/lead (competitors reportedly charge $50-80/lead).
- Competitors: mainly Google's own in-house tools (e.g., Google Maps solar panel roof-estimate feature); other players operate at "Enterprise grade" only, leaving SMB home-service niches underserved.
- Known data limitation: satellite/street-view imagery can be 1-2 years out of date; more current imagery available via paid providers like Maxar, sold per state/county subset (compared to buying MLS listings).
- Koerner's advice: don't spread across many verticals at once -- start with ONE vertical (he suggests trees: dead trees, trees touching/hanging over roofs) because (a) tree/roofing/solar buyers have big budgets but are marketing-oversaturated, while (b) most of the other 500-700 home-service verticals that could use this are unsophisticated/blue-collar and rarely buy software.
- Specific tactic: charge an initial "foot in the door" fee as low as $1/month just to capture the customer's credit card -- cites Baremetrics-style "dollar trial then $300/year" conversion psychology; getting the card on file is described as "80% of the way there."
- Suggested workflow for the software owner (not the blue-collar business owner) to run leads manually on the client's behalf: business owner is not expected to learn the software; the vendor should run it in the backend, generate e.g. 100 addresses per zip code with the target condition, and charge $5-10 per address as a "lead," independent of the client's conversion rate.
- Build case studies from this ("we sent 82 mailers last month, did you get calls from that zip code?") to put on the website as social proof.
- Recommend building 1-3 integrations with direct mail fulfillment companies so the vendor doesn't have to run mail fulfillment itself.
- Market size cited: ~50,000 tree trimming companies in the US alone as a single-vertical addressable market.
- A live caller (Christian, a Miami roofing company owner covering Miami-Dade, Broward, Palm Beach counties) said he'd sign up immediately for tarp-detection leads; Koerner himself said he'd pay for tree-hazard leads -- direct validation during the call.

## 3. Buying a custom souvenir (B2B) business (caller: a self-funded searcher, 15 months searching)
- Business model described: custom-branded souvenirs (magnets, keychains, enamel pins) sold B2B to gift shops, museums, and tourist attractions -- highly custom per client (e.g., "Zion National Park" branded magnets).
- Economics cited for one such target business: ~$1 million revenue per employee; extremely sticky customers who repeatedly reorder as their gift-shop stock refreshes; the specific one he wanted to buy had built up 30 years of business almost entirely from attending 2 trade shows a year (very low CAC once established) -- but was taken off market because the owner did an ESOP exit to reward employees instead of selling for cash (to avoid a large tax hit).
- The buying challenge: customers are B2B buyers (a merchandising/purchasing role inside each gift shop or chain, sometimes 1-3 people, sometimes solicits RFPs) -- a genuinely hard, slow, relationship-based sales cycle, akin to selling to school districts or healthcare systems.
- Koerner's core advice: don't try to build customer acquisition into this business from scratch -- because these businesses are so hard to acquire customers for, once acquired the incumbents rarely lose them, which is exactly what makes the existing suppliers valuable. Instead of building a customer base, buy one of the fewer than ~100 existing companies that already sell to this base.
- Tactical acquisition-of-target-companies approach: hire 5 different virtual assistants to independently build lists of every company that sells custom gifts to gift shops using any method (LinkedIn, Apollo, Outscraper), merge/de-duplicate the lists into one master database, then do outbound relationship-building (gift baskets, personal letters, calls, texts) emphasizing genuine passion/interest in this niche industry to build trust with these owners.
- Law of large numbers framing: even if none of ~86 potential target companies want to sell today, some meaningful fraction will become willing to sell within 6 months -- so play a long game across many targets simultaneously rather than fixating on one deal.
- Reference point from Koerner's own experience selling B2B wholesale iPhone parts: ~94% reorder rate on $2-3k/month per customer -- illustrates how sticky repeat B2B relationships can compound.

## 4. Starting a residential trash/waste collection business (caller: Ed, Missouri)
- Inspiration cited: Spencer Scott, who started a waste collection business in a Texas neighborhood via HOA/Facebook-group targeting; Koerner cautions this was largely a lucky, hard-to-replicate break (Spencer is also an experienced marketing-software founder) -- don't assume the same approach reliably repeats.
- Structural note: waste management is heavily monopolized by a handful of national players (~80% of the market per Koerner's estimate), but the remaining ~20% is "up for grabs," and municipal contracts in many states/cities are put out for competitive bid.
- Local market details from caller: in Missouri, city/municipal contracts are typically bid out; landlords/property managers of multifamily buildings frequently dislike their current vendor (overflowing dumpsters, missed pickup days) -- "trash valet" service for apartment complexes suggested as an alternate/additional entry point beyond HOAs.
- Caller's committed capital: ~$100k, self-rated 8/10 seriousness, currently full-time in an unrelated recruiting business (this would be a side/future venture, not immediate).
- Koerner's explicit caveat: unlike most internet-testable business ideas, this one *cannot* be cheaply/quickly tested -- it requires real capital and time commitment before you know if it will work, so he only recommends proceeding because of the caller's stated seriousness (8/10) and available capital ($100k).
- Recommended go-to-market: identify 3-5 nearby communities (HOAs) likely to need/rebid trash service; build a simple website and put up 1-2 billboards in the area (cost cited: ~$3-700/month, often month-to-month, often including the vinyl) purely for perceived legitimacy -- reasoning: a billboard signals "this is a real, established company," not a test. Do this before buying a truck.
- Sequence: plant awareness (website + billboards + direct mail/cold calls to HOA presidents/management companies) → bid on an actual job → only once you win a contract, buy a truck (only need one truck for 1-2 pickup days/week to start, and the owner can drive it himself).

## 5. Buying a Pineapple/Acai bowl ("Pliables"-style) mall franchise (caller: New Jersey)
- Caller's data: relatively new fruit-bowl/acai-bowl franchise concept (called "Pliables" in the transcript, likely misheard branding), ~220 locations at time of call, average revenue per store cited at $1.1M/year (per an Entrepreneur magazine article), franchise fee + 7% revenue royalty + ~1-2% marketing fee; caller identified a specific opportunity in a new, high-traffic (~200,000 daily visitors) NJ mall ("American Dream") with no existing location within the franchise's 2-mile exclusivity radius.
- Koerner's verdict: pass. General thesis against niche food/beverage franchise concepts: they tend to look great locally right when trendy (cites a Tuscaloosa, AL acai-bowl shop and a nearby cupcake shop that both had lines out the door doing $10k/day, then both closed) -- niche food concepts have high downside (buildout cost $150-500k+, no equipment residual value) versus capped upside (~$1.1M topline, ~15% net margin ≈ $150k/year after a large buildout investment and ongoing 7% royalty).
- Recommended due diligence for any franchise: check the Minnesota Franchise Registry / FDD database (any franchise selling in Minnesota must file disclosures there) for store closure counts, not just openings. Also: treat Entrepreneur.com franchise "best of" lists skeptically -- they're largely paid placements by the franchisors themselves.
- Counter-example of a franchise concept Koerner likes better: a franchise model where the operator audits a small business's expenses (credit card processing rates, shipping rates, vendor contracts), cuts their costs, and takes 50% of the savings on an ongoing/perpetual basis -- described as low startup cost, no physical location needed, near-zero churn, franchisable across territories, with operators reportedly hitting seven figures/year at higher margins than a food-concept franchise.
