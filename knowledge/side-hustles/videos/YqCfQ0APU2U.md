# He Found a Way to Make $1K/Hour From His Phone
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YqCfQ0APU2U | Published: 20260331

Interview with Kip Roland (@kip_roland), who runs a live-selling (liquidation resale) business on Whatnot and TikTok Shop.

## The business idea
- Buy liquidation/customer-return/overstock goods at 80-90% off retail, then resell them via **live-stream auctions** (Whatnot, and more recently TikTok Shop) — items typically start bidding at $1 and run 10-30 second auctions, driving urgency/impulse buys.
- Background: Kip ran an Amazon reselling business (data-driven arbitrage: analyze product-level sales-volume data to find underserved items, buy in bulk, sell for margin) doing ~$150K/month revenue, $10-20K/month profit — but his Amazon account was suspended for 180 days over a technical/compliance issue, forcing a pivot to live selling with zero followers/experience (February 2025).

## Numbers
- First-ever livestream (kitchen appliances: coffee makers, air fryers, toasters, vacuums, sourced as a ~400-unit Macy's customer-return liquidation lot): sold ~150 tested items in 1hr45min, $4,500 total sales, netted to roughly breakeven after accounting for shipping/supplies and some unsellable junk items in the lot — but gained 400-500 followers from that single stream.
- One year later: 40,000 Whatnot followers (grew ~800-1,000/week), over 250 live streams totaling ~1,000 hours of streaming.
- 2025 full-year gross revenue (after platform fees): $450,000; ~$100,000 net profit for the year (~22% margin) — about 30% of that year's revenue came from a single exceptionally good liquidation buy.
- Trailing 30 days (at time of interview, 2026): $45,000 revenue, ~$12,000 profit (~30% margin, consistent with his average).
- Average per-stream profit (last ~8 months): ~$680/stream on ~4-hour streams → ~$175/hour profit.
- Best single-stream results: a 12-hour multi-streamer marathon sold ~600 pairs of shoes (highest revenue show ever). Peak per-hour figures: $1,200-1,500/hour revenue, $400-500/hour profit at best. A live TikTok soccer-jersey stream: bought jerseys at $12/unit, sold ~90 units in 2 hours at ~$22 net (after fees/shipping) each → ~$2,000 profit for that single stream.
- Time to first $10,000-profit month: ~4 months in (with some losing months after). Steady state reached ~6 months in: $10,000-15,000/month profit.
- A live house-stream demo the host (Chris) did with a friend, selling protein bars cold (no existing followers): $900 in sales in 90 minutes, ~1/3 of that profit (~$300).
- Average sale price across all his channels/categories: $16/unit net (after fees and shipping).
- Consignment/luxury sellers (a different niche, e.g. luxury handbags) can average $25,000/hour in sales once they have an established trusted audience — cited as the ceiling of what's possible, not typical.

## Process / steps
1. Get a liquidation sourcing account: use **B-Stock** (bstock.com) — just needs a resale certificate and some searching — or negotiate direct relationships with suppliers who hold retailer liquidation contracts (Target, Macy's, Sam's Club, Costco, etc.), typically buying at 80-90% off retail and reselling at 50-60% off.
2. Research the platform/category before going live: watch top sellers' streams, note their formats/personalities, and self-audit why you scroll away from a stream (bad host? bad items? bad pricing?).
3. **Test physical condition of every unit before selling** (critical for customer-return lots) — categorize by grade (e.g., A/S/C tier); items that can't be functionally tested (e.g., coffee makers — you can't brew a full pot on stream) get sold with a caveat ("it turns on, holds water") and refunded if defective.
4. Start auctions at $1 regardless of retail value — creates instant engagement; works especially well for **high-perceived-value items** where the buyer can't easily estimate a "true" price (e.g., appliances, jewelry) versus **low-perceived-value items** (e.g., shoes/fashion) where buyers already have strong price anchors and premium features/branding don't move the final bid much.
5. Buy "with math, sell with emotion" — the single biggest mistake sellers make is paying too much because they get emotionally attached to a brand or see competitors succeeding with it; a brand unfamiliar to your specific audience carries no premium regardless of retail MSRP.
6. For low-cost items, increase perceived value via **bundling** (e.g., 3 items together) or throw-ins, and address the shipping-cost objection early by bundling to spread a flat shipping cost across more units.
7. Grow initial followers using giveaways and by being in an undersaturated category on the platform (few appliance sellers when he started = easier discovery).
8. To retest new product categories with an existing audience, announce the new category ahead of time and ask people to "bookmark" the show — bookmark count is a live gauge of audience interest before committing capital to a new lot (example: switched from appliances → women's fashion → food, and got 50%+ new/returning buyers trying food for the first time from him).
9. Being an authentic "genuine interest" seller (or hiring a streamer who has that genuine interest in the product category) drives conversion — audiences relate to genuine enthusiasm, not "get money from us" energy; being a category outsider (e.g., a man selling women's shoes) can still work if it's earnest.
10. Hiring streamers: pay slightly above local market rate for comparable retail/service jobs plus a small commission (his structure: $17/hour base + 1% commission, streamers average $21-22/hour). Look for people who like the product category, have natural energy/extroversion (or can turn it on), and can take performance feedback without taking it personally. Keep cost-basis/margin info away from hired streamers to avoid pressuring them into a "sell sell sell" tone.
11. Use platform-integrated shipping tools (Whatnot and TikTok Shop both handle label purchase/shipping deals natively) — no separate tool like Pirate Ship/ShipStation needed.
12. TikTok Shop specifically provides detailed live analytics (average view duration, impression spikes, audience demographics) — useful for more experienced sellers optimizing targeting; TikTok has far higher raw viewership (~180M monthly active US users) than Whatnot but lower per-viewer buyer intent (Whatnot is described as a smaller, higher-intent, community-driven platform).

## First-$1,000-profit playbook (his direct advice for beginners)
- Source items priced under $5/unit so you can start bids at $1 and still profit after just a handful of bids.
- Target an eventual sale price in the $10-20 "impulse buy" range — that's the natural ceiling for a fast auction/impulse purchase, regardless of true item value.
- Plan fulfillment logistics *before* going live — you may sell 100+ items in a single 1-2 hour session and need to ship all of them promptly, repeatedly, day after day.
- Favor categories with high "perceived value but unknown price" (jewelry called out as an especially strong beginner category — small, storable, no clear price anchor for buyers).
- New-account performance gap: with zero followers/experience, an energetic/skilled streamer will vastly outsell a low-energy one (the difference is "almost everything" at zero-follower start); among similarly skilled/experienced streamers, follower count still matters most in the first month (someone starting at 50,000 followers may out-revenue a zero-follower start by ~50% in month one) — but a 3,000-follower account can already competently compete against a 50,000-follower one, i.e., follower-count advantage has strongly diminishing returns after an initial threshold.

## Caveats / risks
- Categories to avoid: **fashion/apparel on Whatnot** is deceptively hard (brand, size, cultural relevance, and style all matter far more than expected) and **vacuums** (already-used, bulky/expensive to ship).
- Liquidation lots are a gamble — quality varies unit to unit; his very first lot included unsellable items (a batch of recalled beef jerky that had to be discarded/eaten as a loss, plus a portion of broken appliances) that ate into margin.
- Requires meaningful working capital for larger buys (he cites putting up $20,000-30,000 on some liquidation lot purchases, expecting to sell it down over following weeks) — not realistic for everyone, and a real financial risk if a lot underperforms.
- Streaming is physically/mentally demanding even for extroverts — 4-hour average streams, described by the host as exhausting even though enjoyable; some streamers can sustain 6+ hours, most burn out well before that.
- Non-competes generally not an issue since the business is highly local/relationship-based on the sourcing side.
- Requires patience: it took roughly 4 months to reach a $10,000 profit month, with losing months along the way; the flagship $450K year depended heavily on one unusually good liquidation buy (~30% of annual revenue from a single lot), underscoring high month-to-month/lot-to-lot variance.
