# He Found a Grant That Feels Like a Cheat Code ($2.4M/Year)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g-wMJf4ckK0 | Published: 2026-07-30

Interview with Hector (trade-school entrepreneur, tradeschoolsecrets.co) on starting online trade schools funded by government workforce grants.

## The business idea
**Start a licensed online trade school** in a skilled-trade niche (forklift repair, HVAC, EV repair, appliance repair, pool cleaning, plumbing, dog grooming, welding, electrical, solar, data-center facility maintenance, etc.) where tuition is paid by a government workforce-training grant (WIOA-style; Hector references "the wheel process and the ETPL" — Eligible Training Provider List) rather than by the student out of pocket. You do not need trade expertise yourself — the course content can be **white-labeled** (bought from an existing provider) rather than built from scratch, and you can partner with an existing operator/expert in the trade to be the instructor/face while you run sales, automation, and CRM.

## Concrete numbers
- Hector's first trade school (forklift technician, launched 2021, benefited from COVID timing): did **$2.4M revenue in year one**, but spent heavily on unnecessary overhead (leased a building, hired full staff, bought furniture/books) despite running an online-only course — most students never physically came in, so much of that revenue was eaten by overhead and a generous salary he paid himself.
- Year two: revenue dropped to **$1.4M but was more profitable**, run essentially as a two-person operation with heavy automation, after going fully online-only (dropping the physical location) and being able to sell across the entire state rather than just one metro.
- Margins once optimized: **~70% overall, up to ~90% margin on the course product itself** — course was purchased/licensed for **$1,500** and sold to students (fully covered by their grant) for **$12,000-15,000**.
- New forklift-technician trade school Hector is building today (white-labeled course, partnered with an existing forklift repair operator): plans to charge **$7,500** for a 55-hour course + 10 hours hands-on, with the underlying licensed course itself costing **$500** — a **$7,000 margin per student**.
- Other cited operator examples: a Houston-based owner (electrical/HVAC trade school) doing **$4-5M/year**; an Idaho-based owner focused on data-center facility-maintenance/appliance-repair/industrial-cleaning courses doing **~$1M/year**; an Arizona IT-course reseller who ran unlicensed for 2 years doing **~$1M/year selling directly to the public** before becoming licensed and adding **an extra $50-60K/month** from grant-funded students on top of his existing business; a Utah-based operator selling exclusively to a prison reentry program doing **~$30,000/month** (smaller than others because Utah's full licensing process takes 2 years, so he's currently only running a partnership program with the prison system).
- Startup capital needed to launch a trade school from scratch and get state-licensed: roughly **$15,000-30,000**, largely to demonstrate 2-3 months of operating cash reserves the state requires to see before approving grant eligibility, plus state application/filing fees.
- Graduates of the forklift program reportedly earn around **$30/hour** starting out, work independently from a van/home dispatch, and "pay themselves back in a couple of months" relative to the tuition cost (which is grant-covered anyway).

## Process / steps described
- **Two entry paths**: (1) become a state-licensed training provider from day one and get direct grant reimbursement per enrolled student (requires proving financial reserves, ~$15-30K), or (2) start as an unlicensed "digital reseller" selling the same or similar courses directly to the general public/employers first (no reserves required), build revenue and a track record, then apply for licensing later to add grant-funded revenue on top of the existing business — this is the recommended path for someone "tied on funds but patient."
- **Sales motion is largely passive on the fulfillment side**: courses are fully online (originally an "ebook with videos" model; one of Hector's programs, HVAC, is delivered via Oculus/VR), so scaling doesn't require classroom space; interaction with students is mainly limited to onboarding and job-placement help at the end. The instructor/expert-in-the-trade delivers the actual instructional content; the business owner's job is largely CRM, sales scripts, automations, and follow-up drip campaigns — which Hector says most existing trade schools completely lack, and where he made his money differentiating.
- **Reimbursement mechanics**: charge exactly up to the state's grant reimbursement cap for that program — never more (no out-of-pocket top-up requirement for the student assuming you stay within the cap).
- **Job placement**: minimal active effort required in most trades because the underlying skills are in-demand — graduates can search job boards (e.g., "forklift technician" or "EV technician" on Indeed) and expect 25+ open listings and a callback almost immediately; Hector's own average graduate reportedly interviews only 2-3 times before getting an offer.
- **Recommended niche scope**: focus on one trade initially, or at most two to three — avoid spreading across many trade categories at once ("be the master of a couple versus offering everything"), though nothing prevents running multiple schools/trades once established.
- **Non-grant customer acquisition channels** for building initial revenue/track record before licensing: local community outreach (churches, nonprofits — note nonprofits often have discretionary funds beyond standard workforce grants for mission-driven training), direct B2B sales to employers who need certified staff but don't want to wait for classroom-based training, and positioning to parents of graduating high schoolers as a lower-cost, faster alternative to a four-year college gap year.
- **Incorporation**: Hector uses Bizee (bizee.com) to form and maintain his LLCs, registered agent service, and EIN rather than filing directly through state websites (sponsor-endorsed in this episode, framed as materially easier/no more expensive than DIY state filing).
- **Why an existing service business owner should add a trade school**: solves the "can't find enough technicians" hiring bottleneck — you get first pick of top graduates to hire directly, while the rest are certified and placed elsewhere; framed as monetizing your own training/recruiting funnel rather than just paying to train competitors' future hires.

## Caveats / risks
- Hector's own first-year experience is a cautionary tale on overhead: chasing "what he thought a school needed" (a leased building, full staff, physical materials) consumed most of the $2.4M in revenue despite the course being deliverable entirely online — profitability came only after stripping overhead down to a lean, largely automated online-only model.
- Licensing requirements, grant reimbursement caps, and rules about whether you can sell directly to consumers/employers without a license vary significantly by state — Hector repeatedly qualifies advice with "depends on the state."
- Grant-funded revenue is not guaranteed to be the only viable path — several of his cited case studies (the Arizona reseller, community organizations) succeeded selling directly to consumers/employers without any state license at all, suggesting the grant is a margin enhancer/moat rather than a strict requirement to build a profitable course-selling business.
- Framed explicitly (host's closing argument, echoed by guest) as durable against AI disruption specifically because it trains people for **physical, hands-on trade skilled labor** (e.g., forklift repair) that current and foreseeable AI/robotics cannot replace — the argument is that specialized physical repair work lacks the training data and economic incentive (unlike higher-volume robotic use cases like warehouse box-packing) to be automated for decades.
- Student non-completion/non-follow-through does happen but Hector characterizes it as uncommon — people who choose a trade program tend to already want hands-on work and follow through more than typical.
