# How to Use AI to Clone Others for Profit
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AZmdT_OK9p8 | Published: 2024-10-14

Freewheeling brainstorm episode with returning guest Sam Thompson (digital/startup-focused entrepreneur) covering several distinct, fairly concrete digital-business ideas. No single idea is executed step-by-step on screen; treat as an idea list with supporting numbers/reasoning rather than a tested case study.

## Idea 1: Low-cost digital products sold at scale (Etsy/notion templates etc.)
- Model: create a narrowly niched digital product (e.g., a Notion template, PDF guide, ebook) with near-zero cost of goods (COGS), sell on marketplaces like Etsy (which has its own ad platform) or via your own paid ads.
- Example economics given: a $30–$50 "new mom organization Notion template" aimed at a niche audience; claim you could realistically get ~3,000 buyers at that price to reach ~$100K in a year.
- Ad-spend math example (illustrative, not verified): if you can acquire a customer for $20 on a $30 product, spending $2,000/day on ads nets ~$3,000/day (~$30,000/month) — used to illustrate that a positive-ROAS digital product can scale ad spend directly into revenue.
- AI angle: use AI to generate/re-skin the same core product for many niches quickly (e.g., "mom's daily journal" → "high school daily journal" in minutes), similar to how dropshippers iteratively test many physical products from China until one hits — but without dropshipping's fulfillment/refund/shipping headaches (2-week delays, Stripe account risk, Shopify build overhead).
- Pricing/positioning tactic ("sell the outcome, not the object"): reference a real example of a sleep consultant charging $650K/year (~$3,000 per client) coaching new parents on infant sleep schedules — the suggested move is to productize the same expertise as a $300 PDF (a "tenth of the price" but still a meaningfully high-ticket digital product) rather than a $30 commodity product, using ad copy modeled on premium physical-product ads (e.g., a $300 "mini fridge" ad that leans into "it's not cheap, and here's why that's good") to justify a higher price point instead of racing to the bottom.
- Testing method: build two landing pages (or use a pricing-A/B-test app, since Shopify supports this even for digital goods) at two price points (e.g., $79 vs. $300) and run ads to see which is actually more profitable/has better CAC — very low operational overhead (no calls to book, no inventory, no team — "you and a PDF").
- Suggested strategic use: don't just chase whatever sells — pick a niche you have genuine interest/expertise in, because early buyers of a cheap front-end digital product become a warm customer list you can later sell services, software, or higher-ticket offers to (explicitly compared to Russell Brunson's ClickFunnels playbook, which started with the low-priced "DotCom Secrets" book as a funnel into higher-ticket products/software). The stated purpose of doing this as a first business: prove to yourself that you can acquire customers/leads — considered the single most important skill/proof point for any new entrepreneur.

## Idea 2: AI "cloning" of content creators as an agency/broker model
- Tooling reference: Delphi AI (and similar tools) let a creator "clone" themselves into an LLM chatbot trained on their content (YouTube videos, Google Drive files, etc.) that followers can chat with for a monthly fee. Host's own experience testing it ~9 months prior (per this Oct 2024 recording) was underwhelming/manual, but expected to improve quickly.
- The bigger idea discussed is not building this tool, but acting as an agent/broker connecting creators who've never heard of these tools to the technology, then monetizing via a revenue cut:
  - Approach a content creator (example given: "Mark Andre," a jazz guitar YouTuber/course creator whose Skool community reportedly earned ~$80,000/month, cited as the 13th most profitable Skool group at the time) with an "ethical bait and switch": message them saying "I made an AI clone of you from your public content, want to see it?" — most (host estimates ~5% response rate) won't respond, but for those who do, pitch: give access to all your content (public YouTube + unshared Google Drive materials), we'll prompt-engineer/package/price a subscription chatbot version of you, sell it to your existing audience, and take a ~30% cut of the resulting revenue.
- Rationale/value prop for the creator: turns a $500 one-on-one 30-minute consulting call into a ~$20/month always-available AI chat version of themselves — 5% of the price for (per the hosts) the "majority of the value" a fan actually needs, unlocking access to people the expert doesn't have time to serve individually. Compared explicitly to existing "access to experts" platforms like Intro and Mentor Pass, whose experts often can't scale their personal time even at higher prices.
- Adjacent, non-AI precedent already tested and validated by the guest: an SMS/iMessage-based cohort access product — creators/experts sold time-boxed (e.g., one month) SMS access via a simple landing page + Stripe checkout, using OpenPhone (or the iMessage-specific API "SendBlue," recommended for higher read/reply rates and no per-SMS character-count billing) to run the conversations; one such campaign made roughly $10,000 in a month off a single creator's audience.
- Retention/pricing lesson from that prior experiment: sell annual access only (not $20/month) for products like this — e.g., $249–$299/year instead of monthly, since the actual price-sensitivity difference in conversion is small but churn management on a low-ticket recurring consumer product is a major operational headache, especially if a subscriber doesn't engage in month one (near-total loss of that customer going forward).
- Activation is flagged as the make-or-break metric for this kind of subscription product, more so than initial acquisition: design onboarding to get new subscribers actively messaging within the first 48–72 hours (e.g., providing sample prompts/questions to click, similar to ChatGPT's or Delphi's own "here are 15 example questions" onboarding UI) since users unfamiliar with chatting with an AI via text need explicit prompting to form the habit. General framing: for recurring/subscription products, retention and referral matter more than CAC/CPA — you have to keep marketing and delivering value to existing subscribers continuously, not just at signup.
- Extension idea: bundle a broader knowledge base (e.g., generic business-content databases in addition to the specific creator's own content) so a subscriber could toggle between "ask [creator]" and "ask the wider knowledge base" for a richer answer set (raises unaddressed content-licensing/trademark questions the hosts acknowledge but don't resolve).
- Related, smaller idea: use SMS/iMessage as a lightweight lead-capture and engagement mechanism generally — instead of a landing page collecting emails for a newsletter, collect a phone number and drop the person directly into a live/AI-assisted text conversation ("what's up, I'm Chris") as the follow-up, arguing text open/reply rates beat email and that iMessage APIs (e.g., SendBlue) avoid the cost and character-limit issues of standard SMS/Twilio-based texting at scale.

## Idea 3: Niche, hyper-specific B2B data/lead-list businesses
- Framed via a "Craigslist unbundling" analogy — big horizontal incumbents (Craigslist → Airbnb, Uber, etc.) get replaced by narrower, purpose-built alternatives; the guest argues the next thing ripe for this treatment is data, specifically hyper-niche B2B contact/lead databases.
- Reference competitors named as the "unbundling target": ZoomInfo, Apollo, GetCyberLeads (targets recently-funded startups, ~$200/month, valuable because newly funded companies are eager buyers), BuiltWith (charges ~$400/month; people frequently ask to "borrow" an account for a single list rather than pay for a subscription — signal of price sensitivity/latent demand for a cheaper, narrower alternative).
- Proposed model: pick an extremely narrow data vertical (e.g., "contact info for the top 3 decision-makers at every Shopify Plus store," or "cell phone numbers of business owners at high-ticket home service companies — roofers, HVAC, fencing, painting, contractors") and sell access to that curated, cleaned list at a fraction of the incumbents' price (e.g., $299/year vs. ~$1,500/year for messier, broader data from Apollo). Preferred pricing structure again is annual, not monthly.
- Sourcing/build process described as low-cost and non-technical: hire an Upwork freelancer to scrape the target data (example cost cited: ~$200 to scrape "every roofing business in Texas"; also references D7 Lead Finder as an existing tool that can already produce this kind of scraped list for ~$100), then load the cleaned list into a no-code database/display tool (Airtable, Glide, Bubble, Webflow, AppSheet-style tools) and put the whole thing behind a paywall.
- The specific business only has two real ongoing jobs: (1) maintain data quality/freshness (the actual product), and (2) market it — the display/access layer is handled by off-the-shelf no-code tooling, so it's not a software-engineering business.
- Sharpened example idea generated live in the episode: a weekly-refreshed CSV of cell phone numbers (landlines scrubbed out) for owners of high-ticket home service businesses (roofing, fencing, painting, contracting, etc.), sold specifically to marketing/ad agency owners who sell to that vertical and don't care about geography (only that the number reaches a real decision-maker) — priced around $300/month, positioned as far cheaper than a $5,000/month alternative while still being profitable, because the underlying scraping/cleaning cost is low.
- Underlying business thesis stated explicitly: the traditional agency model is increasingly commoditized because clients already have self-serve access to the same tools (SEMrush, D7 Lead Finder, etc.) — the actual value-add of a service business now is doing the "80/20" grunt work (sourcing and cleaning data, in this case) cheaply via technology/offshore labor and passing the time savings to the customer at an affordable, transparent price — "done for you" is the sellable differentiator, not proprietary technology. Framed as generalizable to "any industry."
- Realistic scale expectation stated directly: not a billion-dollar ZoomInfo competitor, but plausibly a "half a million dollars a year" business.

## General framing/caveats from the episode
- Both hosts explicitly flag "shiny object syndrome" — chasing a new idea instead of staying disciplined on a current, working business — as a constant tension for entrepreneurs, especially once a service business crosses ~$20-30K/month (margin compression becomes meaningful and forces a decision about whether to build toward enterprise value or optimize for personal income).
- None of the three ideas above have documented, audited revenue results in this video — the $10K SMS cohort experiment and the $80K/month Skool figure are the only real numbers cited, and both are anecdotal/secondhand.
- Explicit caveat on the data-business idea: legal/ethical boundaries around scraping (e.g., they note you can't just scrape a subreddit's whole content without permission) are acknowledged but not resolved in detail.
