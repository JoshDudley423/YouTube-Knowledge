# Make $500k/Year with One Email a Week
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4AWlBGVQ8nA | Published: 2026-02-24

Interview with Tyler Denk — co-founder of beehiiv (newsletter platform) and former 2nd employee at Morning Brew (scaled it to 4M readers in ~3.5 years). Walks through building a newsletter from zero on beehiiv and monetizing it, using his own side-project newsletter "Big Desk Energy" as the live case study. Note: beehiiv is a sponsor of this episode and Tyler is a beehiiv co-founder, so tool-specific claims should be read as promotional but the underlying newsletter business model/numbers are the substance.

## The core business/numbers
- Tyler's own newsletter "Big Desk Energy": ~120,000 subscribers, sent weekly (every Tuesday), run as a side project at ~5 hours/week. Growth is roughly 50/50 organic vs. paid.
- Reported ~$500,000 in revenue generated from the list to date; described as very high margin (beehiiv platform cost is low, one employee, free invoicing software) — implies most of that is profit.
- Tyler's estimate: if run as a full-time business rather than a side project, this could scale to "multiple millions" in revenue.
- Revenue breakdown he gives for his own newsletter: sponsorships ~$5,000-$7,000 per weekly send = ~$20,000-$30,000/month (~$240k-$300k/year); in-person founder "mastermind" events ~$80,000 topline per event, run 2-3x/year (~$160k-$240k/year); Boost (see below) contributing ~$10,000-$25,000/year when actively used. Only ~$23,000 of the ~$500k was earned directly "through the platform" (ads/subscriptions) — most came from direct sponsorships and IRL events.

## Step-by-step: launching a newsletter on beehiiv
1. Sign up for beehiiv (platform hosts both a newsletter AND a companion website simultaneously — every newsletter issue can double as a public blog post to capture organic/search traffic and new signups).
2. Build a website using beehiiv's built-in website builder — no coding/design skill needed; templates available, plus an AI website-builder feature where you can upload a screenshot of a site you like and it generates a similar page.
3. Pick a publishing cadence — explicit advice: **start weekly, not daily**. Daily is a bigger time commitment than people expect and risks burnout; you can always increase cadence later once you have monetization/scale. Missing an occasional week is not as costly to reader relationships as creators fear.
4. Set up minimal core pages: a clean, distraction-free subscriber landing page (used as the destination for paid ad traffic) and a "lead magnet" page (a piece of free, valuable content exchanged for an email signup — e.g., Tyler used his own real $2.5M fundraising pitch deck as a lead magnet, promoted via organic X/LinkedIn posts and paid ads).
5. Set up automations: a "welcome" email automation triggered on signup (can be as simple as a thank-you + cadence expectation-setting, or as advanced as multi-step branches — e.g., a follow-up email sent to anyone who hasn't opened any email within 7 days, or a differentiated welcome message based on which social platform the subscriber came from).
6. Email list hygiene/verification is handled automatically by beehiiv (both backend-added and landing-page-submitted emails are checked against 3rd-party + proprietary validation) — removes the traditional manual CSV-scrubbing step needed with older ESPs, and reduces bounce-rate risk that could get an account suspended from a sending platform.

## Niche selection guidance
- Explicit take: pick a niche, not mass-market — mass-market lists are harder to grow and monetize. "If you are obsessed with [X], I guarantee there are hundreds of thousands of people around the world equally obsessed — it's about finding those pockets" (echoing the "1,000 true fans" concept).
- Example given: a newsletter reaching just 5,000 CFOs is objectively small but extremely monetizable because of how valuable/high-trust that specific audience is to advertisers — "work backwards from who would pay to get in front of your specific audience."
- If you don't have an obvious niche, a "personal diary" newsletter to your existing circle (friends, professional network) is a viable default — cited example: someone built a consulting business (not ad/subscription revenue) purely by using a weekly newsletter to showcase their work to a few hundred (later few thousand) friends/acquaintances, converting readers into "hundreds of thousands of dollars" of consulting contracts over time.
- Math example for the "small list, high-ticket service" model: a 2,000-subscriber list, $3,000 average consulting engagement, 1% of the list converting to a booked engagement per month = 20 clients x $3,000 = $500,000/year — argued as potentially more profitable than a 30,000-50,000 subscriber list monetized only via traditional CPM ad sponsorships, and without needing to hustle for/manage sponsors.

## Monetization methods (roughly a dozen discussed)
1. **Direct sponsorships** — sell ad placement (image + copy block) at the top of each newsletter issue directly to brands. Tyler charges $5,000-$7,000 per weekly send to his 120K list.
2. **beehiiv Ad Network** — a marketplace where advertisers offer to pay you per click/CPM automatically (example shown: an advertiser offering $2.50 per click, projected to generate ~$275+ from one send) — no direct sales conversation needed; drop the pre-made ad creative into your issue and get paid.
3. **Paid subscriptions** — beehiiv lets you paywall/gate specific content or sections; beehiiv takes no cut of subscription revenue (platform fee only).
4. **"Boost"** — beehiiv's pay-per-verified-subscriber growth marketplace (two-sided): you can pay other newsletters in your niche a set price per subscriber they send you (Tyler cites ~$2.50/subscriber as typical, up to $4-$8 for outliers) — and if a delivered subscriber turns out fake/never opens an email, beehiiv's verification system automatically refunds you (escrow-based, "risk-free growth" vs. normal paid ad spend). Conversely, you can earn money by recommending other newsletters and getting paid per subscriber you send them. Tyler has spent ~$190,000 lifetime on Boost (~$6K/month over ~2 years) as his single largest expense line, and separately earns ~$10,000 via Boost from recommending others.
5. **Recommendation network** (free, no money) — mutual newsletter recommendations shown as a post-signup popup ("we recommend these 4 other newsletters"); works best when reciprocated by similarly-sized/new newsletters. At Tyler's scale: sent ~35,500 subscribers to others' newsletters, received ~34,000 back from 774 other newsletters recommending him.
6. **Magic links** — one-click subscribe links you can hand to a friend/larger newsletter to embed in their content ("click here to subscribe to my friend's newsletter") — the recipient is added to your list without visiting your website, removing signup friction; works across major email/newsletter platforms.
7. **In-person events / masterminds** — Tyler's highest-yield lever: an $10,000-ticket "founder mastermind" retreat (a rented mansion in Costa Rica) that only needs ~8 of his 120,000 subscribers to convert per cohort; ~$80K topline per event, run a few times a year. Framed as thinking backwards from "what does my specific audience actually want" (founders want to network with other founders) rather than defaulting to generic ad/subscription monetization.
8. **Merch / affiliate-style tie-ins** for niche fan-club-style newsletters (hypothetical example discussed: a superfan newsletter for a band could sell band merch with a kickback, or organize a paid group trip/experience around a tour stop).
9. **Ticketed live events/conferences** tied to the newsletter's niche audience (hypothetical: a small niche newsletter running an annual $2,000-ticket meetup/conference for a subset of readers).
10. **First-party audience survey data as a sales asset**: Tyler runs an ongoing survey (~15,000 responses from his 120K list) asking about job/income status, business stage, and financial capacity — used to build a "media kit" for advertisers showing hard demographic/psychographic data (e.g., "40% business owners / 60% aspiring," "36% would invest $50K/year," "1% would invest over $1M/year," "3% have generated $10M+ lifetime revenue"). This segmentation lets him charge premium sponsorship rates and email highly specific investor/buyer segments directly (e.g., emailing just the ~86 people who said they'd invest $1M+).
11. **Audience segmentation generally** — Tyler describes himself as running many list segments (e.g., podcast-listening frequency, opted-in "focus groups" for content ideas) which he uses to poll readers before making content decisions, source podcast guests (offered $100 for a good business story), or find hires — treating the list as an ongoing research panel, not just a broadcast channel.
12. **The reinvestment flywheel**: revenue from sponsorships/paid subscriptions is put back into Boost/paid ads to acquire new subscribers, which grows the list, which increases what can be charged for future sponsorships — described as the core compounding loop of the business.

## Paid acquisition math (worked example)
- Tyler's own Facebook ad subscriber acquisition cost: ~$1.45/subscriber (below average because of a strong existing Facebook audience/retargeting pool); he recoups ~$0.65 of that immediately via Boost recommendations, netting ~$0.75/subscriber actual cost.
- He estimates each subscriber is worth $7-$10 in lifetime value depending on how many monetization levers are pulled — describing this as roughly a 10x return on ad spend.
- General framework given for evaluating whether paid growth is viable: if you can reliably acquire a subscriber for ~$2 via ads, and you're recouping $1-$3 of that immediately via Boost/recommendations while the new subscriber is also worth further revenue from sponsorship exposure over time, that's a favorable unit-economics loop.

## Caveats
- Newsletter monetization figures like "50 cents per subscriber per month" from generic ad-based models are described as a conservative default; Tyler's actual returns come from stacking multiple non-obvious monetization channels (events, high-ticket masterminds, first-party data) rather than ad revenue alone.
- Paid subscription conversion rates ("how many free subscribers convert to paid") are explicitly called highly variable and audience/content-dependent — no reliable benchmark is given ("I don't know if we have amazing benchmarks... it really differs").
- This episode is sponsor-supported by beehiiv (Tyler's own company) — platform-specific capabilities described (automations, Boost, ad network, survey tools) should be understood as beehiiv-specific features being demonstrated live, not a vendor-neutral comparison of newsletter platforms.
