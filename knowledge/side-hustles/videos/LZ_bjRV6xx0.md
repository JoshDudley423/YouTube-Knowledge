# The Most Hands-Off Side Hustle Anyone Can Copy
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LZ_bjRV6xx0 | Published: 2025-09-26

Long interview with Peter Askew (deepsouthventures.com, askew.org, Twitter @searchbound) about building a portfolio of niche websites/e-commerce businesses primarily by acquiring **expired/expiring domain names** and building minimal, low-overhead businesses around them.

## Core strategy: buying expired/expiring domains, not building from scratch

- Peter's background: no coding/CS background (history degree); taught himself basic coding while working a 9-to-5, after being laid off multiple times from corporate jobs (including once training his own replacement/assistant before being let go — cited as his motivation to build income independent of an employer).
- Explicit philosophy against the "quit your job and go all-in" advice common online: he kept his day job the entire time he built these projects, working on them evenings/weekends, calling it the **"wing walker's code"** — "never let go of one railing until you have control of the other." Believes financial pressure/urgency kills the "daydreaming" needed to find good weird ideas; recommends running side projects patiently alongside a day job rather than under forced runway pressure.
- Progression: learned basic coding → built small websites → monetized with Google AdSense → realized it was often faster to **buy** an existing outdated but well-ranked website than build one from scratch, especially "mom-and-pop" sites (old Dreamweaver-built sites, unchanged for 5-10 years) still ranking well for valuable search terms.
- Arbitrage insight: many small-site owners don't realize the true value of their traffic/ad revenue potential. Example: **Travel Envoy** (a winery/vineyard directory site) — bought outright for **$10,000** in ~2004 despite it having zero existing monetization; he added Google AdSense (Google keeps ~30%, site owner/publisher gets ~70% of ad click value) and it generated **$1,000-$2,000/month**, paying off the purchase price in about 6 months. Also earned modest affiliate revenue from an embedded wine-region map widget.
- Realized that **owning the domain itself** (not just content) is the real leverage point — if a seller reneges on a content-transfer deal, owning the domain lets you rebuild the whole site anyway.
- Comparable case cited: friend **Warren Royal** bought **Bobbleheads.com** at a domain auction for reportedly **~$30,000** (with zero prior bobblehead industry experience) and built a custom bobblehead manufacturing business now employing 15 people, producing over **250,000 bobbleheads/year** at $100-$200+ each (implying $25M+/year revenue, per the guests' rough math). Royal also later acquired the singular "Bobblehead.com" to own the whole naming "beachhead." A major insight from Royal: **direct type-in traffic** (users typing a domain name straight into the browser instead of searching) can be one of the largest and most persistent traffic sources for a strong, generic/descriptive .com — something most marketers underrate today.

## Where to source domains

- **Expired domain auctions**: when a domain isn't renewed, it reverts to the registrar and goes to public auction. An estimated **50,000-100,000 domains expire per day**, creating a constant stream of inventory.
- Platforms named: **GoDaddy Auctions** (auctions.godaddy.com), **NameJet**, **SnapNames**, **DropCatch**, **Namecheap's own auction marketplace**, and an aggregator/search tool, **ExpiredDomains.net** (free account, searches across NameJet/SnapNames/GoDaddy/Namecheap/DropCatch listings). Notes some older (pre-1995-era) lapsed domains show up only on NameJet/SnapNames, not GoDaddy.
- His personal workflow: checks expiring-domain listings "like a morning newspaper" essentially every day, largely for fun/curiosity, and treats promising names as an inspiration/trigger to build something rather than searching for an idea first and then hunting a matching domain — a full reversal of the usual "have an idea, then buy a domain" order. States this shift ("let the domain's built-in trust/authority do some of the marketing heavy lifting, work backward from there") was what made his projects start actually working, after years of unfocused building.
- Recommends learning basic SEO/paid-search fundamentals regardless of any "AI changes everything" narrative — believes the underlying principles (matching content to search intent) still hold.

## Case study #1: DudeRanch.com (~$50K/year from ~1 hour/month)

- Bought at auction (a "penny/backorder" style deal) after the previous owner (a NY dude ranch that once advertised on Wheel of Fortune/Jeopardy) let it lapse — cost **just under $18,000**.
- Validation signals checked before buying: confirmed real search volume specifically for "dude ranch vacation" (distinguishing from unrelated searches, e.g., a Blink-182 album of the same name) across multiple state-level queries (Colorado, Texas, Idaho, etc.); then estimated a typical family dude-ranch vacation books for **$10,000-$30,000**, assumed the rancher nets roughly 30% margin (~$3,000/booking) to size how much a rancher might reasonably pay for a qualified lead.
- Business model: charges ranches a **flat $575/year listing fee** (not a per-lead or commission cut) to be featured on the site; built the site himself on WordPress.
- Distribution/sales motion: got initial advertisers by attending the industry's annual conference (held at a dude ranch), which doubled as market research — visiting real ranches gave him credibility ("owning one of these exact-match domains, people assume you're an industry expert — trust and authority").
- Competitive dynamic → partnership: noticed ranches would sometimes cancel his listing but keep a rival directory (GuestRanches.com, run by "David") or vice versa. Proposed a partnership to combine/cross-list so that canceling either site would cost a rancher ~50% of referral traffic, making cancellation much less attractive — partnered for about 8 years before eventually buying David out.
- Current scale: **~60 advertisers, ~$50,000/year revenue**, very low overhead (hosting, domain renewal, and a paid freelance developer on retainer "just in case," costing only a couple hundred dollars/year) — describes spending roughly **1 hour/month** maintaining it today (down from much more active management earlier), and considers it currently under-resourced relative to its potential given his attention is split across other projects.
- Personal/lifestyle payoff: has visited ~50 dude ranches over 14 years partly via this business, calling the free travel/relationship access itself worth "hundreds of thousands of dollars."
- Values/selectivity note: says he wouldn't sell it to just any buyer — worried about someone degrading the site with "AI content slop" or over-monetizing with ads, since he feels stewardship over representing the industry.

## Spinoff: RanchWork.com (job board, "probably his cash cow" currently)

- Originated from noticing his dude-ranch partner (David) had an abandoned side-project job board (built ~1998, essentially dormant, pointing to a dead Facebook group).
- Deal structure (a recurring pattern in Peter's ventures): **no risk/cost to David** — David pointed the domain's nameservers to Peter, Peter built the entire site (using an off-the-shelf WordPress job-board theme purchased for ~$60, customized for better SEO architecture, hosted on a fast server) and ran it in exchange for a **75/25 revenue split** in Peter's favor; Peter kept any AdSense revenue too.
- After roughly 5-6 years operating under that split, Peter bought David out entirely for **~$10,000** when David needed cash.
- Current traction: **1.3 million site visits/year**, a **12,000-person email list**, and a **25,000+ member private Facebook job board group** used for cross-promotion.
- Pricing (raised the day of filming, adding a new top "diamond" tier): **Silver listing runs 10 days, Gold runs 30 days**; cheapest paid tier starts around **$100** to post a job. New listings are hand-curated/approved within 24 hours (occasionally refunded if a listing seems illegitimate). Offers a **free listing option** for ranches in financial hardship — an explicit choice to prioritize trust/goodwill over maximizing revenue.
- Also monetizes the **job-seeker side**: candidates can pay (example given: **$99**) to have their own "seeking work" listing promoted/extended, and Peter actively promotes seekers across the email list and Facebook group (example: helped a candidate land a ranch job directly).
- Unlike the seasonal dude-ranch and Vidalia businesses, this one runs **year-round** (ranches need seasonal staffing waves), which Peter says lets him rotate attention across all three projects depending on each one's season.
- Emphasizes daily consistency (posting jobs virtually every day) as key to signaling to users that the board is active/alive, which he believes drives repeat usage and word-of-mouth.

## Case study #2: Vidalia (onion) e-commerce — VidaliaOnions-style boutique DTC business

- Origin: spotted Vidalia.com about to expire, initially had no intent to buy it; entered a modest opening bid (~$2,100) as a lowball attempt expecting to be outbid, and unexpectedly won at that price — his reaction was regret/anxiety about spending the money with no clear plan ("I'll be eating peanut butter and jelly sandwiches for the next month").
- After sitting on the domain, decided to test interest with a **simple email-signup landing page** (no promises, no purchase yet) asking if people wanted Vidalia onions shipped to them — got real signups, which validated demand before he had any farming/fulfillment relationship in place (~2014-2015).
- Discovered "Vidalia" is a **legally trademarked term restricted to onions grown in a specific Georgia region** (regulated by state law) — had to personally visit the Vidalia Onion Committee to prove legitimacy and get informal buy-in before proceeding, since he wasn't a farmer and didn't want to be seen as hijacking the regional industry's name.
- Found his farming partner ("Aries," then general manager of a farm called M&A/A&M Farms) by cold-visiting several local farms; most farmers he approached weren't interested or actively disliked direct-to-consumer shipping ("the biggest thorn in their side"), but Aries immediately understood the opportunity.
- Deal structure: handshake agreement (no formal contract) lasting **10 years** — Peter built the website, ran sales/marketing/customer support and the online storefront for free, in exchange for keeping the business profitable together; the farm (which already had boxes, a packing shed, and climate-controlled storage enabling summer-long shipping) handled physical fulfillment. Peter and Aries eventually bought out early silent investors and now own the business 100% between them (Peter's stake was ~40% before that buyout).
- Revenue-sharing today is informal/ad hoc — Peter (treated as the de facto "employee" of the arrangement) covers business expenses and they split remaining cash based on need at year-end, rather than a fixed percentage.
- Scale disclosed in volume, not dollars (a deliberate choice — farmers in this community consider discussing raw revenue somewhat "tacky"/against the industry's culture): shipped **100,000 lbs of onions** in their record year; explicitly declines to be the cheapest option, prioritizing customer care and reliable fast shipping (UPS) instead.
- Marketing note: found short-form social video (a simple ~30-second unscripted clip — farm footage, harvesting, packing shed) to be a genuinely effective, low-cost acquisition channel for this DTC food product, a shift from his earlier SEO/directory-only approach.
- Tools used for this business: WordPress (site), **Shopify** (checkout/transactions), **ShipStation** (order/shipping management for tens of thousands of orders/year), **Clicky Analytics** (preferred over Google Analytics/GA4 for years).
- Bought the plural **Onions.com** later (~$14,800 after negotiating down from an $18,000 asking price) mostly as a "vanity"/branding play rather than for direct traffic gain — primarily used today for professional branded email addresses for farm staff (e.g., AriesOnions.com) since it impresses people at industry events and is far easier for elderly customers to type/spell correctly than "Vidalia." Notes it does pull some incidental type-in traffic (~50/day) and could eventually support a broader "we farm everything" positioning (analogous to how Monster.com's name no longer strictly limits its business).

## General patterns and philosophy (from Peter, echoed/paraphrased by the host)

- Peter initially saw himself as a "solo builder" but in hindsight every one of his successful projects (Dude Ranch, Ranch Work, Vidalia) depended on finding the right **partner** who instantly "got it," after multiple other people passed. His consistent negotiating approach: offer partners a **no-risk, no-brainer deal** (e.g., "keep 100% control, I take all the work/risk, we split only if it works") to lower the barrier to a yes.
- No formal growth targets/KPIs by design — deliberately avoids setting hard numeric goals (e.g., a specific yearly shipping-weight target), comparing his approach to natural/organic growth ("a redwood doesn't set a goal to be 200 feet tall by a date") and arguing that hitting an artificial goal can create false satisfaction while missing one creates unnecessary self-criticism.
- Describes developing a "failure callus" from years of side projects that didn't pan out (sites getting de-indexed, breaking, or simply not monetizing) — treats each failure as low-cost information rather than a setback, which let him keep iterating for roughly 5 years (2004 to the Dude Ranch acquisition in 2009) before a repeatable model emerged, and another 5 years to Vidalia (2014).
- On today's opportunity: still sees value in **boutique e-commerce** businesses that don't require a "great" domain name, can be started from home for relatively little capital, and can be validated fast using short-form social video and paid social ads (Facebook Pixel tracking) rather than waiting on SEO/domain authority to build. Cites an unnamed example of a bootstrapped **premium/luxury version of a kids' pull wagon** business reportedly reaching multi-million-dollar revenue via strong paid social, and a specialty gut-health pickle brand as further evidence food/novelty DTC niches remain wide open.
- On whether content directories remain viable in the AI era: argues yes — cites Dude Ranch's traffic being up ~39% year-over-year, attributing part of that to AI tools/chat assistants citing his site as a trusted source of first-party info (real photos/video/background) when users ask for niche recommendations (e.g., "dude ranches with pickleball near a major airport").
- Mentions philanthropic/vanity domain purchases unrelated to revenue (e.g., buying Miss.com and donating it to the University of Mississippi, his alma mater) as evidence this is as much a hobby/passion practice as a business strategy for him.
