# AI Agents Are Overhyped. Use THIS Instead
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u7dJlv9bouA | Published: 2025-04-04

Podcast-style episode (Chris Koerner + Nik of Cofounders) mixing AI tooling advice with a few standalone business ideas.

## AI tooling framework (for research / idea generation, not a standalone hustle)
- Getting-started framework for AI tools: (1) pick one of ChatGPT/Perplexity/Gemini and get its paid plan (~$20/mo); (2) stop using Google for anything non-geographic (restaurants, hours, sports scores stay on Google; everything else — facts, research — goes to the AI); (3) anything you'd normally hand to an expert/assistant or bounce ideas off (contracts, financial statements, long documents) — upload it to the AI and interrogate it.
- Perplexity's "deep research" ($20/mo) was rated as good as or better than ChatGPT's $200/mo Pro plan for research tasks, mainly due to UI/organization.
- Custom GPTs: useful mainly as a way to save a detailed recurring prompt (e.g., a "weekly newsletter GPT" trained via examples to pull business ideas out of podcast transcripts in a specific format) rather than as true fine-tuning. Uploading a full personal dataset (e.g. all of your tweets) and expecting a good custom GPT out of it was described as generally disappointing.
- Prompting lesson: be as specific as you'd be briefing a human employee (exact scope, sample size caveats — e.g. specify "all 520 rows" or the AI may silently sample only ~10 rows) — vague prompts get eager-to-please but wrong output, same as a new employee.
- Distinction drawn between true "agents" (do open-ended tasks autonomously with reasoning) vs. what's currently sold as agents (Operator, Lindy, Gumloop, Zapier AI, etc.) which the hosts felt behaves more like traditional RPA (rigid, scripted automation) and is often slower/more manual than doing it yourself. Deep research tools (Perplexity, ChatGPT deep research) are argued to already be more "agentic" and useful today than dedicated agent products.

## Business idea: AI-generated forward-looking trend/industry reports (contributed by a viewer, Zach Ashburn)
- Premise: industries that must commit to production/inventory decisions far in advance (e.g., fashion brands deciding a year+ ahead what colors/fabrics will be popular) currently buy expensive third-party trend reports.
- Pitch: use AI deep research tools to generate similar forward-looking trend reports and sell them at a much lower price than incumbent research firms.
- Other industries flagged as good fits (same lag-time-between-trend-and-production dynamic): food & beverage (what will be popular in a year), general retail/consumer products (using Google Trends data, e.g. spotting "never touch plastic.com" as a rising product category), technology (translating top Google Trends items into "what this means for your industry" reports), automotive (predicting long-term problems in current car models, e.g. LCD screens needing replacement in ~5 years).
- No specific pricing given for this idea; framed as "sell forward-looking reports cheaper than incumbents currently charge."

## Business idea: illustrative research example — banana ketchup (demonstrates the research method, not a business pitch per se)
- Banana ketchup is ~90% of the ketchup market in the Philippines; US market for it is ~$225M/year, growing ~7%/year, but search volume (Google Trends) is described as "booming."
- Suggested retail price point if launching a US product: $4-6/bottle.
- Framed as an early-stage "Sriracha-like" opportunity, partly riding the trend of consumers avoiding red dye (recently banned/restricted in the US) in condiments.
- Method demonstrated: use deep research to find rising foreign foods/trends, cross-check via Google Trends for "redundancy" (confirming the AI's claim with independent data), then do a targeted deep-research prompt (e.g. "deep dive on banana ketchup in the Philippines and launching something similar in the US — is there an opportunity?").

## Business idea: mobile/pop-up light-up floor tile arcade (inspired by "Activate Games" franchise concept)
- Reference point: Activate Games, a ~4,000 sq ft family entertainment franchise (like an upgraded Chuck E. Cheese/escape room/trampoline park hybrid) — rooms full of interactive light-up floor tiles and touchscreen games, $35-45/person entry, hundreds of mini-games across ~15 rooms. Estimated (from public booking-slot data at 100% occupancy) to do roughly $7M/year at a single location.
- Smaller-scale/mobile spinoff idea (sourced from a Twitter user who sells this hardware): buy the interactive light-up floor tile hardware+software (~$15,000 for a full room setup) and either (a) set up a fixed room and charge $10-20/person for ~10 games, or (b) run it as a mobile side hustle — bring the tile setup to corporate picnics, school fundraisers/carnivals, parks, outdoor shopping areas.
- Claimed unit economics (from the tile equipment seller, so treat as biased/unverified): charge $25/person, 6-8 people per session, 5-6 sessions/day = ~$1,000/day; claimed the $15,000 setup can pay for itself in about a month.
- Setup time estimated at 30-60 minutes (roughly $20 of labor) for a mobile deployment.
- Suggested a college-town/dorm placement (e.g., a "dry" campus like BYU/Provo, Utah with no alcohol) as a strong niche location for a date-night version of this.

## Business idea (illustrative story, not a direct how-to): vending machine retrofitting for college campuses
- Story of entrepreneur Ethan Cohen (later on host's podcast): after losing money gambling, started an informal campus sportsbook, then pivoted into buying old/used vending machines cheap (~$3,000 each) and retrofitting them with credit-card readers (using an Israeli payments company, not named) to sell nicotine pouches (Zyn) in his dorm/frat house — reportedly making ~$3,000/month from one machine.
- After a video of a broken machine went viral (3M+ views on Barstool Sports), he got ~100 inbound orders for retrofitted machines, took a $250,000 friends-and-family loan to scale, projecting ~$80,000/month profit — then COVID-19 (March 2020) shut down campus placement entirely, forcing a pivot.
- Key transferable idea: buying cheap used vending machine "shells" and retrofitting with modern electronic/credit-card payment hardware is cheaper than buying new machines, and using college campuses as a captive distribution channel is a reusable model beyond vending (e.g., other campus-distribution business ideas).

## Caveats/risks
- Claims from the floor-tile equipment seller ($15,000 setup paying for itself in a month) are flagged by the hosts as coming from someone with a financial interest in the claim ("he's selling these things") — no independent verification given.
- AI "agents" as currently marketed are characterized as overhyped relative to actual capability; hosts had not found a true autonomous agent that reliably completes open-ended tasks without heavy babysitting (Lindy specifically cited as failing at inbox integration after hours of setup).
- Campus-based businesses carry concentration/timing risk — the vending machine business was nearly wiped out by COVID campus shutdowns despite rapid growth just before.
