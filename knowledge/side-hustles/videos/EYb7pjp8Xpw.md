# How Can I Start and Grow a Pet Cremation Business?
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EYb7pjp8Xpw | Published: 2024-07-16

Live call-in Q&A episode covering four callers' business ideas. Distilled by segment.

## Quiet study-room rental business ("anti-WeWork/anti-Starbucks")
- Idea origin: caller noticed 20-30 people lining up daily outside a public library (McKinney, TX area) waiting for it to open, because there's no quiet, private, pay-as-you-go workspace alternative — not a WeWork ($200+/month membership), not a coffee shop (noisy).
- Target use cases identified beyond studying: proctored/remote certification exams (e.g., PMP) that require a quiet, monitored room — floated as a potential B2B partnership angle (becoming an "approved referral provider" to testing centers/Pearson VUE).
- **Validation method recommended before building anything**: go stand at the library door and survey the waiting crowd directly — ask age/gender/occupation (or estimate), why they're there (renting books vs. studying vs. working), and repeat over 5 consecutive nights to get real demand data and headcounts, before committing capital.
- **Physical build-out concept discussed**: either retrofit a 40-ft shipping container (spray foam insulation for soundproofing, ~4 small ~8x8 offices off a narrow central hallway) as a cheap way to test the model, or rent ~1,000 sq ft of commercial space (analogous to a Massage Envy layout — hallway plus small private rooms, self-service electronic door codes, no communal area since privacy/quiet is the entire point) once validated.
- **Unit economics modeled live (illustrative, not validated)**: ~10 rooms in 1,000 sq ft; at $5/hour, 24/7 theoretical revenue ceiling of $1,200/day, but realistic estimate around $36,000/month gross at low utilization; at $10/hour and 50% capacity, one estimate reached ~$300,000/year net after costs. Rent estimated at ~$3,000/month (under 10% of gross), staffing minimal (one part-time attendant, unattended most of the time like an Anytime Fitness), implying potential 80% gross margins at scale. Rough customer-count sanity check: ~80-100 regular customers using the space ~3x/week for ~2 hours could support the $250-300K/year range; a flat ~$100/month membership (with free room booking) suggested as potentially more attractive/sellable than a high per-visit rate.
- **Key caution given**: test the concept (via direct customer surveys and possibly a Facebook Marketplace A/B listing test — see next item) before signing a long-term commercial lease with a personal guarantee.

## Facebook Marketplace fake-listing demand test (general technique, mentioned for an RV park idea but broadly applicable)
- To validate demand for a not-yet-built offering: post two nearly identical Facebook Marketplace listings side by side in the same market — one advertising the new/planned concept (with its differentiators, e.g., more space, a playground) using stock/borrowed photos, and one advertising a generic version as if it were just like every existing competitor.
- Compare click-through and message/inquiry rates between the two. A meaningfully higher (3-5x) response rate on the differentiated listing is a signal of real demand; a similar response rate suggests the differentiation doesn't matter to the market. Caveat explicitly stated: this measures interest, not actual willingness to pay — "people speak with their money," and this test collects no money.

## Pet cremation business (courier/broker model, avoiding incinerator capex)
- Core model discussed (used by both caller's Phoenix startup and host's own Dallas business): instead of buying an incinerator (expensive, plus zoning hurdles), operate as a courier/logistics middleman — partner with an existing cremation facility that has excess capacity, pick up deceased pets (mostly from veterinary clinics, sometimes households), deliver to the facility, then return the ashes.
- Concrete pricing example given by the caller: retail markup structured around communal cremation (~$35), individual cremation (~$65, partially cut off in transcript but implied), and private cremation (~$135).
- **Vet-first customer acquisition strategy strongly recommended over consumer-direct**: direct-to-consumer acquisition (e.g., Google Ads) was described by the host as close to breakeven in his own experience — pet death is highly time-sensitive and unpredictable, so unless you're already top-of-mind (i.e., the vet) at the moment of need, the consumer will just Google a competitor. Vets, by contrast, are a stable, repeatable referral channel — prioritize larger multi-location vet groups first for efficiency.
- **Deal structure with a vet**: insert yourself as the middleman between the vet and the cremation facility — pitch to the vet is "change nothing about your workflow, we just handle logistics"; pitch to the cremation facility is "stop worrying about logistics" for a cut of margin.
- **More advanced version of the same play, pitched by the host as what his own company is doing**: go to a cremation facility that already has direct vet relationships and offer to buy their vans/take over their logistics function entirely — "sell us your vans, stay in your cremation facility, give us some of your margin, and we'll grow your business by finding you more vets." This intentionally reduces the facility's top-line and possibly bottom-line in exchange for eliminating their capex/opex/staffing headaches (vehicles, drivers, callouts) and outsourcing growth (new vet acquisition) to the courier partner.
- Host's proprietary market research tidbit shared with the caller: after calling vets/cremation providers nationally for pricing data, found that the caller's target market's current pricing was likely underpriced relative to national norms — suggesting room to raise prices even in the "value-add, take some margin" vet partnership model.
- Ancillary revenue not yet captured by either operator: paw print/ear/memorial keepsake items — currently just handled as a pass-through logistics task (returned to the vet) with no margin taken, flagged as a possible future upsell.
- Caveats: zoning restrictions on incinerators are a real, non-trivial barrier (both operators hit this and pivoted away from owning cremation equipment); consumer-direct acquisition profitability was explicitly called out as weak/marginal via paid search.

## Yacht charter brokerage acquisition (SEO/lead-gen business for sale)
- Business for sale: a 15-year-old, fully virtual charter-yacht booking brokerage (British Virgin Islands boats), no owned physical assets (boats, crew) — pure lead-generation/booking-commission model connecting travelers with independent boat owners/crews. Average package price: $20,000-$30,000/week (rarely up to $100,000).
- Revenue model: flat 15% commission on bookings (not tiered by ticket size); reported ~$250,000-$280,000/year in what the seller called "revenue" but which the buyer (correctly, per the host) identified as **gross merchandise value (GMV)** — i.e., ~85% of that figure passes straight through to boat owners and isn't real revenue to the brokerage. True revenue is closer to the 15% commission take (~$37,000-$40,000/year on that GMV), which roughly matched the seller's stated ~$38,000 in reported "profit," implying the business essentially has almost no real operating costs (~$500-600/month: association fees, insurance, broker fees) once GMV/revenue accounting confusion is corrected.
- Volume: roughly 15-20 bookings/year (about 1-2/month).
- Deal terms discussed: seller offered to sell for $60,000 (about a 1.5-2x multiple on the ~$37-40K annual "profit"/commission revenue); buyer was contemplating countering lower (~$35-40K). Host's take: even $60K looked like a good deal given estimated time commitment of only ~1 hour/day and a ~2-year payback with zero additional value-add.
- **Key risk flagged**: business is heavily dependent on Google (organic SEO + paid ads) for lead flow — a single-channel dependency risk, especially amplified by AI-driven changes to Google's search results potentially disintermediating SEO-dependent sites.
- **Earnout/deal-structuring ideas proposed to hedge that risk**: (1) tie part of the purchase price to an earnout contingent on the buyer successfully diversifying traffic away from Google within a set time window (discount if they fail, full payout if they succeed) — can be structured to also reward the seller with upside if new channels the buyer develops (e.g., cold email, travel-agency partnerships) generate meaningful revenue; (2) alternative "option" structure: pay a small sum (e.g., a couple thousand dollars) for the exclusive right to buy the business at a fixed price after a defined trial period (e.g., 3 months) during which the buyer tests new customer-acquisition channels without full commitment — if it doesn't work out, the buyer only loses the small option fee.
- Buyer's angle/niche idea mentioned in passing: charters marketed to groups (e.g., three couples splitting a boat) as a differentiator versus a typical cruise-ship vacation.

## RV park expansion for blue-collar / oil-worker areas (recap only — recording failed)
- Caller (Daniel, Texas) wanted to build a new RV park from scratch targeting the same high-earning blue-collar/oil-worker demographic as existing nearby "man camp"-style parks (dense pad spacing, no amenities, built for workers doing 12-18 hour shifts), but differentiated with more space between RVs and added amenities (playground, etc.) at a similar price point.
- **Bear case given**: being the first to differentiate in a market doesn't guarantee success if the target customer doesn't actually value the differentiation enough to change behavior — illustrated with an analogy about a friend's healthy-food restaurant that failed in a mall food court because customers there wanted Panda Express, not salad ("people speak with their money, not their mouth"). Possible that oil workers, working extreme hours, simply don't care about extra space/amenities if price is equal, or that the local market is already saturated.
- **Bull case given**: host's own company owns a Louisiana park with a similar high-earning blue-collar tenant base where amenities (multiple pools, a lookout, golf carts) do meaningfully differentiate — there's a waitlist for pads specifically because of amenities lacking at competing parks; so amenities can work, but it's market-dependent.
- **Recommendation**: don't build from scratch — replacement cost to build a new RV park is typically higher than buying an existing park off-market; advised looking for acquisition targets instead. Also recommended the Facebook Marketplace dual-listing demand test described above (post an ad for the not-yet-built park's concept vs. a generic listing, compare response rates) before committing to a construction loan.
