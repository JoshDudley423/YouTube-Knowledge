# The 5 Most Genius Business Ideas I Saw on Instagram
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IMq85OF3GsY | Published: 2025-02-11

Co-hosted episode ("IGenius" segment) with Nik (Nikonomics/Co-Founders Podcast) reacting to viral Instagram business concepts and extracting reusable frameworks. Lighter/more speculative than most episodes — ideas are reactions to viral reels, not verified case studies.

## Business ideas discussed

- **Experiential add-on for existing low-differentiation businesses (e.g., "sandbox coffee shop")**: viral example (30M+ views) of a Dubai coffee shop with a large sandbox and remote-control toys/excavators inside, drawing both kids and adults. Framing: coffee itself doesn't need to be good — the experience differentiates an otherwise commodity business (coffee shops "are all about the same") for very low incremental cost. Business model variant: **"sandbox/experience-as-a-service"** — build and install this experiential add-on for other existing businesses (coffee shops, restaurants, trampoline parks — anywhere with existing foot traffic matching the target demographic) either as a paid installation/management service, or as a revenue-share/referral model (bring the business customers, take ~30% cut, become a pure "distribution channel" with no fulfillment responsibility).
- **Upmarket kids' play area with a premium "parent lounge"**: real example cited — a Saratoga Springs, Utah play space (climbing/trampoline-park style equipment) run on a "Planet Fitness" pricing model: ~$20/month unlimited play per kid (maybe ~$10 for additional kids). Differentiator: an elevated platform where parents can watch their kids while enjoying WeWork-quality amenities — good food, fast Wi-Fi, leather couches. Compared to pickleball clubs pairing courts with an upstairs bar/co-working space. Reported to be crushing it as a first location (owners reportedly not sleeping since opening due to demand) though Utah is called an unusually favorable/saturated-with-families market, so results may not generalize.
- **Framework: don't compete in the "messy middle"** — pick either the lowest-cost option (own being the cheap, high-volume Chuck E. Cheese-style operator) or the premium option (charge significantly more and be clearly better); competing on both price and quality simultaneously is described as a difficult, unstable position. If pursuing the premium/experiential angle, execute it well (no visible "homemade," half-finished feel) or go the opposite direction and lean into rawness on purpose (e.g., "bring your own excavator, we have a real dig pit").
- **"Cold wet socks" health-trend product/kit**: viral wellness claim (10M+ views) that applying cold wet cotton socks then dry wool socks over them before bed improves circulation/immunity. Hosts are skeptical of the underlying science but note the view count proves demand exists ("even if entertainment demand"). Suggested improvements if monetizing this style of trend: (1) clarify/sharpen the specific problem being solved rather than vague "overall health" claims, since a clear specific pain point sells better; (2) market to adults instead of kids (skeptical it would survive a real multi-kid bedtime routine); (3) products that show a visible before/after (like a wart or skin condition disappearing) are more convincing/marketable than an invisible internal claim like "better circulation."
- **Wart-removal kit** (based on a personal home-remedy anecdote — soaking hands, or applying vitamin E gel + oral vitamin E pills nightly for ~2 weeks with socks over the hands to lock in moisture): framed as an example of a marketable "no-proof-needed" SMB/health-trend product, similar in appeal to "buy a business with no money down" pitches — sells on a simple, singular promised mechanism people want to believe.
- **Wholesale/niche e-commerce around the "microplastics" trend**: idea to sell reverse-osmosis filters or similar "avoid microplastics" products, riding rising consumer anxiety about microplastics in water/food. Anecdote: the speaker bought several microplastics-related domain names ~3 years prior (e.g. "neverusedplastic.com" style) purely speculatively and has since received unsolicited purchase offers for them, plus separate inbound interest in another plastics-related domain from an actual plastics company — used as evidence of real commercial interest in the space, though offer amounts were not disclosed as significant ("not very much").
- **"Save the fish" live vending/impulse-purchase mechanic for kids**: viral example of a claw-machine-style live-goldfish "rescue" vending machine (scoop a fish from a crowded tank, "save" it into a clean display tank, pay to take it home) in Asia. Generalized into a framework: **make an experience for a child so emotionally compelling ("I saved this fish!") that it becomes very hard for a parent to say no** to the purchase — described as letting the product/experience do the selling instead of a salesperson (analogous to a pet store letting a kid play with a puppy for 20 minutes, or a Lego/sporting-goods store letting kids play with a demo unit before buying). Framed as installable at existing retail traffic locations (e.g., a pet store) as a pure upsell mechanism.
- **Mobile bookstore (niche genre) in a repurposed vehicle**: real example — "Good Girl Books Knox" (Knoxville), a woman selling romance novels out of a pink car fitted with bookshelves, traveling to events, farmers markets, and conferences; reportedly doing well despite a very narrow niche. Extension idea discussed: a mobile bookstore stocked specifically with business books parked outside conferences/trade shows (e.g., CES) or on a target Main Street — could partner with a major publisher (e.g., Penguin) for wholesale book supply and follow niche conference circuits. Suggested differentiator needed vs. buying the same book on Amazon: added value like a QR code linking to bonus content, custom bookmarks, signed copies, or limited/first-edition print runs.
- **"Indistractable" framework** (borrowed from a media-industry contact who buys up magazine properties): physical media (a book, magazine, newspaper) commands full attention in a way phones/social feeds do not, because there's no notification pulling attention away. Cited as the reason a media investor keeps buying print magazines despite the print industry's decline. Applied as a general framework: businesses that get people into an "indistractable" physical activity (digging in a sandbox, holding a book) have a captive-attention advantage worth designing around.
- **Novelty/mobile-format halo effect**: any vending, mobile, food-truck, or party-bus-style business that is visually unusual gets a "positive confirmation bias" from onlookers simply for being new/different — the underlying idea doesn't have to be strong; being visually fresh and unique is itself part of the value.

## Numbers cited

- Kids' play area membership: ~$20/month unlimited play per kid, ~$10/month for additional kids (Planet Fitness-style pricing).
- Sandbox coffee shop viral video: reported ~30 million views on the original clip that inspired the segment.
- "Cold wet socks" video: ~10 million views cited as evidence of demand despite host skepticism about efficacy.
- Revenue-share model suggestion for the sandbox-as-a-service idea: roughly a 30% cut to the business bringing referrals/installation, though described as a rough/illustrative figure, not a tested rate.
- Historical TV-ratings tangent (not a business number, used to illustrate the "attention economy"/addiction framing): Oprah ~16M viewers/episode; Jerry Springer started at ~1-1.5M/episode and later peaked around 17-18M during a viral scandal episode.

## Caveats / risks

- These are reactions to viral social clips, not verified financial case studies — hosts explicitly note they're speculating/improving on ideas they don't have inside data for (e.g., "supposedly the owners have crushed it," "I don't know what's going on here").
- Utah kids'-play-area example is flagged as possibly an unusually favorable local market (family-dense), so its results may not transfer directly to other regions.
- Cold-wet-socks/wart-kit style "trend" products are explicitly framed as unproven, "bro-science"-style marketing plays rather than validated health claims — the hosts recommend leaning into the marketing/packaging opportunity while being aware the underlying efficacy claims are questionable.
- The "save the fish" concept drew an explicit ethical pushback in the discussion itself when extended to an analogous hypothetical with dogs (crowded kennel "rescue" mechanic), which the hosts call out as distasteful even while liking the underlying psychological framework.
- Mobile/novelty-format businesses need a genuine differentiator beyond novelty (e.g., something not replicable by just buying the same product on Amazon) to sustain repeat business once the initial novelty wears off.
