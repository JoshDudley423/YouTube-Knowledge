# How to Double the Value of an RV Park Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5YliRuIClwM | Published: 2024-06-27

An investor webinar/pitch (not a typical interview format) where Chris Koerner, acting as a co-GP with real estate firm Blue Metric, walks prospective accredited investors through the acquisition of an $8.6M RV park ("God's Country") near Shreveport, LA, explaining the value-add investment thesis for RV parks as an asset class. Less a "side hustle" and more a real-estate/small-business acquisition case study, but includes concrete operating and underwriting numbers relevant to running an RV park as a business.

## The specific deal (case study numbers)
- Property: ~113-acre RV park/campground near Shreveport, LA (technically in Blanchard, LA), purchase price $8.6M, closing raise of $6.5M equity + $6.5M debt (floating rate with a 5% floor / 9% ceiling, ~7.75% at time of recording).
- Existing amenities: gated/keypad entry, cement RV pad sites (rarer than gravel), a rented mobile home ($2,300/month), general store, arcade/game room (12 pool tables, adults-only), a community pool, a $100,000 shaded kids' play area, a stocked fishing lake, a rentable lodge (previously VRBO-only, being added to Airbnb), a second "east side" section with a steep water slide, glamping teepees, $48,000 "glamping" covered wagons with AC, stone cabins with hot tubs, and a bathhouse with heated/sensor Japanese toilets for primitive camping sites.
- Seller's reported 2023 NOI: $836,000. Buyer projects a *drop* in NOI in year 1 because they're replacing the previous owner-operator with a paid on-site manager, pushing the expense ratio from 32% to 39% before improving over time as revenue grows.
- Independent appraisal came in $676,000 above the purchase price, creating immediate "baked-in equity" for investors at closing.
- Investor terms: 10% preferred return (described as high end of Blue Metric's normal range, used because this raise was larger than typical), 2.0x target equity multiple, 25.6% target IRR, quarterly distributions, 50%+ minimum investment for accredited investors only. Waterfall: investors get 100% of first 10% return, 60% of the next tranche (10-15%), then 50/50 above 15% IRR.
- Value-add plan: add 37 new RV pad sites in the near term (a total of 75 planned over 5 years), which management claims (per the seller) would fill immediately given an existing waiting list — expected to more than double the park's NOI. Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) at acquisition: 1.57, projected to rise to ~3.0 by year 3 as value-add work completes.
- Adjacent catalyst (explicitly NOT included in underwriting/projections, treated as pure upside): a new 547-acre, state/Louisiana-Wildlife-Commission-funded mountain bike park ("One North") is being built next door with 23 miles of trails (rolled out over 7 phases, ~5 years to complete), designed by a top mountain-bike-park design firm from Bentonville, AR (the sport's "world headquarters"). This RV park will be the only lodging directly connected to the new bike park.

## General RV/mobile home park investment thesis (Blue Metric's playbook)
- Target acquisition profile: parks with "vacant land, strong occupancy, and poor marketing" — the three ingredients management says predict a successful value-add. Playbook: increase marketing, increase pad site count, increase occupancy, thereby increasing NOI.
- Preferred buy criteria: acquire at a 9-12% cap rate; refinance around year 3 to return most/all investor capital while keeping them in the deal for continued ("perpetual"/"infinite") returns; the fund does not plan to sell parks individually — the strategy is to aggregate many parks to compress the blended cap rate (from ~12% down to 5-6%) and eventually exit the whole portfolio to a private equity buyer.
- Cost benchmark: building new RV pad sites from scratch costs roughly $15,000 (low end) to $35,000 per pad; this park's existing revenue per site is cited as $11,500/year, implying a fast payback period for adding pads to an already-operating park versus building a park from zero.
- Occupancy figures cited: 89% blended occupancy across RV pads, cabins, and glamping units; RV pads specifically run close to 100% occupancy, while cabins/glamping run closer to 60% — this occupancy gap is the stated reason management prioritizes adding more RV pads over more cabins/glamping units.
- Revenue mix insight: nightly RV tenants pay $60-$100/night (potentially 3x the gross revenue of a monthly tenant, who pays around $700/month) — nightly tenants are far more profitable when a park already has an on-site manager, but require more active management. This is framed as a natural macro hedge: in a strong economy, more people buy RVs and travel, favoring higher-margin nightly stays; in a weak economy, the same parks naturally attract more long-term/full-time RV residents (noting many Americans live in RVs full-time for years) providing more stable, if lower-margin, occupancy — "no conversion process happens, you just naturally attract" the other tenant type as the market shifts.
- Site-selection strategy: prefer parks near national/state parks, major "attraction" lakes, or other big draws (compared explicitly to the logic behind Buc-ee's site selection). Explicitly avoid buying in Texas overall due to loose zoning — cited a specific 13-cap-rate deal in Abilene, TX passed on because three new competing parks could realistically open next door within a year; contrast with more supply-constrained/zoning-restricted markets (they primarily buy in the Southeast, states bordering Tennessee, plus Oklahoma) where new competing supply is harder to add.
- Firm's positioning in the market: deliberately targets a "messy middle" deal size (~$6-10M raises) that's too big for small retail buyers (who buy $0-2M parks) but too small for large institutional players like Blackstone (who are moving into mobile home parks but typically deploy $10M+ per deal in that asset class) — argued as a less-competed niche within an otherwise consolidating/roll-up-prone sector (side reference: someone rolled up 53 music schools in Denver and sold for $42M as a general example of roll-up dynamics applying broadly).
- Housing unit sourcing for cabins/glamping: rather than building structures from scratch, the firm buys and places pre-built units — cited examples: 48 used Disney "Fort Wilderness" single-wide cabins (with cabin-style facades) bought as a batch and distributed across multiple properties (Smoky Mountains, Montana, Mississippi, Tennessee); Canadian-made "yurts" at ~$15,000 each; luxury tiny homes from "Ever Rooms" (a company owned by one of the firm's co-GPs) at ~$150,000 each for lakeside placement; primitive campsites are built in-house since they're simple.
- Firm's operating model: vertically integrated — does not outsource park management or development (uses subcontractors but keeps a full-time in-house land-development employee), unlike syndicators who fully outsource operations.
- Track record disclosed: Blue Metric has acquired ~$50M worth of parks over 21 months, now valued at an estimated $80-90M; this would be roughly their 21st/22nd park acquisition; the firm has never missed a preferred-return payment and has never had to make an investor capital call (they intentionally raise more capital than the underwriting requires).
- Tax note: bonus depreciation of 50%+ (up to ~62% in some cases) passed through to investors in year one; flagged that recent legislation was reducing this benefit starting in 2025.

## Key risks disclosed (from Q&A)
- **CapEx/expense-ratio risk is the single biggest risk category** — cited a real prior mistake: at their second-ever park acquisition ("Newport"), an inadequate electrical inspection missed that service was only 30-amp (believed to be 50-amp), forcing a partial park shutdown (about a third of sites) for months during $20,000-$330,000 in repairs; this materially hurt that park's performance versus projections (though the fund overall states no park has been a total loss, and even the underperforming Newport park is still expected to return investor capital). Lesson applied going forward: much more rigorous electrical inspections before acquisition.
- **Wastewater/septic infrastructure** is called out explicitly as one of the biggest recurring diligence questions on any park acquisition ("how do we get the poop out") — city water/sewer connections are preferred; well water is usually fine; septic/treatment-plant issues are a common risk area.
- **Owner non-disclosure risk** — issues not discovered until after closing.
- Occupancy risk is explicitly described as *not* a major concern for this asset class historically (unlike other real estate types), the main occupancy threat being new competing supply entering a market (which is why they prioritize supply-constrained locations).
- Natural disaster risk (e.g., flooding in Louisiana) is mitigated via insurance covering property damage, liability, and up to a year of lost income.
- Refinance/exit assumptions: underwriting assumes 7-9% rates at year-3 refinance (no bet on rates dropping); year-5 exit is modeled as a full portfolio buyout (the most common outcome the firm has observed in the industry) rather than individual park sales, with cap-rate compression across the aggregated portfolio being the core value driver for investors.

## Caveats
- This is an active capital-raise pitch by a co-GP with a direct financial interest, not neutral analysis — all return projections (25.6% IRR, 2.0x equity multiple) are forward-looking targets, explicitly caveated multiple times ("lots of ifs") and dependent on execution of the pad-site expansion plan.
- The adjacent bike park's potential occupancy boost is explicitly excluded from the financial underwriting — treated as unconfirmed upside, not a projection input.
- This is a private, accredited-investor-only securities offering example, not a small/solo side-hustle concept — most relevant to someone considering passive real estate syndication investing or larger-scale park ownership, not a low-capital side hustle.
