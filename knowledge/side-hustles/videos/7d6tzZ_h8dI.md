# I Scraped Every Skool Community. This is What I Learned.
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7d6tzZ_h8dI | Published: 2024-09-20

Podcast/co-host episode (Chris + Nick) covering: Skool community economics, a "lead magnet agency" business idea, and a programmatic-SEO lead-gen business model (including selling programmatic sites to established companies instead of/alongside searchers buying businesses).

## Idea 1: Skool community data / analysis (not really a standalone business, but market intel)
- Skool (Alex Hormozi-backed paid community platform) — author scraped every public Skool group (member counts + price = revenue estimate), spreadsheet available free via the channel's newsletter (chriskoerner.com).
- ~80% of the top 50 groups are about "how to get rich," "how to look better," or "how to get girls" — the "how to get rich" niche is the dominant 80/20 within that 80%. Implied target demo: ~20-34 year old males.
- Average MRR of the top 50 groups (data a few months old at recording): ~$53,000/month. Largest: "Roadmap to $100k with Your Personal Brand," $1,000/month, 555 members ≈ $555,000/month.
- Notable outlier: #27 largest group is a "Jazz Guitar Accelerator" run by a Quebec-based coach (Mark Andre / jazzguitarlessons.[site], YouTube channel ~72k subscribers) — $200/month, ~250 members ≈ ~$55,000/month, plus YouTube ad revenue, a course, and a separate community on another platform. Framed as proof that niche-teaching skills with poor local monetization (session musician/teacher income) can be extremely lucrative online with the right distribution.
- Business-idea spinoff mentioned (not executed): approach niche teachers/coaches (music teachers, etc.) as an "agent," offering to build/run their community and marketing (reverse-engineering what a successful creator like Mark Andre did) in exchange for 20-50% of revenue.
- Caveat: communities can collapse fast — one example ("Zero to $10k/month," 100 members at $150/month = $15k MRR) no longer exists; the creator apparently quit.

## Idea 2: Lead-magnet building/consulting for local businesses
- Definition: a lead magnet is a landing page offer (calculator, quiz, white paper/ebook) that trades a useful tool/resource for a visitor's contact info. Cited examples: online business-valuation calculators, "how to start a tree trimming business" ebooks.
- Observation: most local businesses (unlike digital/online businesses) don't use lead magnets at all, even though they may already spend on PPC ads or SEO — a gap opportunity for someone entering entrepreneurship or freelancing.
- **Two business models proposed:**
  1. Build one great, reusable lead magnet for one business type/niche (e.g., a tree-trimming lead magnet), then resell/license the same asset to dozens of similar businesses across different markets; offer to track click/conversion performance for them.
  2. Cold-approach individual local businesses, show them data on lead-magnet value, offer to build one for free, then monetize on the back end via a percentage of resulting revenue or a per-lead fee.
- Recommendation: target **high-ticket service niches** (e.g., foundation repair, custom home building) rather than low-ticket ones (e.g., landscaping) since each lead is worth much more, making the pitch/ROI easier to sell. Example given: host spent ~12 hours building a "how to pick a custom home builder" ebook as a lead magnet for a home-building business partnership — considered but never launched selling it standalone as an Amazon ebook.
- Recommended "land and expand" approach: get in the door with one lead-magnet/lead-gen offering, then help the business owner actually convert those leads (many local owners don't track or manage inbound leads well — described anecdote of leads sitting unanswered in an inbox), and expand into broader sales/marketing services from there.
- For picking a niche: don't specialize immediately — test 5-10 industries, see which gets the best response, then double down on the winner rather than committing too early.
- Two entry-persona options suggested: (a) an industry insider (e.g., 10 years in home health/hospice) targeting that same industry using domain credibility, or (b) a generalist entrepreneur testing across several industries to find traction.
- Tactic named "strategically introduced friction": some SaaS tools (video editors, etc.) let users complete an entire task for free, then require payment only at the final export/download step, exploiting sunk-cost bias; similarly, long personality/IQ-test quizzes withhold results until email capture, driving near-100% conversion at that final step because users have already invested effort. Framed as counterintuitive to standard "reduce all friction" advice — sometimes adding a specific point of friction increases conversion or (in personal habit change) prevents unwanted behavior.

## Idea 3: Programmatic SEO lead-gen websites (business model + "sell to a company" twist)
- Definition: programmatically generating thousands of near-identical landing pages by combining data columns (e.g., top 10,000 US cities × a keyword like "grill cleaning") via automation (e.g., Zapier, not necessarily AI) to create a landing page + lead-capture form for each combination. Only ~10% of pages may ever rank, but at scale that's still meaningful organic traffic with near-zero marginal cost.
- Works best for services/products that are NOT geographically constrained in supply (i.e., the site owner doesn't need a local location to answer the lead) — examples given: a household degreaser product (sell it wholesale regardless of buyer's city), "how much do [job] graduates from [university] make" pages, "how much do [job title] make in [city]" pages, or business-broker directory pages by city (scrape all brokers from BizBuySell, build "best business broker in Minneapolis/Orlando/Tampa" pages).
- Author's own team is running an undisclosed ("stealth") programmatic SEO project and does agency work building programmatic sites for other clients — pricing for that agency service: $5,000-$15,000 per site build, with high margin since the actual production cost is low relative to price.
- **Alternative to buying a franchise**: pitch to business "searchers" (people actively looking to acquire a business, e.g., considering a $300k franchise purchase) that they instead buy a pre-built, already-organically-ranking programmatic lead-gen website for ~$20,000, then run paid ads on top of the existing organic traffic and handle sales/fulfillment themselves. Analogy used: like renting a fully-equipped former restaurant space (someone else's failed pizza place, all the kitchen equipment already there) instead of building a $500k build-out from scratch — still have to do the "hard part" (bring in customers, do the work) either way, but skip ~90% of the setup cost.
- **Twist — sell the pre-built site to an established company instead of a searcher**: build a programmatic site tied to a specific brand (e.g., a large plumbing company like "Baker Brothers," described as a $200-300M business) and pitch the CEO directly, offering guaranteed incremental lead volume; price much higher than the searcher price (e.g., $100k instead of $20k) since it's pre-branded and the buyer has the multi-location capacity to fully exploit the leads. Can create a competitive bidding dynamic by approaching the top 3-5 companies in a region simultaneously and letting them bid against each other.
- If a company balks that they could just have a developer copy the site cheaply, the counter is that a copy creates head-to-head competition splitting the same leads/rankings, which reduces the value for both parties — so the seller can credibly threaten to instead sell the same asset to a competitor if the first company declines.
- Fallback monetization if no company buys the site: keep it and run it as a standalone lead-gen business, selling individual leads to service companies (e.g., ~$20/lead cited for a plumbing lead), or set up a rev-share/affiliate relationship so the first company to respond to a lead gets it and pays per-lead. Framed as a way to "de-risk" the large time investment in building the site.
- Stated value insight: a single lead may be worth only ~$20 sold piecemeal to an unaffiliated buyer, but the same lead is worth much more (cited ~$900) to a vertically-integrated buyer who owns the funnel and captures full customer lifetime value.

## Caveats
- Programmatic SEO/lead-gen concept relies on identifying keyword/niche combinations with real search volume and low existing competition; most generated pages won't rank.
- Regulatory/geographic rollout constraints matter for some verticals (example cited: an alcohol-delivery programmatic site built neighborhood-by-neighborhood in Dallas because the business had to get state-by-state regulatory approval before expanding).
- Lead-magnet consulting's harder part isn't building the magnet, it's getting an often set-in-their-ways local business owner (example: a 62-year-old roofing business owner) to actually change behavior and act on the leads generated.

Skool, Zapier (for the programmatic automation), Google Sheets, BizBuySell (for scraping business broker listings), ChatGPT (implied for content), various free-then-paywalled SaaS video editors (example of "strategic friction").
