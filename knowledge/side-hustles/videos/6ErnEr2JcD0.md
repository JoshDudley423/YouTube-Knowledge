# The Most Hands-Off $192K/M Business Anyone Can Copy
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6ErnEr2JcD0 | Published: 2026-07-11

Guest: Luke Vanderweil, runs "lead generation website rental" business, websiterentalcoaching.com.

## The idea
- Build small, single-purpose local lead-gen websites (city + service niche, e.g. "WoodsideTowing.com," "GreenvilleGutterCleaning.com"), rank them organically on Google (map pack + organic), then rent the incoming phone leads to a local business owner in that niche for a flat monthly fee or a revenue share — you own the asset (the website/rankings), not a client relationship, so you can swap which business you route leads to at any time.
- Pitch to the origin story: Luke ran an SEO agency for clients, got angry at a client micromanaging him despite "stellar" results, and realized he didn't own anything — he owned "a job," not a business. He fired all clients (took a $108K/year immediate income hit) and rebuilt the same skillset into owned assets instead of client services.

## Numbers
- Most recent month: $192,500 in revenue across 232 sites (up drastically from the prior year, credited to shifting from flat fees to revenue-share deals).
- Reported >90% net profit margin on the passive/rental sites.
- Only spends 2-3 hours/week overseeing the rental-site portfolio (excludes newer equity/revenue-share deal work, which takes more time).
- Annual overhead (~$50K/year total) for the whole 232-site portfolio: 2 VAs (~$1,200/mo + ~$1,000/mo), cloud hosting ~$300/mo (shared hosting across many sites), CallRail (call tracking) ~$500-600/mo on a legacy plan, Rank Tracker ~$150/mo, Vultr cloud servers for map listings.
- Example single site (Irving, TX carpet cleaning): live since ~May 2017/2018, has earned the same buyer $500/month essentially the entire time (lifetime revenue estimated ~$50,000), same buyer never churned.
- First 6 sites built (towing x2, HVAC, limo, tree service, pest control) fully replaced his $108K agency income within a few months at ~$9,000-9,500/month combined.
- Example rental deal math: garage-door-style example — a towing site generating 30-40 calls/month rented for a flat $700/month; buyer converts ~40-50% of calls (12-20 jobs) worth an estimated $3,000-5,000+ in new revenue to them — strong ROI for the buyer.
- Revenue share terms: typically 20% of net revenue (sometimes gross, depending on the niche's margin structure — e.g., landscaping is taken as net because gross includes materials cost). Requires the business owner to grant him read access to their CRM/payment system (e.g. QuickBooks) to verify what they're actually closing before trusting a rev-share deal.
- One "profit interest" / equity-like deal: got 20% of a paving company valued at $3.5M in exchange for driving SEO leads — pays him revenue share now plus a cash-out when the company eventually sells.
- Later diversified into buying small businesses outright to use lead-gen skills to fix them and to offset taxes (bought a catering company for $1.7M using an SBA loan, ~$230K down; installed his brother as operator for 50% equity, remains a passive partner).

## Process / steps
1. Pick a service niche + a mid-size city (population ~60,000-500,000; avoid huge metros like Manhattan — too much competition, and avoid tiny towns with insufficient demand). Look for low organic/map competition: use thumbtack.com/near-me to browse service categories, then Google "[niche] [city]" and check: number of Google Maps reviews (fewer/lower = less sophisticated competition), whether competitors lack a real website or use only a Facebook/social profile as their "site," whether any competitor holds the exact-match keyword, and whether organic results are just inner pages on generic business sites rather than a dedicated niche site.
2. Cross-check search volume with Google Trends to avoid picking the wrong keyword variant (e.g., "radon mitigation" vs. "radon removal" — pick whichever people actually search).
3. Build a full site (not just a landing page) with a dedicated page for every individual service offered (e.g., separate pages for roof repair, roof installation, roof inspection, not just one bullet list) — signals topical authority to Google. Include prominent phone number/click-to-call AND a quote form (some leads want to call now, some prefer to fill a form).
4. Content is described as "the foundation" — aim to have dramatically more depth than every competitor (research all competitors' sites, including in other cities with heavier competition to see what a mature site looks like, then write far more complete content, e.g. 1,000+ words per service where competitors have 3 bullet points). Content produced by VAs using AI (mentions Claude) then human-edited/QA'd. Uses ChatGPT to generate FAQ content by prompting it to pull common consumer questions from Yelp/Reddit/social media for a given niche+city.
5. Prefer an exact-match domain (city + service, e.g. WoodsideTowing.com) — estimated to be only 5-10% of overall ranking success but makes early results come faster; if the exact domain is unavailable, add a modifier ("... Pros") to keep both keywords in the domain.
6. Set up a Google Business Profile / Google Maps listing for the site — biggest early win: local map pack in a low-competition city can hit #1 almost immediately with no backlinks.
7. Wait roughly 2 months for a new site to accumulate rankings and inbound calls before actively shopping the leads.
8. Find a buyer: cold call local business owners ("I've got customers that want this, can we work out a cut?"). Prioritize businesses already spending on Google/Facebook ads (found via Google search or Meta Ads Library) — that signals they already believe in marketing spend and have budget. Avoid national companies/call centers; want a real local owner.
9. Vet the buyer before committing: ask how fast they follow up with a lead (if they say "today" and don't call today, drop them); do "shadow calls" (secret-shopper style) to hear how professionally they answer the phone; verify closes by calling customers later posing as a satisfaction survey to confirm the business actually converted the lead and charged what they claimed.
10. Give free leads for only a few days as a trial, not longer ("free has no value" — protects the asset from being exploited for free).
11. Start deals as flat monthly fee or referral/commission, then pitch a switch to revenue share once the relationship is proven — revenue share dramatically increased Luke's income vs. flat fees, since it scales with the buyer's success rather than capping out.
12. Bill via Stripe with card on file / autopay — deliberately avoids invoicing so the buyer has no leverage/control over payment timing.

## Niche selection guidance
- Sweet spot: high-enough ticket value that leads are worth paying for, but the business type is small enough that you can reach the owner directly on the phone (not a gatekeeper/secretary). Good owner-accessible niches: towing, tree service, pest control, some landscaping (only if landscape design, not plain mowing — mowing lead volume needed to be worthwhile is too high, e.g. "8,000 leads to get paid anything"). Harder to reach the owner: HVAC, plumbing, limo services (secretary/gatekeeper). Very high-ticket but inaccessible owners (e.g. dentistry) require a personal referral/intro to work.
- Explicitly recommends avoiding red-ocean niches like concrete, roofing, plumbing where many people already compete, in favor of niches/geographies with little to no real competition — with "10 million" possible service x geography combinations in the US (his own rough math: ~10,000 rankable geographies x ~1,000 service categories), there's no need to fight over saturated niches.
- Niches to specifically avoid, from direct experience: elevator repair (ranked #1, got leads, but couldn't rent the site — elevator companies are locked into long-term contracts with steep early-termination fees, so searchers browsing alternatives never actually switch); private jet rental (found only an unreliable middleman/broker to sell leads to, no direct access to jet owners, deal fell apart when the broker tried to overcharge).
- SEO/content quality is described as the "last 10-20%" that can multiply a site's value (e.g., turning a $400/month site into a $4,000/month site), but niche/geography selection is described as more fundamentally important.

## Tools/platforms named
- WordPress (current site builder; used Weebly originally). Elementor, Divi, Beaver Builder for no-code page building.
- thumbtack.com/near-me — for browsing niche categories to find ideas.
- Google Trends — to compare keyword variants by search volume.
- Google Maps / Google Business Profile (GMB) — critical ranking channel; can get suspended (happened to his first towing site, which then tanked because most towing leads come from map/mobile calls, not organic search).
- ChatGPT/Claude — content generation (FAQ research, VA-assisted drafting).
- SEMrush — off-the-shelf tool for researching supply/demand gaps; Luke also has a custom-built private research tool made by a developer friend.
- CallRail — call tracking/reporting.
- Rank Tracker — rank tracking.
- Vultr — cloud server hosting for map listings/infrastructure.
- Stripe — billing/autopay for lease-the-leads clients.
- Meta Ads Library — used to identify local businesses already spending on Facebook ads (a buyer-qualification signal).

## Caveats / risks
- Finding a serious buyer is harder than generating the leads themselves — many prospects are emotionally resistant even when the math clearly favors them (e.g., turned down leads worth an estimated $20K in revenue over a $1,000 fee) because they're used to running on referrals and are "prideful" about it.
- Revenue-share/profit-interest deals require a high level of trust and usually an existing relationship — hard to do cold with a brand-new business owner; requires access to their financial systems to verify honesty.
- Some verticals look great on paper (high ticket, ranks easily) but fail commercially due to contract lock-in (elevator repair) or lack of a direct buyer (private jet rental) — a niche isn't validated until you've actually tried to sell the leads.
- Getting Google Business Profile listings for many niches/geographies is getting stricter; Luke typically waits until he already has an established, paying relationship with a real local business before asking them to help legitimize additional GMB listings, rather than spinning up fake LLCs (possible but not worth the tax/legal complexity unless a niche is very high value).
- Success in this business created an unexpected tax problem (large recurring pass-through income); his CPA's fix was reinvesting in real estate or acquiring an operating business to absorb income via depreciation/FF&E write-offs — but that just creates a new income stream to manage/tax next year, so it's an ongoing cycle, not a permanent fix.
- Rev share requires the business's margins/reporting to be verifiable; not all niches make "net" easy to define (e.g., landscaping's material costs complicate what counts as net revenue).
