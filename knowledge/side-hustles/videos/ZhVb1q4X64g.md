# Secrets to Starting a 7 Figure Grill Cleaning Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZhVb1q4X64g | Published: 20240918

Brainstorming session (part 1 of 2) with Connor (owns self-storage, recruiting, and e-commerce businesses; runs a newsletter at bizbrainstorms.com). Two main ideas covered: a grill-cleaning service business, and a podcast/content "A/B testing" agency. Framed using Connor's revenue-tier system: $100K/year idea, $1M/year idea (potentially much bigger), and "reliable $10M/year" business categories — grill cleaning and the content agency are both discussed as base ideas that need upsells/scaling mechanics layered on to grow past their natural ceiling.

## Idea 1: Grill cleaning business

### The base idea and numbers
- Service: deep-clean a homeowner's grill (grates, smoke stains, grease buildup) — pitched as a seasonal (roughly 6-month, Northeast climate) service.
- Anecdotal price point: a real quote Connor's father received for a "nice grill" cleaning was $300; the two hosts estimate a competitive/attractive price point around $200 per job.
- Base-case math (as a solo operator/side-hustler, e.g., a student home for the summer): $200/job, 2 jobs/day, 12/week ≈ $75,000/year for roughly 6 months of part-time-per-day work — categorized as a "$100K/year" ceiling business without structural changes, because scaling further means hiring/subbing labor which eats into thin margins.
- Rough value framing: cutting per-job time from ~3 hours (typical DIY/manual cleaning time) down to ~30 minutes via better tools/process makes $200/job equivalent to ~$400/hour of effective labor value.

### How to speed up fulfillment (the scalability lever)
- Bottleneck: grates need extended soaking (hours) in a cleaning solution/acid — this, not the scrubbing itself, is identified as the true bottleneck limiting how many jobs one person can do per day.
- Proposed model (borrowed from mobile e-scooter [Bird/Lime] battery-swap subcontractor logistics): drive a route through a neighborhood, use a handheld device (blower/vacuum-style tool) to quickly detach, label/tag, and collect grates from multiple customers in a single pass (~5 minutes per stop) without needing individually scheduled appointments — customers just "opt in" (like a scout-troop canned-food-drive collection model) rather than booking a specific time slot.
- Batch-process all collected grates overnight at a central location/warehouse (soak, pressure-wash, dry), then return them on the next pass, doing final polish work at drop-off.
- This turns per-unit fulfillment time from ~3 hours down to as little as ~5 minutes of the technician's direct time per unit, which is what would make the business genuinely scalable (i.e., viable to hire out labor for, since fulfillment cost per unit drops dramatically).
- Visual/marketing hook: leverages the strong "before and after" appeal of a clean grill for organic social content — cited comparison to a viral tweet (22M views, retweeted by Paul Graham) about a renovated train car, where the poster attributed its virality specifically to a strong before/after first image; also compared to the inherent appeal of pressure-washing "ASMR" content on social media.

### Upsell ideas discussed (to push past the $100K ceiling)
- **Propane tank swap-outs**: carry a spare tank in the vehicle, offer a swap for a flat ~$25 fee if a customer's tank seems low — roughly a 10% revenue bump per job with minimal added effort (self-recycling/refilling propane tanks was noted as expensive, so a straight swap using a local gas-station exchange is simpler).
- **Marinade/cleaning spray product**: inspired by the "clean/marinate a grill with a cut onion" folk technique — potential branded "onion juice spray" product that doubles as a grill cleaner and marinade, sold as an add-on.
- **Selling grill-adjacent hard goods via a mobile mini-e-commerce model**: stock the same van used for pickups with a small inventory of high-margin outdoor cooking accessories — e.g., knockoff/aftermarket pizza-oven attachments (name-brand ones like Ooni or Roccbox cost $500-700 retail; near-identical alternatives sourced from AliExpress cost roughly $150), or trending grill tools discovered by scouting viral cooking-tool videos on Instagram, then sourcing matching/similar products cheaply from AliExpress. Example economics given: marking up a $150 pizza-oven knockoff by $100 (selling around $250-300, still ~$300 cheaper than a name-brand unit) could increase per-customer margin by roughly 50% from that single upsell alone.
- **Flavor-infusion consumables**: sell cedar planks (used to grill fish/salmon, imparting flavor) as a recurring consumable upsell — since planks get used up, this creates a natural reason for repeat customer contact/reorders, which can also be bundled with a repeat grill-cleaning pitch.
- **Adjacent outdoor cleaning services**: since grill owners are self-selected "people who like things clean" (and are more likely to already be buyers of other cleaning services), upsell or cross-sell other outdoor cleaning jobs at the same visit — e.g., outdoor fridge scrub-downs (+$30), patio/outdoor furniture pressure washing. Compared structurally to a Christmas-light installation business: a generalized service (grill cleaning) pairs well with a complementary seasonal niche service (e.g., something for the off months) to smooth out a business that would otherwise be highly seasonal.
- General framing: use the one-time/annual "no-brainer" service (grill cleaning) as a low-friction foot-in-the-door, then "land and expand" into higher-frequency or higher-margin adjacent offers with the same customer base.

## Idea 2: Podcast/content "A/B testing" agency (categorized ~$1M/year business, potentially much larger)

### The concept
- Sell a service to podcasters/YouTubers: take their raw long-form recordings, use AI-clipping software to auto-generate many short clips (5-15+ per episode), publish all of them simultaneously across TikTok/Instagram/YouTube Shorts, and then use resulting view/retention data to identify which specific segments/angles of an episode actually resonate — informing which angle to title/promote the full episode with, or which parts to cut in editing.
- Directly inspired by (and modeled after) a technique attributed to Steven Bartlett (Diary of a CEO): reportedly publishing AI-generated clips before an episode's full release, to learn what content resonates and adjust editing/promotion accordingly.
- Even brand-new/zero-follower social accounts still get meaningful algorithmic distribution on TikTok/YouTube Shorts/Instagram, making this data-gathering approach viable without needing an existing audience.
- Chris (the host) had already started doing this personally for his own podcast at the time of the episode, using a real example (an unreleased episode with a guest) where two test clips covering the same underlying idea from different angles showed one being a clear cross-platform "winner" — an explicit content A/B test.

### Revised/refined idea: apply the same method to paid ad creative
- Instead of (or in addition to) selling this to podcasters (who often have limited budgets — "99% of podcasts don't make money"), sell it as a service to **advertisers/agencies/e-commerce brands** running video ad campaigns: systematically test many different hooks/angles/offers on the same underlying ad content to find what drives the longest watch time or best conversion, framed through an "organic-feeling" content lens rather than an obvious ad.
- Rationale: this audience has much larger budgets and higher willingness to pay for incremental performance gains — cited real conversation where an advertising-agency contact said getting even 5 extra seconds of ad watch time could be a "million-dollar decision," implying strong willingness to pay for this optimization service.
- Related creative tactic discussed: **fake "podcast-style" hook ads** — recording a testimonial or pitch that visually mimics a well-known podcast setup (e.g., staging to look like a Joe Rogan-style background/mic/headset) to borrow the "this feels like real, organically-interesting content" credibility of podcast interviews, then naturally working in a product mention. A proposed micro-agency spinoff: charge something like $10K to produce ~80 different "hook" variations (scripted by a writer/strategist team) for a client's ad campaign, using techniques like on-camera wardrobe/background changes mid-recording to reduce creative fatigue across variants.

### Sizing the market (rough TAM exercise)
- Assumption: ~200 broad content categories exist (business, marketing, health, etc.); in each, maybe the top 50 podcasts make "good money" from advertising.
- This is heavily power-law distributed — cited real example: a top podcaster (unnamed but implied to be Joe Rogan-tier) reportedly charges ~$50,000 per ad, up to 5 ads per episode (~$250K/episode), 2 episodes/week → roughly $500K/week, ~$26M/year in ad revenue alone — illustrating how concentrated ad dollars are at the very top.
- Rough TAM estimate: top 25 shows per category × 200 categories ≈ 5,000 podcasts as realistic target customers; capturing just 1% of that (50 customers) at $1,500/month ≈ $75K/month, framed as a plausible path to a $1M/year business.
- Existing analog/competitor tool mentioned: **PickFu** — a rapid A/B-testing service where you submit two content variations plus a target demographic and get direct consumer preference feedback; framed as the "tried and true" version of the same underlying idea, whereas the proposed agency instead uses free algorithmic distribution (real platform data) rather than paid survey-panel feedback.

### Tools/platforms named
- Unnamed AI video-clipping software that ingests a full episode and outputs multiple short clips with auto-added captions/b-roll (used personally by Chris; not a specific product endorsement in this transcript).
- Riverside (podcast recording platform) — mentioned as something a client might share login access to for content sourcing.
- PickFu — content/ad variant testing via paid consumer panels.
- Social Blade and Pod Status — mentioned in a tangential discussion about detecting fraudulent/inflated follower and download counts (relevant context, not part of either core business idea).

## Caveats / risks and ethics discussion (tangential but notable)
- Flagged as outright fraud (discussed, not recommended): buying fake podcast downloads/listens (cited price: $5-20 per 10,000 fake downloads) to fabricate inflated download-count screenshots used to solicit real sponsorship deals (e.g., from Shopify) — described as illegal if a podcaster affirmatively claims those numbers are real listeners to a sponsor.
- Separately flagged as ethically gray but not fraud: a real-estate podcast (named in the episode as "Best Ever CRE") allegedly produces low-effort/low-quality interview episodes purely for SEO/lead-funnel purposes (ranking when a guest's name is searched) rather than content quality — not illegal or technically dishonest, but low integrity.
- A specific fraud pattern described: purchasing fake social media followers (cited example: an unnamed interviewer with millions of followers gained in an implausibly short multi-day window, verifiable via Social Blade, with fake engagement patterns like generic emoji-only comments) to falsely project legitimacy/authority, then charging real high-profile guests real money (~$7,500/appearance) to appear — flagged as a real, occurring scam pattern in the podcasting/interview space, though it does not concern grill cleaning or the content agency idea directly.
