# A Rare Look Inside the Fly-In Neighborhood Business Model
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QOISCnOQo8A | Published: 2024-09-17

Interview with John Patten of Patten Properties (Airparkland.com), which took over development of Pecan Plantation, a ~4,000+ acre "fly-in neighborhood" (airpark community) an hour south of Dallas-Fort Worth. Niche real estate development business model, not a low-capital side hustle, but included in this topic for the business-model substance and land-development lessons.

## The business model: airpark / fly-in neighborhoods
- Concept: a residential community built around one or more private runways, where residents can taxi their own airplane directly to a hangar attached to their home. Originally started as an actual pecan plantation in the 1970s by the Anthony family; developed into ~3,400 existing homes before Patten Properties took over, with a plan to build out to ~4,500 total homes on lots averaging close to an acre.
- Market growth context: per the guest's research, there were roughly 20-30 fly-in communities in the US in the 1970s; today there are an estimated 600-700 — described as a fast-growing real estate niche. Guest personally knows of two more being developed in Tennessee.
- What differentiates a successful airpark from failed ones: proximity to a "real" surrounding town/amenities. Many airpark developments fail or underperform because the land/runway may be fine but there's nothing (restaurants, grocery, gas stations, things for kids) within a 30+ minute drive. Pecan Plantation succeeds because it's a largely self-sufficient small town (own grocery store, drug store, hardware store, gas station, country club, two golf courses, fire department/EMS, equestrian facilities, marina) while still being reasonably close to Dallas-Fort Worth.

## Deal structure (a replicable partnership model for buying/taking over a legacy development)
- The original family owners (elderly patriarch, ~92, after losing a son who ran the real estate side) wanted to sell but were so personally embedded in the community that an outright acquisition/takeover wasn't feasible/desirable for either side.
- Deal instead: Patten Properties visited the family's other flagship development (Grand Ranch, north of Houston) to establish credibility, then structured a partnership where the original family retains involvement (HOA, community relations) while Patten Properties takes over lot development, sales, marketing, and daily operations.
- Since the partnership began (Nov 2022) through the time of the interview: 350+ properties sold for ~$57 million in sales.
- Explicitly framed as a repeatable model: "with the right person we could replicate it... it's definitely a model if we found the right owners in the right project that we could roll out." Key criteria for doing this kind of deal: high-quality/scarce underlying asset, principled/good-character ownership on the other side, and a personal "sniff test" (would you want to live there yourself) that convinced them within 45 seconds of visiting.

## Numbers
- Remaining inventory at interview time: ~650 home sites left to sell (down from ~3,400 existing before the current phase, building toward ~4,500 total).
- Lot pricing: standard neighborhood lots (1/3 to 1/2 acre) ≈ $100,000. Airpark ("The Landings") taxiway lots (up to 52 remaining) priced in the low $200,000s; direct runway lots (only 2 remaining) priced higher. Larger acreage "ranch" lots (up to 7-8 acres, equestrian-oriented) range from the low $200,000s up to $400,000-500,000 depending on the property.
- Existing home price range across the whole community: roughly mid-$400,000s to low $2 million, varying by neighborhood/lot type.
- HOA cost: ~$200/month for standard home sites; an additional ~$55/month for airpark ("The Landings") membership — described as extremely low relative to the amenities provided (golf courses, clubhouse, gym, pools, kids' facilities, grocery store access, etc.), cited by the developer as the primary driver of the project's success ("pay $200 a month to have access to a mall is unheard of").
- Sales velocity: initial airpark lot release sold 60-80 lots in the first three months; guest expects to sell 4-5 lots per week on a new release for the remainder of the year.
- Insurance: because the community is long-established and mature, HOA-level insurance covers most needs; no unusually high insurance costs cited specific to being a fly-in community versus a standard development.

## Pricing lesson (explicitly generalizable business advice)
- When first pricing the airpark lots, the original family (via their shared project manager) pushed back that prices were too high and asked for a reduction to build sales momentum. The developer held firm on his higher pricing instead of discounting — and sold out quickly (60-80 lots in 3 months), proving the higher price was correct. Lesson stated explicitly: "business owners are much more likely to price their product or service way too low... own the value that you're offering."
- General land-development pricing philosophy stated: never aim to be the cheapest or the most expensive in a market — aim to deliver the best perceived *value* (price is what you pay, value is what you get).

## Buyer demographics / customer segments
- Airpark ("Landings") lots: primarily current or retired pilots (average lot size ~1.5 acres, allowing oversized hangars/barns), but also non-aviation buyers drawn to the extra space for RVs, cars, or other large hobby equipment.
- Entry-level neighborhood ("The Orchards," smaller lots): starter families or downsizing couples.
- "The Ranches" (up to 7 acres): equestrian/horse-focused buyers building larger compounds.
- Overall demographic mix described as deliberately broad/diverse across price points and lot types — "something for everybody," which the developer contrasts with many single-segment developments that only build one type of product.

## Noise/liability concerns (addressing common skepticism)
- Of the eventual ~4,500 homes, only ~200 will be in the airpark section itself, and of those maybe half to two-thirds actually keep an aircraft — noise/crash risk raised by online commenters is characterized by both host and guest as an overblown, largely theoretical concern not borne out by the guest's direct experience visiting the property repeatedly. Host notes a comparable personal experience owning an RV park next to a small airport with no resident complaints.
- Runway access for non-residents: allowed for prospective buyers doing flight visits, and informally for residents' visiting friends with aircraft (a new 12,000-gallon fuel tank and 1-acre guest parking area were built to support this) — explicitly NOT run as a revenue line; framed as a private amenity for residents and their guests only, not a public/commercial airstrip.

## Ancillary/future revenue lines discussed (potential business extensions)
- In-community real estate brokerage: resale inventory in the neighborhood has historically ranged from ~40 to ~120-130 homes available at any given time, suggesting a standing resale brokerage opportunity.
- Commercial development: as the community fills out, additional commercial space (a new Ace Hardware had just opened; the Anthony family retains some additional commercial land in the development) represents a future revenue opportunity, though not the current primary focus (which remains completing lot sales and full build-out).
- Pecan production itself (mail-order pecan business, candied/chocolate pecans) is retained entirely by the original Anthony family and is not part of Patten Properties' business — the trees are preserved by residents mainly for aesthetic/community-character reasons.

## Caveats
- This is capital-intensive, long-horizon land development (buying and financing thousands of acres, patiently building out and selling over years/decades) — the host explicitly contrasts this with his own preference for de-risking deals before closing (e.g., pre-leasing RV park vacancies before purchase), noting this kind of large-scale land development "is not for the faint of heart."
- The specific partnership structure (retaining a legacy family as a minority operational partner rather than a clean acquisition) worked because of the particular relationship/trust built with the Anthony family — the guest cautions it requires the right counterparties and isn't guaranteed to replicate with less principled sellers.
