# How to be a Creative Genius to Flip Items Online in 2026
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rOVseOGF7ow | Published: 2024-07-19

Part 2 of an interview with Shannon Jean (serial entrepreneur/flipper) on flipping items, deal-making, and using asymmetric information. Much of this episode is mindset/personal discussion (insecurities, spouse dynamics, storytelling as a branding tool) which is skipped below in favor of the concrete tactics and numbers.

- Market-research method for flipping in a new local market ("sister city" technique): before flipping an item type (example: trailers) in your own city, first run the research in a comparable "sister city" (similar size/demographics/unemployment, e.g. Houston as a baseline for Dallas) to establish a normal baseline of supply/demand. Process: log Facebook Marketplace daily, message every listing of the target item, track in a spreadsheet how many sellers respond, how many say "already sold," how many go unanswered. Compare that baseline against your own city's numbers -- meaningfully fewer responses / more "already sold" replies signals demand exceeds supply (opportunity); the opposite signals a saturated market to avoid. Recommended to repeat across multiple sister cities (e.g., also San Antonio) to build a solid baseline before acting locally.
- Related idea: instead of trailers (large item, ~$5,000 capital needed to buy one to flip), look at appliances, furniture, and other big/heavy items that most people don't want to physically handle (framed as "things that cause you to sweat in 103-degree heat") -- lower capital requirement (flipping ~$500 items) with less competition because of the physical effort barrier.
- Wholesale liquidation pallet reselling: buy Costco liquidation auctions / truckload liquidations (buying overstock/returned goods in bulk, e.g. spending ~$60,000 on a truckload), repalletize into smaller lots, and resell $1,000 pallets to other resellers via Facebook Marketplace and at local swap meets (walk around with a business card offering "I'll buy the truckload so you don't have to"). Rationale: most small resellers lack the capital or logistics know-how to buy at truckload scale themselves.
- Storage-unit auctions (pre-"Storage Wars" era): buy a locker's contents sight-unseen for a low price (example: $500 for an entire woodworking-shop storage unit), then resell to bystanders/other bidders on the spot -- in the example given, the seller flipped one tool from the unit for enough cash to cover the entire $500 purchase before even leaving the auction, making the rest of the unit's contents pure profit.
- High-ticket watch resale/liquidation brokering: got into reselling returned/liquidated watches for a major retailer worried about returned inventory ending up back on shelves; took a small percentage fee to manage logistics, financing, and the relationship rather than buying/reselling directly himself; connected with buyers via in-person networking (walking the NYC jewelry district, cold calls) until finding reliable buyers; described this relationship-brokering model as an "$8 million side hustle" done on a handshake deal (no formal contract).
- General principle on ticket size vs. buyer pool: there's a tradeoff between item price point and the number of potential buyers -- commodity items (e.g. t-shirts) have huge buyer pools but require huge volume to make money; higher-ticket items (e.g. golf carts at ~$2,000 profit each, high-ticket Bitcoin mining rigs at ~$15,000 each) have far fewer buyers but much higher profit per sale. Advice: start flipping at the ~$1,000 price point to build skill/confidence, then move upmarket to higher-ticket categories using the same fundamental sourcing/negotiation skills.
- Example of a very large deal built the same way: a ~95,000-unit iPhone liquidation deal -- described as "just another deal" once you've done 20 years of smaller reps building the same negotiation/logistics/security/buyer-sourcing skills.
- Asymmetric-information sourcing example (historical, illustrative of the underlying pattern): in college, exploiting early access to a local classified-ad paper's office (getting listings hours before public distribution) to be first to call and buy underpriced trucks for flipping -- offered as a template pattern (find where there's an information/timing edge), not a directly repeatable tactic today.
- Modern equivalent of the same "unfair advantage" pattern (offered as directly actionable today): hire a virtual assistant for ~$3/hour to continuously refresh Facebook Marketplace for a specific item type/ZIP code and instantly message sellers ("I'll be there in 10 minutes") the moment a new underpriced listing appears -- beating other buyers on response speed. Also cited: fully automated bots that scan Craigslist for people selling gold/jewelry using certain keyword phrasing, and immediately email a prepaid shipping label to get the seller to mail in their gold before anyone else responds.
- Revenue-stacking mindset: build multiple small income streams over time (podcast income cited as modest but has "paid for kids' school, bought trucks, funded vacations" cumulatively over 10 years) rather than expecting one big win; framed as a way to train pattern-recognition for spotting opportunities.
- Self-publishing/audience-monetization anecdote (adjacent revenue-stack example, not the main flipping topic): a book coach who wrote ~30 niche books (women's fitness at different life stages), sometimes earning ~$1,000-$5,000/month in royalties across the catalog by iterating the same content for adjacent niches (new moms, over-50s, etc.); also an anecdote about a printed book outselling its Kindle version 10x after including a note offering a free digital copy via email if the (low-res, unfixed) images didn't render well in print -- inadvertently built an email list this way.

Caveats/risks:
- Several of the largest numbers cited (the $8M watch side hustle, the 95,000-iPhone deal) are self-reported, informal-handshake-deal arrangements built over 20 years of relationship and reputation -- not something a beginner should expect to replicate quickly or without that same track record.
- The watch resale business specifically had real friction: customers falsely claiming built-in altimeters malfunctioned after taking watches on flights and demanding refunds -- a genuine product/warranty headache in that niche.
- The truck-flipping-via-early-classified-access anecdote involved never registering purchased vehicles in their name, which the guest explicitly flags in hindsight as "stupid risk" he wouldn't take today, despite it working out at the time.
- Costco liquidation/truckload buying requires meaningful upfront capital (tens of thousands of dollars) and logistics capability (storage, transport) that most beginners won't have -- the guest's own suggested entry point for others is the smaller-scale reselling-to-resellers role, not buying truckloads yourself without that capital.
- Facebook comments and public skepticism ("this will never sell") are common when posting original resale ideas (e.g., trailer flips) -- the guest recommends only weighting critical feedback that comes with specific evidence (e.g., someone citing a real $7,000 remediation cost issue), and ignoring unsubstantiated negativity.
