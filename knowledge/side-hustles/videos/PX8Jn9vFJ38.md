# Companies Are Panic Paying for This Skill. Here's How to Get In
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PX8Jn9vFJ38 | Published: 2026-01-08

Interview with Brandon Gadoci (gadociconsulting.com), who built an AI consulting/implementation business from zero to an ~$800K annualized run rate in 5 months, working solo plus a developer and one other contractor.

## The business
- Service: "AI operations" consulting for companies of all sizes — from startups to enterprises with 6,000+ employees — helping them adopt AI practically (not just talk about it).
- Background: previous 3 years doing "AI operations" inside a company (a term credited to Rachel Woods of Austin); self-taught coder; broad generalist skill set that AI dramatically amplified ("all my normal skill sets plus AI equal superpowers").
- Client mix at time of interview: split between mid-size companies (~400-500 employees) and large companies (~6,000 employees), plus some smaller agencies (~40-50 people). Not much traction yet with small local/home-service businesses (helps some informally/for free, e.g., a family member's gym).

## The "three levels" of AI solutioning (his core mental framework, used for both selling and scoping)
1. **Level 1**: Show a client an existing feature of a tool they already have (e.g., a ChatGPT capability) that solves their stated problem — no build required, immediate time savings (~30% time savings cited as an example outcome).
2. **Level 2**: Combine existing off-the-shelf tools/integrations (Zapier, Make, n8n, Relevance AI) with AI in the loop to move information between systems — no custom development, but more complex than Level 1.
3. **Level 3**: Build custom orchestration — combining deterministic code (if-this-then-that) with probabilistic AI (LLM outputs) for complex multi-step tasks requiring custom development (e.g., using Claude Code to build bespoke internal applications).
- Key insight: always start scoping at Level 1 and escalate only as needed — often 80% of the value is achievable at Level 2, so it's not worth the added cost/complexity of a Level 3 build.
- Distinguishes "AI" from "automation": AI solutions actually use AI to do things automation alone couldn't (interpret nuance, evaluate context); roughly 70% of what he builds actually uses AI in the delivered solution, while the other 30% is software that AI simply made faster/cheaper to build (bespoke apps for small teams that wouldn't have been cost-effective to build before AI-assisted coding).

## Pricing / numbers
- Early pricing: first engagements were retainer/hourly, roughly $4,000-5,000/month or one-time, done partly to prove commercial viability to himself and build case studies/soundbites.
- First 6 weeks: ~$30,000 total across several small projects.
- Growth to $800,000 annualized run rate within 5 months (roughly $20K/month scaling up to $60-80K/month, acknowledged as "lumpy").
- Fractional/ongoing engagement pricing at larger companies: roughly $20,000-$35,000/month.
- A standalone "AI readiness audit" (survey + AI-generated insights report + chatbot deliverable) for a ~400-person company: $10,000-$15,000, scaling with depth requested.
- Pricing method: researched market rates using ChatGPT/Claude for competitor/agency pricing (noted that most public pricing data comes from the big 4-5 consultancies), then incrementally raised prices project to project until clients started hesitating — a "if they're not saying no yet, go higher" approach, capped by his own sense of whether he can still deliver proportionate value.
- Believes people are far more likely to underprice out of insecurity than overprice.

## Sales / discovery process
- Discovery-first approach: ask open questions ("what's your experience with AI," "where does this happen," "how long does that take," "what does it cost") rather than proposing solutions mid-call — solutioning too early ends discovery prematurely and risks proposing something infeasible, damaging credibility.
- Uses a "SPIN selling"-style implication approach: ask what happens if a problem gets fixed vs. not fixed, without pitching a specific solution yet.
- Runs a "solutions brief" template process across an organization: trains an internal point person (an "AI operator," a role he credits to Rachel Woods) to gather use cases from employees and enter them into a standard template capturing the problem, steps, people involved, and current cost/time — this produces an aggregated pipeline leadership can review, prioritize (by Level 1/2/3), and calculate potential ROI/time savings from — a concrete tool for overcoming the "no budget precedent for a new technology" problem large orgs face.
- Believes ~5-10% of any organization's employees are already self-motivated toward AI adoption — identify and empower these volunteers as the AI operator rather than assigning it.
- Recommends against choosing technical people by default for the AI operator role — the most effective people tend to be curious, high-agency systems thinkers and good communicators, not necessarily engineers.
- Advises using process mapping (Miro/Lucid board style: "this box is X, tell me what happens next") to understand a client's actual workflow before proposing a specific app/product.
- Frames "PII-heavy" industries (fintech, healthcare) as structurally harder to sell/work with due to data-sensitivity compliance overhead — doesn't believe there's one clearly "best" industry otherwise; nearly every company has AI-adoption pressure right now, largely driven by leadership fear/mandate ("we need to do something about AI" pressure from boards/investors/earnings calls).
- Disputes the "bigger companies are harder to close" sales myth — says closing large enterprise deals can take equal or less effort than small ones at his current price points, though internal politics/bureaucracy make bigger companies harder to execute within once signed.
- Ties value delivered to measurable ROI wherever possible (e.g., "we saved 92 hours and $2,455" dashboards) because leadership under pressure to prove AI isn't hype wants defensible numbers; softer wins (general AI literacy training) are harder to quantify but still valued.
- Recommends capturing baseline time/cost metrics during discovery specifically so they can be cited later as proof of ROI ("you told me this task took 92 hours" becomes the anchor for the value delivered).

## How to get a first client (his advice for someone starting from zero)
- Solve a real problem for someone for free first, to build confidence and initial proof, then start charging and raising prices incrementally as demand validates it.
- Once a client sees one meaningful AI win, follow-on paid work reliably materializes — he reports getting follow-on work from every engagement so far, and that a single discovery/audit process alone can surface ~40 use cases at a single company.
- Example small deliverables cited: (1) a 10-day turnaround building a functional training-application proof of concept for a client after one intake meeting and a large document/knowledge dump; (2) automating a manual internal Salesforce-data research-and-CSV-reformatting workflow (previously ~90 minutes per instance done by a 3-person team) into an AI-agent pipeline that completes the same task in 1-3 minutes.
- Note on churn: has not experienced meaningful churn in 5 months — clients keep generating more use cases faster than he can service them, and some large clients have offered him full-time roles (which he's declined so far, citing commitment ethics to existing clients).

## Internal-adoption / persona framework
- Describes encountering ~6 employee "personas" toward AI adoption inside client organizations (e.g., "Enthusiastic Emma," "Traditionalist Tim," "Curious Chris/Clara," "Cautious ___," "Principled Pat") — each has a different adoption curve and needs different handling (e.g., an over-enthusiastic early adopter may try something themselves, fail, and lose momentum without support).
- Frames the sales pitch to skeptical employees as: "AI probably won't take your job soon, but someone who knows how to use AI will put pressure on you" — reframes AI-driven headcount efficiency as "growing the pie" (serving more market share with a smaller team) rather than pure layoffs, and says leadership mindset often evolves from "cut headcount" framing toward "do more with what we have" framing over time.
- Explicitly runs a dedicated "AI concerns" follow-up presentation addressing objections (ethics, environmental impact, artist/IP concerns) rather than avoiding them.

## Skills / tooling stack
- Primary tool for custom development: Claude Code. Typical stack: Next.js, MongoDB, Tailwind CSS, deployed to Heroku (varies by client).
- Mid-tier (Level 2) tools: Zapier, Relevance AI, n8n (make.com less used); watching OpenAI's newly launched "AgentKit."
- Level 1 / org-wide AI operating system recommendation: ChatGPT (says it has productized "the magic" best of any vendor, with Anthropic/Claude a close second).
- Estimates roughly 50% of his current AI knowledge/skill came from the 3 years prior to starting the business (frameworks, change management, understanding organizational adoption) vs. 50% learned in the most recent 5 months (newer agentic coding tools like Claude Code emerged very recently).

## Caveats / risk factors
- Believes genuine technical understanding still matters even in an AI-accelerated world — cites teaching his teenage son to "vibe code" a website with Claude Code, which worked until the son hit an issue he didn't understand (databases, models, connections) and couldn't debug; argues "you have to know how to fly the plane to put it on autopilot," which is part of his differentiation vs. non-technical AI consultants.
- Explicit warning about hype in the AI-consulting space: he says a large share of viral AI claims ("I fired everyone," "I cloned Shopify with one prompt") are exaggerated, and that credibility/authenticity/honesty are unusually important in this niche because of an ambient "is this bullcrap?" skepticism among buyers.
- Cites the oft-referenced stat that ~90% of generative-AI enterprise pilots reportedly fail (referencing an MIT-adjacent report), though says he hasn't personally experienced that failure rate.
- Warns that agentic AI's marketed promise is currently ahead of what the technology reliably delivers — the most successful real-world implementations combine deterministic code with probabilistic AI rather than relying on pure "agentic" autonomy.
- Enterprise clients sometimes want to hire him full-time specifically to put "a face" on the AI transformation for anxious employees — he's turned these down so far to honor existing commitments, but acknowledges the internal politics of being perceived as "the guy hired to replace us" is a real dynamic to navigate.
