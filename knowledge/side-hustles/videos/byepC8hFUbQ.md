# How to Build a $900k Junk Removal Business with No Money
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byepC8hFUbQ | Published: 2024-08-19

Guest: Kirk McKinney, co-founded Junk Teens (formerly K&J Removal) with his younger brother, starting at ages 17 and 15, self-funded with no parental capital.

Origin story: discovered a local dump/transfer station had an "electronics section" where people dropped off used goods; began "picking" (like the show American Pickers) — found and collected discarded speakers, initially as a hobby (not for profit) while working a grocery job at $11-12/hour. His mother eventually forced him to sell off the growing pile; his first sale was a Bose radio sold on Facebook Marketplace for $50, taking under an hour of effort — more profitable per hour than his day job, which prompted him to sell more broadly (bikes, weights, misc. items) and quit the grocery job.

Path into junk removal: dump employees who ran junk removal as a side hustle noticed how hard he worked (showing up when the dump opened until it closed, helping them for free) and brought him on, paying him $50/hour; he realized if they could pay that and still profit, he could run the same service independently. They mentored him informally and were fine with him branching out.

Key numbers:
- First truck: a used Ford F-150, bought for $4,000, split 50/50 with his brother (partners from the start).
- Year 1 (started Feb 2021, just a pickup truck, doing junk removal plus moving jobs, tree/landscape work, gutter cleaning, etc.): approximately $120,000 in revenue.
- End of year 1 (around Feb 2022): decided to focus exclusively on junk removal rather than diversifying across services, based on what was most enjoyable and most scalable/profitable.
- Winter (slow season for junk removal): used downtime to rebrand from "K&J Removal" to "Junk Teens," rebuild the website, and buy a dump truck.
- Dump truck: cost ~$75,000, paid entirely in cash from savings (ordered new but took 6-8 months to arrive due to COVID-era manufacturing/supply shortages; kept working the pickup truck in the meantime). A dump truck run nets roughly $1,000-2,000 per load vs. $500-700 per load with the pickup truck, due to needing far fewer dump trips.
- Also upgraded pickup truck to a used high-mileage Ford F-250 for about $10,000.
- No CDL required to drive the dump truck (treated like driving a U-Haul-class vehicle).
- Year 2 (2022): $460,000 in sales; profit margin estimated around 50%, though nearly all profit was reinvested into the business rather than taken out, since they were in a high-growth phase; started hiring their first employees this year (mostly friends, similar age).
- Year 3 (2023): revenue roughly doubled from year 2; increased spend on marketing, upgraded team/resources, rented a warehouse, bought another truck.
- Current state (at filming): 3 trucks, a warehouse, 2-4 full-time employees plus about 20 part-time on-call workers; ~40% of business now comes from paid Google ads and paid Facebook ads, remaining 60%+ from word-of-mouth/referrals driven by service quality.
- Costs cited: LLC formation, insurance on the truck, dump/tipping fees, gas, tools/truck maintenance — no marketing spend in the early period (grew via free/organic channels only).

Marketing/growth tactics that worked, in the order of impact reported:
1. Customer referrals — actively asking every customer after a job to refer others or leave a review; described as more effective long-term than any single channel.
2. Nextdoor app — especially effective for a brand-new business; community members are often more willing to support a new local business on Nextdoor than to respond to posts in general Facebook groups.
3. Facebook groups/local presence — reaching "Facebook moms" and local target customers directly.
4. Traditional low-cost tactics: business cards, door-knocking, yard signs.
5. Networking directly with real estate agents for referral flow.
6. Later stage: paid Google ads and paid Facebook ads (~40% of current business), plus building a YouTube/social media following that established authority in the niche and created (in the founder's words) "an endless labor supply" of people wanting to work for them.

Operational notes:
- No CRM used for years — scheduling was done in Apple Notes; a CRM was only being considered around year 3+. Explicit philosophy: don't invest in tools/automation/branding polish before you've proven you can make money doing the actual work — called this over-investment in setup "an advanced form of procrastination."
- Confidence-based sales approach: when asked "have you done this before," the answer was always framed as confident capability ("we're confident we can get this done") even for genuinely new job types, then delivering by fixing any mistakes and owning them — resulted in no dissatisfied customers reported.
- Managed the business fully around a school schedule: dumping the truck before/after school, working weekdays after school (~7 hours/day) plus full weekend days; described this as sacrificing sports/social time deliberately.
- Hiring: prioritized finding "A players" over cheaper/easier "B or C players" once they understood the difference (took a few months to learn this). Hired friends/family deliberately, rejecting common advice against it, citing built-in trust as valuable; kept a firm boundary of not discussing business during actual family time. Had one termination event: an employee who was privately poaching customers to do side jobs and pocket the revenue.
- Growth plan discussed: considering expanding to 2-3 locations and eventually franchising; near-term focus was on systemizing the existing location so it could run without the founders' day-to-day involvement, plus building an in-house recycling facility to reduce dump fees and increase the portion of collected material that's donated/recycled rather than landfilled.

Caveats/lessons the guest emphasized:
- It's "really hard" to get a from-scratch junk removal-only client base — company took a slow, gradual approach, took on adjacent jobs (moving, landscaping, tree/brush removal) to pay bills while transitioning toward specializing.
- Revenue is inconsistent week to week even at scale ("$20K one week, $40K another").
- The business became meaningfully harder to run once formalized (hiring, firing, managing non-family employees) — no shortcut for this beyond direct experience; advises founders be skeptical of generic advice (e.g., "never hire friends/family") that isn't grounded in your specific situation.
- Believes a jack-of-all-trades, do-everything approach only works short-term as an exploration phase; sustained growth required picking one service line and specializing.
