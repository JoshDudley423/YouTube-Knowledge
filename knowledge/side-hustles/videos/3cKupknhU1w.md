# You Don't Need an Online Business to Get Rich
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3cKupknhU1w | Published: 2025-07-12

Chris Koerner + Nik (Nikonomics) riff on a series of offline/local business ideas. No single business is deep-dived; this is a grab-bag of concepts with some real numbers pulled from research.

## Hot tub servicing for short-term rentals
- Real example found in rural Idaho: family business services 25 hot tubs/day, charges $50-$100/week per tub, visits take ~5 minutes each.
- Same company also does hot tub *moving* for retail hot tub stores that don't want to own delivery/logistics equipment — quoted cost to move a hot tub: ~$600.
- Related ideas floated: hot tub maintenance/servicing for Airbnb owners, hot tub rentals to Airbnb owners, hot tub moving as a logistics arm for retailers.

## Concierge prep service for lake/vacation homeowners
- Idea: text-ahead service that gases up boats/jet skis, services and maintains them, cleans the yard, stocks the fridge before the owner arrives at a lake house or cabin.
- Suggested pricing: flat fee (e.g. "$1,000 plus cost") per visit.
- Target customer: anyone wealthy enough to own a lake house/cabin.
- Go-to-market suggestion: flyer the target neighborhood directly (slow but low-cost customer acquisition).

## "Touchless rental" SaaS for boat/watercraft/equipment rental companies
- Idea: build (vibe-code) a SaaS tool that streamlines the rental checkout process — waiver signing, safety instruction (video + checkbox), so renters can show up and go instead of spending ~1 hour on paperwork/training.
- Model: rental companies (boats, ATVs, jet skis, paddleboards, kayaks, sidebysides) pay monthly for the tool.
- Framework given for finding these ideas generally: "find an established industry (e.g., car rental), see which new convenience features are emerging there (e.g., no-check-in car rental like Enterprise/Hertz), then port that feature to an adjacent sub-industry as a SaaS/service."
- Also flagged demand for hardware innovation: a paddleboard with an integrated electric pump (none exists yet per hosts) — manual pumps take ~30 minutes to inflate a paddleboard.

## Buying/owning local rental monopolies in vacation destinations
- Example: Maxin, a hotel/rental outfit on the Henry's Fork River near Island Park, Idaho (pop. 236, near Yellowstone), rents float gear at extreme markups vs. retail: inner tube $95 (vs. ~$15 retail / $5/day elsewhere), stand-up paddleboard $105, kayak $130, canoe $170 — all for a 3-hour rental only (not full-day). Contrasted with BYU-Idaho's equipment rental center charging ~$2/week for the same gear.
- Example: Bear Lake Rentals (bearlakerentals.com) — a legacy boat rental business with its own marina at a major Utah/Idaho vacation lake; framed as a near-unkillable local monopoly (decades of history, prime location, owns the domain).
- Thesis: businesses with a physical location moat in a captive vacation market are extremely durable and high-margin ("not AI, not a competitor... going to disrupt" them) — cited a sign-creation/printing business (not just billboards, custom neon etc.) under LOI as another example of a durable, boring, high-margin local business.
- Idea: "real-world hackathon" mastermind retreat — take entrepreneurs to a vacation destination, split into teams, give them a week to identify a local pricing/service gap (e.g., undercut Maxin's inner-tube rental price) and launch and profit from an actual in-person business as a competition.

## Shopify order-confirmation cold-email piggyback (reader-submitted idea, credited to "Connor Brereslin")
- Mechanism: Shopify order confirmation emails land in the primary inbox (not spam/promotions) because they're sent from Shopify's trusted infrastructure, not the merchant's own domain.
- Method: set up a Shopify store, use a CSV import plugin to manually create fake "orders" for your cold-outreach target list (put target emails in the email field, mark orders paid, product name = your pitch, e.g. "Outsource your stump grinding"), which triggers Shopify's order-confirmation email to land in each target's main inbox.
- Rationale given: cheaper than cold-email software (~$39/mo Shopify vs. hundreds/month for cold-email tools), solves inbox deliverability (the hardest part of cold email), and stands out because it looks like a transactional email.
- Risk/caveat: unclear how fast Shopify would detect and shut down the account for this misuse; suggested workaround is cycling new 30-day free trial accounts if banned.

## Vertical B2B directories as a lead-gen funnel (not ad-monetized)
- Example: a founder built an ABA (applied behavior analysis / autism clinic) directory, then used it as a source of *cold email leads to the clinics themselves* (not to end patients) — pitching clinics "you're listed on my directory, want to rank higher?" then upselling his separate digital marketing agency's SEO/PPC services once they're engaged. Reportedly 90% of his own clinic's patient leads already come from a similar directory.
- Contrast: directories monetized by banner ads need huge traffic volume and produce low returns; a B2B directory that funnels business-owner leads into a high-ticket recurring service (digital agency) is far more valuable per visitor — "I'd rather have 1,000 hits/month of business owners than 10,000 hits/month of consumers."
- Suggested niches to target with this model: emerging/high-ticket, high-trust, not-yet-saturated verticals — healthcare subverticals, CPAs, lawyers, restaurants, "hometown businesses." Home services called out as already oversaturated by this playbook.
- Rule of thumb comparison: like owning Subway franchises, one directory alone won't make much money (~$20k/yr per underlying unit-economics analogy); building a portfolio of ~10 directories with an 80/20 outcome (2-3 winners) is the real model.
- A wedding-niche directory idea is also proposed: aggregate hyper-niche wedding vendors (novelty acts like a "beer burrow" donkey rental at $700/day, charcuterie carts, photo booths, Italian pizza trucks) into a directory/marketplace (a "Zola/TheKnot for niche wedding services"), scraped and displayed via a tool like Replit, monetized later by charging listed vendors for premium placement/ads once traffic is proven. Suggested launch approach: start in one city (e.g. Dallas), manually list ~50 vendors, prove the funnel, then expand market by market.

## Subscription "story letters" business — case study: flowerletters.com
- Business: sends 2 handwritten-style letters per month telling a serialized WWII-era historical romance story (with stickers/artwork), $10/month subscription, multiple story lines/genres (6 themes).
- Scale (data pulled from a USPS blog case study, ~1 year old at time of recording): 31,000 active subscribers, launched August 2020 with 38 subscribers, 4 years old, ~1.8 million letters mailed since launch, run from a warehouse in Orem, Utah, 24 employees.
- Revenue math discussed: 31,000 subs x $10/mo = ~$310k/month (hosts estimate actual figure may be $400-500k/month by now); back-of-envelope ~$5M/year topline. COGS estimated at <$2/letter all-in (stamp <$0.50 bulk + paper/printing) — ~$60k/month COGS, ~80% gross margin (~$250k/month gross profit). Estimated payroll ~24 employees x $75k/yr = $1.8M/yr, implying net profit plausibly $2M+/year.
- Growth channel: word of mouth + SEO only — no paid ads found (checked Meta ads library). SimilarWeb showed ~60,000 unique visitors/month; at an assumed 3% conversion rate that's ~1,800 new subs/month = ~$18k of new MRR/month.
- Many customers subscribe to multiple story lines simultaneously ($20-50+/month per customer instead of just $10).
- Strategic takeaway: "zig while others zag" — succeeded by leaning into slow, delayed-gratification delivery (a trickle of letters) in a market obsessed with instant gratification; also flagged as effectively subsidized by USPS's below-cost bulk mail rates (true delivery cost > price of a stamp).
- Opportunity flagged: a competitor could replicate the exact model in other genres (chosen from top Amazon book categories for women — postapocalyptic, space opera, interstellar romance, historical fiction) and/or spend on paid ads (e.g. Meta targeting) to accelerate growth in what's currently an unpaid-acquisition business; churn described as very low because subscribers don't want to miss how the ongoing story ends.

## Other business riffs (lighter/less fleshed out)
- **Correspondence chess by mail**: revive old-school postal pen-pal chess (letters describing moves) with modern customer acquisition — buy banner ads on chess.com (cited: ~26M hits/month, ~85% market share for online chess; grew from 35M to 100M monthly users between June 2020 and Dec 2022) or roll up/acquire existing Facebook chess groups to build a subscriber base (~$5/month suggested price), matched by skill level, mailed via pre-stamped kits so customers don't need their own stamps/envelopes.
- **Story-letters for kids / interactive choose-your-own-adventure by mail**: a mailed subscription where a kid receives a story with prompts, can write back to influence the plot, and an AI (fed the returned pages via scan) generates the next installment in a handwritten-style font; upsell = printing the finished personalized story into a bound book at the end.
- Related idea mentioned in passing: "One Life Tale" app (built by the host's brother-in-law) that generates a personalized illustrated story about a specific child/relative from prompted details (used to capture family/assisted-living stories), printable via Barnes & Noble — framed as a thoughtful personalized gift product.
- **Whitelabeled vibe-coding tool for a specific niche** (e.g., "Lovable/Replit but just for building video games"): repackage an existing no-code/AI app builder (Lovable is cited as starting to offer white-labeling) as a niche-specific wrapper product; upsell path = when users hit the "last 10-20%" of a build that vibe-coding can't finish (Stripe integration, backend/Supabase wiring, security), refer them to a paid technical developer to close the gap.

## General caveats/framing raised
- The hosts frame all of this as *unsexy, offline* business ideas as a deliberate contrast to online/tech businesses — argument is that durable local monopolies (rental shops, sign businesses) are harder to disrupt than digital ones.
- On AI: repeated framing that AI will "grow the pie" (expand market size) rather than simply take zero-sum share, and that AI knows public/internet information but not private, undocumented "institutional knowledge" (e.g., a rental shop's actual customer/inventory mix) — which is where human-sourced business ideas retain an edge.
