# Forget AI. Make $10K+ With These Offline Side Hustles
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dTnidR4eLus | Published: 2026-06-24

Interview with Brandon Doyle covering five offline/physical side hustles requiring no tech skills.

## 1. Temporary construction fence rental (the standout idea; sold for $600K)
- Business: buy temporary fence panels (plastic barrier posts + panels, spaced ~12 ft apart, not chain-link) and rent them to active construction sites that need fencing (often a zoning/legal requirement -- sites can't legally proceed without it).
- Customer acquisition: no marketing tech needed -- literally drive around, spot new construction sites lacking fencing, find the site supervisor in the job trailer, and pitch renting fence on the spot. (Suggested improvement not actually used by the friend: building permits are public record and searchable, so a fence rental operator could find upcoming sites weeks/months before they're visibly under construction.)
- Pricing: charged by linear foot and duration -- roughly $4/linear foot at shorter durations, discounting to ~$2/linear foot for longer rental terms (construction projects reliably run longer than planned, which favors the renter). Example: a 1,000-linear-foot property = ~$4,000/month.
- Economics: average job was $20,000-30,000 in total contract value; commercial builds can run 2-4 years, meaning a single sale generates a long revenue stream from one setup.
- Labor: at most 2 days of active work at the start (setup) and 2 days at the end (teardown) per job; zero maintenance in between -- if the fence gets hit by equipment, the site crew resets it themselves as their own responsibility, not the renter's.
- Suggested payment structuring for cash flow: get 50-75% of the contract paid upfront (or offer a discount for full upfront payment) rather than only collecting monthly.
- Can theoretically be started with no capital: sell the first job before buying any fencing, then use the deposit/payment to purchase the actual panels.
- Outcome for the friend who ran it (Sacramento suburb, no prior industry experience): grew it to "several hundred grand" earned while operating, then sold the company for $600,000 after 18 months. He later regretted selling -- reason given: the business became "too passive" (all panels were rented out and there was nothing left to actively do), and boredom (not financial pressure) drove the sale.

## 2. Portable mini golf hole rental / build-to-sell
- Product: individually built "mini mini golf" holes -- small portable units (~6x3 ft or 8x3 ft wood frames with AstroTurf and obstacles on a plywood base). One seller (found on Facebook Marketplace) sells these units for hundreds of dollars up to ~$1,000 each.
- Rental model: rents them out for weddings, parties, and events at $300 per hole per day -- pricing set below the cost of materials on the assumption that repeat rental income will cover payback quickly.
- Customer acquisition: entirely free via Facebook Marketplace listings.
- Revenue math to hit $10k/month: need to rent out roughly 35 hole-days at $300 each (e.g., two 18-hole sets rented once, or fewer holes rented more often); after ~3 months, materials are paid off and remaining rental income is close to pure profit (minus gas/time).
- Related variant spotted at an Arkansas RV resort: portable cornhole-based "mini golf" course using cornhole boards (as cheap as ~$30 each on Amazon) with obstacles instead of turf/golf mechanics -- cheaper to build than true mini golf since no ball-rolling surface is needed (works on grass, gravel, or dirt). Suggested business models: sell/install permanent course setups to RV parks/venues (e.g., $700/hole, installed and maintained by the seller), sell the boards outright, or rent them out to events like the mini-golf model.

## 3. Outdoor inflatable movie screen rental
- Equipment needed: an inflatable movie screen (like a bounce house, plugs into a wall outlet; sourced from Alibaba, eBay, etc., cost $500-3,000 for full setup), a projector ($50 budget option not recommended; $200-300 "smart" projectors with built-in streaming apps preferred), and a couple of Bluetooth speakers (~$100 each, nice stand-up units).
- Customer base: backyard parties, birthday parties, neighborhood/HOA events.
- Proof of scalability: named companies that scaled this concept significantly -- FunFlicks (California-based franchise, 40+ locations), Southern Outdoor Cinema (Atlanta, $3-4M/year revenue, 17 years in business), and an Austin-based operator with 20+ satellite locations.
- Economics: similar payback pattern to the mini-golf and fence businesses -- equipment typically pays for itself within 1-6 rentals, after which income is largely profit.
- Adjacent comparison: bounce house rental is a mature but still very lucrative version of this model; the hosts note that even poorly-run, poorly-maintained bounce house companies (observed long return lines, uncleaned units) still make significant money -- cleanliness between rentals was flagged (from a prior deep-dive video on the bounce house business) as an easy way to be in the "top 10%" of operators in that space.
- Framing/thesis for durability: as AI/screen-based digital life increases, real-world outdoor/in-person experience businesses are expected to hold or increase in demand, not decline.

## 4. Flipping original artwork (and other unique goods) sourced abroad
- Concept: buy original, inexpensive local artwork while traveling abroad (not mass-produced/"Hobby Lobby" art, not sold online, found in small independent shops/antique stores) and resell it at a markup back home.
- Personal example given: typically spends $40-60 per piece while traveling, occasionally up to $150 for a framed piece loved enough to splurge on; always sized to fit in a suitcase.
- Case study: a relative bought ~12-14 original paintings in the UK/Ireland at an estimated ~$50 each, resold them at "a few hundred dollars" each (some higher) via a dedicated personal Instagram account (very small following, ~100 or fewer) shared from her main account -- expected to net a few thousand dollars, enough to cover a meaningful part of the trip cost.
- Scaling idea proposed (untested) to avoid needing to travel there in person: cold-call or WhatsApp/FaceTime small local shops (found via Google even without a website) in a target country/region, ask them to photograph their inventory of original work, negotiate a bulk price sight-unseen based on photos (e.g., "I'll take 10 pieces for $350, plus $50 shipping"), then resell domestically via Facebook Marketplace or Instagram. Suggested workflow: get shop owners to send photos, list items for sale locally while collecting deposits before you've actually purchased/received the goods, then buy and ship only what's already presold -- reduces unsold inventory risk (the described downside -- selling something already claimed by someone else -- is expected to be rare since shop inventory usually sits for months).
- Generalizes beyond art: named example of vintage watches (particularly from Japan, South Korea) or unique regional goods (e.g., Hungarian paprika) as other candidate items for this same cross-border arbitrage model -- thesis is that with 300+ countries and thousands of item categories, there will always be underexploited arbitrage opportunities, and it doesn't have to route through the US (e.g., Poland to Colombia, Argentina to Hungary).
- Distribution note: posting content about the sourcing process on social media (even to a small audience) lets algorithmic targeting find the specific niche audience interested in that product category, even with low view counts.

## 5. Liquidation rug flipping (real teen case study)
- Sourcing: bought a pallet lot of 115 rugs (mix of full rugs, runners, and doormats, mostly Costco returns) via B-Stock liquidation auctions for ~$1,500 total including shipping (~$12-13/rug landed cost).
- Validation done: checked existing Facebook Marketplace listings in the area beforehand and saw rugs selling in a wide range, roughly $20-200 -- used this range to judge the auction lot as a safe bet even at a conservative resale estimate ($20 average would still avoid a loss).
- Sales channels used: Facebook Marketplace listings (through a parent's existing account, since the teen seller didn't have his own) plus one in-person garage sale (advertised as a "massive rug sale" with signs posted around town) -- the garage sale was reported as the single most effective channel, moving roughly half of all units sold and being the easiest to upsell in person (e.g., pushing an unwanted $5 doormat onto a $50 rug buyer).
- Presentation mattered: moved rugs indoors or onto a clean background for photos rather than photographing them on grass/dirt, to improve perceived quality.
- Results after "a handful of months" (through winter, described as the worst season to sell rugs): sold about 80 of 115 rugs, averaging ~$40-45 each, netting just over $3,000 in profit -- enough to buy a $3,000 first car (a 1995ish Toyota 4Runner). Roughly 30 rugs remained unsold.
- Financing structure used by the parent: fronted the $1,500 purchase cost and charged the teen $300 in interest (an effective ~20% rate, acknowledged as high) but also gave free storage space (a barn) and free use of the Facebook account/marketing support -- framed as informal seller financing/mentorship rather than a pure loan.
- General takeaway offered: entry cost as low as $1,500 (or "a credit card") and zero industry expertise required; conclusion offered is that $10k/year is achievable at even modest, part-time effort with this model, though $10k/week specifically was called out as unrealistic for rugs without a much more dialed-in operation than this one.

## General caveats raised across the episode
- All five ideas share a common "buy the equipment/inventory, sell/rent it repeatedly, break even within a handful of transactions" pattern -- but "passive" income still requires real upfront active work (sourcing, selling, setup) before payoff.
- Weather dependency flagged for outdoor rental businesses (movie screens, bounce houses).
- Franchise/scaled comparables (FunFlicks, Southern Outdoor Cinema) show a proof-of-concept ceiling well above the hosts' own "$10k" framing, i.e., these ideas can scale meaningfully beyond a side-hustle if operated seriously.
- For international goods-flipping ideas, no in-person visit is required to scale -- remote sourcing via video calls with local shop owners is proposed as equally or more capital-efficient than personally traveling.
