# I Found an Untapped Home Service Business with 75% Profit Margins
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7k2QkmRYK3s | Published: 2024-10-07

Interview with Joe Weinberger and Dean (J&D Lake Services), two college sophomores (Nebraska, Missouri) running a lake shoreline / pond weed-and-seaweed removal business as a summer side hustle, using manual raking (no chemicals) instead of the industry-standard chemical spraying approach.

## The business / core service
- Removes lake grass/seaweed from private lakefront shoreline areas and aquatic weeds/cattails/algae from residential and golf-course ponds — entirely by manual raking (started literally with a Home Depot/Menards garden rake), no chemicals.
- Started organically: began doing dock installation/removal for lake homeowners in high school (~$15-20/hour), then one client asked them to hand-rake seaweed from her dock area; neighbors immediately asked for the same service, revealing unmet demand around an entire 74-home lake.
- Differentiation vs. incumbent "pond maintenance" companies: those competitors mostly do one-time chemical spray treatments (~$1,000/pond) that kill vegetation (and sometimes fish — some competitors are shown on their own websites spraying chemicals in hazmat suits near swim areas) but leave dead weeds/debris in the water and take weeks to show results, with no consistent follow-up maintenance. J&D's method shows immediate, visible results during the same visit (the "painkiller vs. vitamin" framing — visible immediate fix outsells a slow/uncertain treatment) and avoids the ecological/chemical drawbacks entirely.

## Numbers
- Season (roughly mid-May to mid-August, ~3 months of activity): ~45-50 clients, ~$40,000 revenue, ~$30,000 profit → ~75% net margin.
- Lakefront ("shorefront") jobs: flat fee, typically $250-1,200 range, most fall around $250-300 for standard-size shore areas; ~3 hours per job with 1-2 crew guys at $20/hour — labor is nearly their only expense since equipment was already owned.
- Pond jobs: full-day jobs, ranging $1,200-4,500 in the season covered; e.g., a $2,000 pond job (~10-11 hours) nets roughly $1,400-1,500 profit after paying crew ($400-500) and incidentals (water/snacks) — consistent with the ~75% margin.
- New equipment cost: adding a specialized aquatic "weed removal boat" (a "WeeDoo"-style boat) introduces a new minimum ~$700/day operating cost, which will compress margins going forward (exact new margin still being determined at time of interview) but should let them take on bigger ponds and fewer crew.
- Founders target/charge themselves at roughly $100/hour of their own time, and pay crew $20/hour; a mentor advised pricing based on "what do you want to be making," then building the price around that target rather than cost-plus.
- Pricing evolved from a flat $250/job (regardless of shoreline size — worked because most lots on their original lake were similar) to project-based custom quoting once they expanded to ponds and encountered wide variance (floating weeds vs. bottom-rooted weeds vs. cattails-in-muck vs. cattails-not-in-muck; multi-lot properties).
- No formal quoting formula/spreadsheet (they explicitly rejected building one) — pure experience-based "gut feel" estimation, with a deliberate buffer built in (if a job looks like $1,500, they'll quote $2,000) because they've found clients rarely say no to a quote; close rate believed to be very high but not tracked (the host suggested they should start logging quote-vs-outcome to find the real price ceiling).

## Customer acquisition
- Door-to-door canvassing: use Google Earth to scout homes with visible ponds, then knock and offer a free quote. Roughly 9 out of 10 ponds they cold-knock on actually need work; even pristine-looking ponds get a compliment + business card, since owners who maintain a pristine pond are more likely to be motivated, paying customers than owners of a neglected pond who simply don't care (a "false negative" trap to avoid).
- Framing insight: door-to-door works far better for this niche than commoditized services (e.g., window cleaning, pest control) because it's a novel offering people haven't been pitched before — homeowners react with curiosity/gratitude rather than the usual door-to-door annoyance, even at homes with "no soliciting" signs. General framework cited from host: "sell something new in an old way" (an old sales channel + a genuinely novel pitch beats a familiar pitch through a novel channel).
- Social media: Instagram (~15,000 followers) is their primary channel, also TikTok (~2,000), Facebook, and YouTube Shorts; post the same short-form (<60 sec) videos to all four platforms. A slow-building video showing "rake lines" left in a swim area after a routine weekly cleanup eventually hit ~900k-1M views; the framing (comparing their rake pattern to a landscaper's lawn-mowing stripes) was suggested to them by a co-founder, not the host. Polarizing/emotionally charged content performs well (e.g., footage of pulling out lily pads draws hate comments from people who love lily pads — the video's reach and engagement benefited from the controversy). Got a referral job in Northern Wisconsin via an Instagram Story while on vacation, using a borrowed rake and garbage bins at the vacation house — earned ~$500 for a 2-hour job doing this ("paid for the vacation").
- Also got a referral from a golf course (via one founder's caddying connection there), giving credibility/case-study material to pitch other pond owners.
- Host's suggested improvements (not yet implemented): (1) get before/after photos with every job and pay/incentivize customers with a gift card (e.g., $100) to post them to their own social feeds/local Facebook groups — third-party endorsement posts ("J&D came and did a great job") outperform brand's own posts; (2) get drone footage/time-lapses of jobs (they had just bought a drone near season's end); (3) systematic, low-key entry into local Facebook groups (host's own tree-trimming business works with an agency specializing in this because Facebook groups are "overlooked").

## Equipment / capital story
- Started with basic garden rakes from Home Depot/Menards — nearly zero starting capital.
- Footwear evolved through trial and error: waders (too hot in summer) → bare feet/Crocs (fall off) → neoprene "dive boots" (~$30 on Amazon) as the final solution — snug fit, keeps debris out, not overheating.
- Got a specialized aquatic weed-removal boat via an unusual deal: a customer (an equipment-rental-company owner) who needed a job "too big" for their manual-rake approach offered to personally buy the ~boat and rent/lend it to them so they wouldn't take on debt, with an option to buy it later. They pressured the boat manufacturer (normally a 4-week lead time, only 6 employees at the company) into a 2-week turnaround because the customer paid cash and they were leaving for college in 3 weeks. A competing boat manufacturer also approached them via TikTok trying to sell a similar product — showing there are multiple vendors for this niche equipment.

## Caveats
- Even in a market with no direct pond/lake-cleaning competitors, the "real" competition is customer awareness and willingness to spend, not other companies — akin to the host's own experience running a solo iPhone-repair shop in college, where the actual bottleneck was people not knowing the service existed.
- Margins will compress once new equipment (the boat, ~$700/day operating cost) is factored in; the model was built on essentially free/owned tools and cheap manual labor, which won't scale the same way for bigger, more remote jobs.
- Extremely wide job variance (weed type, muck depth, cattails, lot count, floating vs. rooted vegetation) makes clean per-square-foot pricing (like lawn care) impractical; they rely entirely on judgment/experience, which is a risk for anyone trying to replicate the model without that accumulated pattern-matching.
- Willing to travel to unusually far/high-value jobs when it's clearly worth it (e.g., drove ~1 hour to a large pond, which is how they secured the boat deal), but generally geography (windshield time) constrains route economics like other home-service businesses.

## Tools/platforms named
Google Earth (used both to measure pond areas and to scout prospects for door-to-door canvassing), Instagram/TikTok/Facebook/YouTube Shorts (all four, same <60-sec videos cross-posted), Amazon (for neoprene dive boots), WeeDoo-style aquatic weed-removal boat (specialized equipment vendor), Home Depot/Menards (basic rakes/garbage bins).
