# The Most Profitable Side Hustle to Start With No Money
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_1DziIwKyGk | Published: 2026-06-14

Guests: Carson and Jackson (brothers, started at 19/21, now 4+ years in). Business: land wholesaling to home builders ("Carson and Jackson," carsonandjackson.com).

**Core business model**
- They find vacant residential lots and sell (assign) the right to buy them to home builders, without ever purchasing the land themselves in ~90-95% of deals. Standard real-estate wholesaling mechanic applied specifically to raw land: get a seller under an *assignable* contract, then assign that contract to a builder-buyer for a higher price via a title company; the seller gets their price, the builder gets the lot, you (the assignor) collect the price difference as a wire transfer at closing — no capital required, no earnest money actually due (structured with a feasibility-study clause allowing withdrawal any time before closing).
- Two acquisition approaches: (1) original method — find a deal first, then shop it to builders; (2) current preferred method — "builder first": contact home builders to get their **"buy box"** (what they'll pay, in what zip codes, for what lot size/type) up front, then go find off-market sellers willing to sell below that price, guaranteeing a sale before ever making a seller offer.
- Target sellers: owners of off-market, un-listed vacant lots, especially longtime/out-of-state owners who bought cheap decades ago (e.g., paid ~$800/lot 10-20 years prior) and have no near-term plans to build — often motivated by rising property taxes on land they no longer want.
- Target buyers: primarily **spec home builders** (build 10-100+ homes/year to resell, as opposed to custom builders building on a client's own lot) — identifiable because they list finished spec homes for sale on Zillow. Named example: D.R. Horton (largest builder in the US) is one of their biggest buyers, sourced through relationships with the company's regional land-acquisition managers; deals also done with small builders doing as few as 1-3 builds/year.

**Free/low-cost way to start (explicit "$0 startup" method given)**
1. Go to Zillow, filter for newly-built (2025/2026) houses listed *for sale* in your target area — this identifies spec builders (someone building their own home to live in wouldn't list it for sale). Their name/phone number is often shown right on the Zillow listing.
2. Call the builder, ask if they want more land, and get their buy box (price, size, area) — this step is entirely free.
3. Use Zillow to find vacant, unbuilt lots in the builder's target area; identify the owner via **TruePeopleSearch.com** (free, works like white pages) to get contact info.
4. Cold call the owner, offer roughly $5,000-10,000 less than what the builder will pay.
5. Generate a free assignable land contract (they suggest even using ChatGPT to draft one, "legal in all 50 states," reviewed by a lawyer/local attorney) and a matching buyer contract; send both to a title company with all party contact info.
6. Wait 2-3 weeks for closing; collect the price-difference wire.
- Two people the hosts recently walked through this for free: one made $5,200 in the first 14 days; another closed three deals for $9,500 in total assignment profit with zero marketing spend and no LLC set up yet.
- Framing: "if we lost everything today, we could go to a public library for free, use Zillow, and make $20k/month wholesaling land" — used to stress the near-zero capital requirement of the core skill itself once learned.

**Deal economics**
- Average deal profit: **~$8,600 per deal**, at **90-95% margin** (essentially pure profit since no capital is deployed in most deals).
- Typical lot values handled: mostly mid-five-figures ($30k-100k range), with profits typically $10k-15k on the larger end, but they've done lots as cheap as $4,000 and as large as $3 million.
- Biggest single-month result: **37 deals, $532,000 in profit, in one month**.
- Best year: **2.1 million in total assignment revenue, $1.7 million in profit**, with 239 land deals closed that year (almost one deal every business day).
- Ongoing baseline: **$100,000+/month in profit for 40 consecutive months** (though the business is "lumpy" — big deals can skew any given month, so they track performance quarterly rather than monthly; deals slipping between months can make one month look artificially high/low).
- Rule of thumb given: closing just **one deal a month at their average profit level equates to $100,000+/year**; each deal reportedly takes only 30-60 minutes of actual hands-on work once you know the process.
- First big deal (origin story, 2021ish, ages 19-21): a seller called them directly offering four lots (Jacksonville, FL) originally bought decades ago for ~$800 each, asking $40,000 total; they put all four under contract for $37,000, liquidated personal stock investments to fund the purchase (one of the few times they bought outright rather than assigned), and resold for ~$180-190k, netting roughly $140,000 profit — described as their "breakthrough" deal.
- One anecdote: a beginner found a market-listed lot for $1,000,000, had a builder lined up at $1.11M, and made a $110,000 profit 30 days later on his very first deal, entirely off an on-market listing (no off-market sourcing needed).

**Negotiation tactics**
- Never straight-up lowball on land value; come in with a genuinely strong, fair cash offer (avoids the "wholesaler bad rep" that comes from lying about intentions or fabricating problems like fake wildlife issues on a property).
- Be transparent with sellers about being a wholesaler/middleman working with 20-30 builders and taking an 8-10% margin — sellers respond better to honesty and it builds trust/referrals.
- Frame the offer against realistic net proceeds from listing traditionally: subtract typical realtor commission (8-10% on land, since land takes longer to sell and good agents avoid low-commission land listings) and closing costs (~$1,500-2,000) from a comparable market listing price, then show the seller their cash-offer net is equal or close to what they'd net after months of waiting via a realtor.
- Never counter down on price by exactly matching a seller's counteroffer immediately (e.g., seller says "can you do $17k?" on a $16.5k offer) — pause the call ("let me check with my partner"), then come back and accept; the artificial delay makes sellers feel they got a genuinely negotiated win, increasing actual close-through rates versus instant acceptance (they report immediate "yes" agreement sometimes doesn't stick — people back out more if it felt too easy).
- Same logic applied to counter-offers over text: never simply accept a seller's counter; counter back slightly lower than their ask (not out of greed, but because instant acceptance makes sellers second-guess and shop around).
- Calls are extremely short: typically 90-120 seconds; framed as "you're not selling them anything, you're offering to give them money," which removes the psychological friction of a typical sales call. Fastest deal cited: contract signed within 5 minutes of first-ever contact with a seller.
- "Save my number" tactic: on any call where price isn't agreed, tell the seller to save your name/number (e.g., "Jackson Land Guy") for whenever they're ready to sell — when they call back months later (often because their circumstances changed), they'll often accept whatever price is offered rather than re-negotiate, because they're now motivated. Free, low-effort, high eventual yield.
- With builders: build trust by consistently bringing good deals; builders reportedly proactively ask for more lots once relationship is established, and are receptive to margin adjustments (e.g., raising your ask from $40k to $42.5k once an area's deal flow is "rinsed out") because they value volume/reliability over squeezing every dollar. Land investors/land-bankers, by contrast, are described as the tougher counterparties — habitual lowballers.
- Referral incentive: pay $1,000 cash (via a handwritten thank-you note from an executive assistant after a closed deal) to any past seller who refers a friend/family member whose land deal also closes — a meaningful, not stingy, incentive relative to the ~$8,600 average deal profit, since the $1,000 only comes out of the built-in margin (not upfront cost); generated ~28-29 referral deals in one year.
- Always contact the next-door neighbor of a lot before selling to a builder, as a courtesy heads-up about incoming construction — neighbors frequently want to buy the lot themselves (to expand their yard, avoid construction noise, etc.) and will often pay above the builder's price/at retail, since it has non-market value to them specifically.

**Deal-killers / risk factors to screen for (land-specific due diligence)**
- **Wetlands**: visible on Zillow satellite/aerial images — trees appear gray (dead, standing water) vs. green (healthy/dry); can devalue a lot dramatically or make it unsellable (example: one lot's wetlands report came back 40% clean and sold for $275k vs. an estimated $600k if 100% clean; an adjacent 100%-wetlands lot could not be sold at all).
- **Slope**: setback requirements vary by locale; can swing lot value by tens of thousands of dollars (example given: $5,000 to $25,000 range in one Arkansas market based on slope severity).
- **Utilities/water-sewer access**: presence of connections (checkable via county appraiser websites) adds meaningful value; lack of them requires well/septic, reducing value.
- **Protected wildlife**: the single most common deal-killer in Florida — gopher tortoises cost roughly $6,000-8,000 per turtle to legally relocate/remove; other regions have their own protected species (example: "scrub jays," a protected bird) — always check locally, since the specific species/regulations vary by state.
- **Seawalls** (waterfront lots): add roughly $20,000-30,000 in value.
- **Main roads/highway frontage**: builders avoid lots directly on busy roads.
- **Perc tests** (soil percolation, for septic feasibility) are a major buildability factor, state-dependent.
- Because they always talk to the builder first, builders often flag these region-specific red flags proactively before an offer is even made, reducing the wholesaler's own research burden.
- Framed positively: these deal-killers affect a small minority of properties (~5% or less), and their contract structure (feasibility clause, no earnest money due) means they can walk away from a bad deal at any point before closing with zero financial loss — they report having never lost an earnest money deposit.

**Market/geography notes**
- Best target markets: fast-growing suburbs roughly 30-45 minutes outside a metro core (not the already-built-out urban core itself) with 5+ active spec home builders and lots that are relatively uniform ("cookie-cutter") in size/type — uniformity enables sending identical mass offers efficiently. States with low/no state income tax (Florida, Arizona, etc.) tend to be hotter markets because rising home values there drag lot values up with them. Named active markets: Florida broadly (Cape Coral, Lehigh Acres, Bonita Springs), Bentonville/NW Arkansas, Wilmington NC, parts of Tennessee/South/North Carolina, Nevada, Georgia, Washington, Hawaii.
- Rural/slow-moving markets are explicitly avoided — land is cheaper there but takes far longer to sell, breaking their preferred 2-3 week (sometimes as fast as 72 hours) close cycle.
- Market direction doesn't matter much — a rising market means builders compete and pay more; a falling market means motivated sellers panic-sell — the only genuinely hard condition is a *stagnant* market where buyer and seller price expectations haven't yet converged.
- Texas specifically avoided by this team due to strict state mass-texting/TCPA rules and because they'd already built enough volume elsewhere — noted as a market with real deal flow they simply hadn't focused on, not a bad market per se. Texas is also a non-disclosure state (Zillow sold prices are hidden by default, though a workaround via scrolling to funding/price history info was mentioned) which deters some competitors, implying reduced competition there for those willing to dig.
- Pricing rule when no builder is lined up yet: base your offer on current comparable *listing* prices in the area (what a builder could buy the lot next door for right now), not on past sold comps — always price your offer below the cheapest current alternative a builder could otherwise buy.

**Scaling / marketing at volume (their business at scale, not required to start)**
- At peak volume: sent ~50,000 texts/month (~$1,200/month cost, reported 37x lifetime ROI on SMS spend) and ~40,000 direct mailers/month, plus 9 overseas cold callers making thousands of dials/day. With roughly 90,000 total monthly offers and a ~10-sale/month close rate, they modeled this as capable of ~$1 million/year in profit.
- Direct mail produced slightly higher absolute profit than SMS despite lower ROI, because of far greater send volume/spend capacity; they specifically favor mailing an actual pre-filled *contract* (not just a letter) directly to the seller's address, with their own return address — sellers would simply sign and mail the contract back with no phone conversation ever taking place.
- General response-rate principle stated: response rate scales with the effort/cost put into the outreach method — a physical "lumpy mail" package can cost ~$6/piece but convert around 0.5%, versus a plain postcard converting around 0.0005%. Low-effort tactics (mass ringless voicemail, mass texting) are popular precisely because they're low effort, but that correlates with low results — the "harder," less scalable methods (personally dialing sellers yourself) build far better instincts, higher answer/conversion rates, and avoid VA-driven losses (foreign-accent friction, missed nuance/objection-handling) versus outsourcing cold calls to overseas VAs.
- TCPA/SMS legal compliance: not offered as legal advice, but practices mentioned — excluding Texas from text lists, respecting call-time-zone restrictions, avoiding explicit "sell/solicit" language in texts, using A2P 10DLC verification, and working directly with texting-platform providers for compliance guidance; the hosts eventually stopped SMS entirely themselves to limit personal liability, while still teaching the tactic.
- Team structure at scale (30 deals/month level, described as juggling roughly 120 sellers, 90 buyers, ~50 title companies, 10 states simultaneously each month): 1 transaction coordinator (manages contracts, title work, seller relations, calls each seller roughly every 7 days), 2 acquisition managers (call sellers, negotiate price, generate signed agreements), the two founders (run marketing campaigns, handle large/complex deals), plus up to 9 (averaging ~5) overseas cold-calling VAs.
- VA hiring: sourced from the Philippines via onlinejobs.ph, hired in batches via group Zoom "mass interviews" (~20 minutes, ask for name/experience/personal story) to gauge personality/friendliness rather than accent or intelligence — attitude reportedly drives the difference between 3 vs. 12 leads/day from the same caller. VAs described as loyal (one employee retained 4+ years) when treated respectfully, which the hosts say much of the wholesaling industry fails to do.
- Software/tech stack: **Buyer Bridge** (custom-built CRM for land wholesaling specifically — contracts, title tracking, buyer database with ~12,000 buyers and ~6,000 buy-boxes); **Land Portal** and **PropStream** (pull owner/mailing lists by zip code for lead generation); **Kind Skip Tracing** (phone number lookup for owners); **ReplySmart** / **Smarter Contact** (mass SMS); **REA Port Mail** (direct mail, including legal/compliance guidance); **ReadyMode** and **Batch Dialer** (multi-line cold-call dialers for managing outsourced callers). Explicitly noted these tools are for running hundreds of deals at scale — the entire business can be started with just pen, paper, a whiteboard, and an iPhone.

**Comparisons and structural advantages vs. wholesaling houses**
- Far fewer variables/condition issues than house wholesaling: land deals mainly hinge on wetlands/slope/utilities/wildlife, which are objectively verifiable via aerial photos and county records, vs. houses where structural, cosmetic, and inspection issues create huge appraisal uncertainty and room for scuzzy tactics (e.g., wholesalers falsely claiming "the layout is bad" to justify lowball offers).
- Repeat-buyer advantage: a spec home builder (especially a large one like D.R. Horton) buys dozens to thousands of lots per year and will happily keep buying from you repeatedly in the same area — unlike house-flipping buyers who typically only buy 2-4 houses/year and are cash-constrained, or unlike selling a wholesaled house where buyer and seller might physically meet and cut you out of the deal.
- The hosts explicitly reject buying/selling houses themselves (except when one "falls in their lap") because of much higher execution risk (termites, roof, foundation, electrical issues not caught in inspection).
- One adjacent niche they've moved into: **teardown deals** — buying an old, small/undervalued house purely for its land value in a low-supply, high-end neighborhood (paying the seller a premium, e.g., $3 million for a $100k-purchase-decades-ago property) with intent to demolish and build a $10-20 million luxury home; valued purely as land, not as a home.

**Downsides / caveats explicitly raised**
- Not a business you can meaningfully "exit" or sell for a multiple — unlike, say, an insurance agency doing similar profit that might sell for ~3x profit, a land wholesaling operation has essentially no enterprise/resale value if you stop actively running it (despite ~$6 million in cumulative profit over 4 years).
- High effective tax burden: very high margins with relatively few write-offs led to large tax bills as beginners, cited as their biggest early financial downside.
- Warn against sending indiscriminate "mass high offers" to build a pipeline (e.g., offering $30k on a $20k-value lot just to get attention) — this permanently anchors the seller's price expectations too high and poisons future negotiations for every other wholesaler who later approaches that same seller.
- Don't attempt this without first knowing what to actually offer (comparable pricing knowledge) — under-researched offers waste time and damage market trust; know your target market, have a builder buy-box lined up, have proper (ideally attorney-reviewed) contracts, and confirm your state's SMS/direct-mail legal restrictions before scaling outreach.
- Volume/close-rate isn't a fixed formula — it depends heavily on how aggressive/attractive the offer is (near-market offers can convert ~6% of contacts; deep-discount offers might need 1,000+ calls for the same yield); the hosts' rough guarantee: consistently offering roughly $10k below builder-buy-price to enough sellers over time will make money, but persistence over weeks/months (not days) is required — many people quit after only ~10 calls in a week and wrongly conclude the model doesn't work.
