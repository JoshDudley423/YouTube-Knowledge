# AI Is Replacing Content Creators (Here's How to Profit)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8XEXkWAxPAQ | Published: 2025-05-21

Podcast conversation (Chris + returning guest Sam, who runs a marketing/lead-gen agency called Service Scalers / Unlimited Content) covering: passive income from licensing your voice on ElevenLabs, viral AI-generated animal-rescue videos as an "impression farming" business model, a detailed AI-avatar ad-creative production pipeline (ChatGPT + Photoshop + face-swap + ElevenLabs voice cloning + HeyGen), and a data-driven billboard-vs-Facebook-ads comparison for podcast growth.

## Idea 1: Passive income by licensing your voice on ElevenLabs
- ElevenLabs lets users upload/clone their own voice and opt it into the pool of voices other ElevenLabs users can select for text-to-speech generation, paying the voice owner a proportional revenue share.
- Key insight: you do NOT need a "good" or professional-sounding voice — average, non-studio, accented, or unusual voices are in high demand precisely because they sound authentic/non-robotic (a "Sally from North Carolina" voice, or an Indian-American accented voice, are cited as desirable rather than a disadvantage) for ad/marketing use cases where a natural, relatable voice outperforms a polished "Morgan Freeman" narrator voice.
- Framed as "a few hundred bucks a month" passive opportunity: just clone your voice once and opt into the marketplace.
- Personal-brand risk flagged: cloning and publishing your own voice makes it trivially easy for others to rip your existing publicly available content (podcasts, videos) and clone your voice anyway, so guarding against that by not publishing a voice sample is not actually protective — someone can already extract it from existing public audio.

## Idea 2: "AI animal rescue" viral video content farms (analyzed, not necessarily recommended)
- Observed pattern: Facebook accounts (suspected to be overseas "click farm" operations) posting 5-10 short, ultra-realistic AI-generated videos per day depicting dramatic animal-rescue scenarios (e.g., a tiger surviving a chainsaw hit, a dog pulling a baby from floodwater, a helicopter lifting a giant turtle) — consistently involving danger to babies/young animals because that content reliably stops the scroll ("no one's going to swipe past a baby in danger").
- Getting millions of views; hosts speculate the accounts are monetized via Facebook's content monetization program, likely earning six figures, purely from ad-impression revenue (no product/service being sold) — described as "impression farming."
- Technical production note: longer AI videos are made by stitching multiple short generated scenes together sequentially (using a video-generation engine like Kling AI, see below), not one continuous generation.
- Business framing: this is a legitimate (if ethically gray/low-effort) content-monetization business model at sufficient scale, even though monetization is capped at whatever the platform pays per impression.

## Video-generation tools named
- **Kling AI**: video generation engine; version 2.0 is described as most expensive and slowest but by far the best quality; version 1.6 can produce comparable results but requires much more precise prompting.
- **Flora Fauna AI**: preferred by the guest over "out of the box" AI ad-creative tools (e.g., market/aggregator tools built on the same underlying video models) because it gives more direct control and avoids paying a markup for a wrapper around the same base models everyone else uses.

## Idea 3: Building a reliable AI-avatar ad-creative pipeline (for cold outreach / Facebook ads lead gen)
Business problem being solved: needing to rapidly generate many (~15+) video ad variations (different hooks/headlines) without relying on real humans' time/availability, since human-based content creation was the bottleneck for the agency's ad-testing pace, and video-based ad creatives consistently outperform static image ads.

Step-by-step pipeline described:
1. Generate a base face/image asset using ChatGPT (image generation), which also can help write ad scripts.
2. Import that image into Photoshop and use generative "expand"/fill to widen the frame — ChatGPT-generated images tend to crop too tightly for ad layouts that need room for text overlays.
3. Use a separate AI face-swapping tool to replace the generated face with a specific real trusted face (their own, or a team member's, e.g., their head of sales "Ethan") — more reliable than trying to prompt ChatGPT to recreate a specific person's likeness directly (it "never gets the face right").
4. Clone the voice of that same real person via **ElevenLabs' voice cloning** (voice-to-voice, not plain text-to-speech). Key nuance: recording yourself actually speaking the ad script (with intended pacing/emphasis on the hook) produces far more natural inflection than typing/pasting a script into a generic text-to-speech voice — plain TTS is what causes the "weird inflections" and unnatural sentence flow that make AI voice ads sound obviously fake. For a specific ad script with precise pauses, record the actual line yourself rather than relying on a broader podcast/voice sample.
5. Feed the expanded, face-swapped image plus the cloned voice output into **HeyGen's Avatar 4 model** to generate the final talking-head video. Guest rates Avatar 3 at roughly a 60% "hit rate" toward looking human, and states Avatar 4 has produced videos he considers "indistinguishable from human."
6. Add subtitles to the video — found to help mask remaining AI "tells" (viewers' attention shifts from the mouth to the text), effective even on the earlier Avatar 3 model.
- Real-world result: one such 100%-AI-generated ad (a fake host discussing lead generation) was running live and generating booked sales calls at ~$10 per booked call — an unusually low cost per booked call by the guest's own standard; overall show-rate benchmark across all their ads (human and AI) is ~60%.
- Current limitation acknowledged: they're intentionally sticking to a "podcast-style" visual format (person seated at a microphone) because studio-quality AI voice audio combined with a realistic "selfie video" visual mismatch is a detectable tell; a more casual selfie-video format with matching casual audio quality hasn't been solved yet but the guest believes he knows the general approach needed.
- Explicit success criterion stated: not "does it look perfectly real" but "does it convert on ads" — by that measure, the pipeline had already crossed the bar for the stated use case of generating leads without needing a real human to shoot content.
- Same general workflow (asset generation, face-swap, voice-clone) is being adapted for **UGC-style ad creatives for physical/e-commerce products**, described as "getting there" at time of recording.

## Idea 4 / competitive intelligence: content theft as inadvertent validation
- Guest notes 5-6 accounts actively copy his Instagram content, including one in Canada that translates 100% of his videos verbatim into Hindi and republishes them — can't be copyright-struck because it's a different language, so platform algorithms don't flag it as duplicate content (parallel drawn to MrBeast's strategy of proactively dubbing/publishing his own content in other languages via ElevenLabs rather than letting others do it first, since posting first in a given language preserves your ability to claim copyright there).
- Suggested response/opportunity: proactively publish your own content in high-opportunity foreign-language markets (using ElevenLabs voice dubbing/"overdub") before copycats do, capturing that audience directly instead of losing it to imitators.

## Idea 5: Billboard marketing vs. Facebook ads for growing an audience (podcast growth case study)
- Guest ran a real billboard (rural outskirts of Houston, ~20,000 cars/day) for one month to grow podcast downloads in the Houston area, and separately ran a 2-day Facebook ad test (30 creative variations of billboard photography, ~$260/day) targeting non-followers in the same Houston area.
- Baseline: before any promotion, Houston-area listeners were a consistent 1-1.5% of total podcast downloads.
- Facebook ads (2 days, ~$260/day = ~$520 total spend): Houston-area share rose to ~2.5%, roughly a 50-74% increase in that region's download share (74% average across the tested episodes, ~50% excluding one outlier), attributed specifically to the brief ad flight rather than ongoing spend.
- Billboard (~$20/day cost, per a 3-month rural billboard contract): with podcast overall growing 30% and Houston-specific growth reaching 60% (roughly double the overall growth rate) attributed largely to the billboard — described as achieving "almost as many new downloads" as the Facebook ad flight at roughly 1/13th the daily cost ($20/day vs. ~$260/day).
- Billboard placement matters a lot: moving the same billboard ~300 yards down the same road/side of the street (to a spot with a longer, more direct "full frontal" view as cars approach a curve, vs. the original spot's shorter/angled visibility) increased the performance ratio from 65% to 95% to 120% (an indexed download-lift metric) — cost stayed the same because the billboard company's account manager proactively suggested the better spot for free.
- Winning billboard ad copy across ~20 tested phrase variations (same visual): the phrase "get rich" outperformed all others — framed as evidence that a "silver bullet get rich" hook has enduring universal pull.
- Pushback on "Facebook ads don't work for podcast growth": guest argues this is usually a cash-conversion-cycle / CAC-to-LTV problem, not a channel-effectiveness problem — podcast growth has no direct/immediate monetization per subscriber, so most creators can't tolerate the cash outlay period the way a business with fast payback (e.g., booked sales calls) can.
- General marketing math takeaway reiterated: performance ultimately reduces to impressions × unique viewers × frequency/repetition needed before conversion × conversion rate — the same underlying math applies whether the channel is Facebook ads or a physical billboard; billboards just require more manual work (contracts, physical placement, no built-in analytics) to gather the same kind of data Facebook provides natively.
- Noted diminishing-returns risk not yet resolved: a fixed driving population will see a static billboard the same message hundreds of times over a 3-month run; if repeat viewers don't convert in the first ~10-20 exposures they likely won't ever convert, meaning ROI could taper as the "reachable new audience" pool depletes — the guest's 3-month billboard contract term was noted as plausibly aligning with when that saturation point tends to hit.
- Secondary/flywheel benefit noted: publicly discussing marketing experiments (e.g., tweeting about a personal e-commerce side project) drives its own audience/community growth even when the underlying venture (a hummingbird-nectar e-commerce brand, referenced but not detailed in this part) isn't independently highly profitable — the personal-brand exposure itself has monetizable secondary value.

## Caveats
- AI avatar ad pipeline is explicitly described as still evolving — the podcast-style format is a current workaround for a specific unsolved "casual selfie video" audio/visual mismatch tell, not a fully general solution yet.
- "Impression farming" AI animal-rescue content is presented as an observed phenomenon/business model, with explicit acknowledgment that monetization is capped by ad-impression payouts and doesn't build a brand or product business.
- Billboard performance data is a single-market, single-flight case study (Houston, 3-month contract) and the hosts explicitly flag unresolved questions about long-term ROI/saturation and how to measure durable "loyal listener" conversion vs. one-time download spikes.
