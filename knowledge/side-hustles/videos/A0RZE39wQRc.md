# How I Built a $10m Business in 32 Minutes
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A0RZE39wQRc | Published: 2025-01-20

Chris Koerner's own history of "LCD Cycle" (iPhone parts wholesale business, 2013–2019/2020), told for the transferable frameworks, not as a step-by-step launch guide for a new listener.

## The business(es)
- Predecessor business (2010–2013, while a college senior at University of Alabama): "Phone Restore," an iPhone repair retail store, launched after being informally mentored for $60 by another student who repaired his broken iPhone. Learned the business model existed after hearing about "iPhone MD" in Baton Rouge making $20–30K/month from one store.
- Grew Phone Restore to 4 locations over ~2 years with a 50/50 partner (later bought the partner out). Sold Phone Restore, retaining a minority (~1/3) ownership stake.
- LCD Cycle (started 2013): pivoted into buying broken iPhone screens/parts from repair shops (recycling/refurbishing them via a China supply chain) and reselling replacement parts to the same repair shops. Triggered by an unsolicited phone call from someone wanting to buy his store's broken screens for recycling in China.
- Revenue trajectory: $28K (month 1), $56K (month 2), $150K (month 3, March 2013); $2.1M year 1; ~$5M year 2; grew toward ~$10M/year at peak, run across 4 U.S. distribution centers (Huntsville AL, Dallas TX, Orlando FL, Michigan). Business then contracted (~$10M → $8M → $6M) over 2017–2019 amid rising competition, a partner falling-out, and management strain (23 employees across 4 states); eventually sold the customer list "for pennies on the dollar" relative to peak value and wound down — but stayed profitable every month until the very end (no bankruptcy/failure in the traditional sense).

## Concrete process / numbers
- Initial supplier sourcing: cold-messaged "hundreds" of Alibaba suppliers in Shenzhen/Guangzhou with an identical templated pitch (repair-shop owner wanting to become a parts supplier + buy back broken screens); requested free samples (2–4 screens, home buttons, earpieces, batteries — "the big four" parts) to vet quality.
- Took a major risk wiring $17,000 upfront to a Chinese supplier ("David Sue") based on relationship trust with no contract — this became a long-term, high-volume, high-trust supply relationship worth "millions of dollars" over subsequent years.
- Lead generation: scraped every iPhone repair shop in the U.S. by Metropolitan Statistical Area (~242 MSAs, ~20,000 shops total) using a (now-defunct) Google Maps scraping tool, compiling results across many separate spreadsheets merged via a macro.
- Outbound sales: hired 3 virtual assistants via oDesk (now Upwork) at $5/hour net (~$5.55 gross after the platform's 10% fee) — experienced telemarketers — using a Zoho CRM with the 20,000-lead list and a simple script: offer $3/unit for broken iPhone screens, provide a prepaid shipping label, pay via check or PayPal.
- Refurb/QA process: bought $15 Chinese testing rigs to check screens for dead pixels; a perfect screen was worth ~$6–7 wholesale after being purchased for $2–3 (dead-pixel units worth only 25–50 cents but still shippable) — the buy-low/test/resell margin was the core unit economics.
- Logistics: shipped bulk foam boxes of used screens to Hong Kong rather than directly into mainland China to avoid high tariffs.
- Business model shift: repair shops that sold their broken screens for cash were then pitched to buy replacement parts — 93% of revenue was recurring, since repair shops that bought once tended to buy every month.
- Differentiators introduced to the industry: (1) first company in the space to offer a lifetime return policy (rarely used in practice, but a strong marketing/sales "no-brainer offer" — mathematically priced in since few customers ever invoked it); (2) free shipping over $200, engineered around a ~$170 average order value to nudge customers over the free-shipping threshold and raise AOV.
- Geographic strategy: analyzed customer location data (concentrated in Southeast US) and used FedEx/UPS ground-shipping radius maps to find the best relocation hub for 1-day ground delivery reach — moved personally from Huntsville, AL to Dallas, TX because it put Texas, Oklahoma, and Arkansas metro areas within 1-day ground shipping; opened additional distribution centers in Orlando (where a soon-to-be business partner lived) and Michigan (to reach Northeastern cities within 1–2 days ground).
- Financing: used a personal-relationship line of credit (~$250K) co-signed by a mentor/investor (no equity given — mentor asked only for ongoing donations to his nonprofit) to fund growth cash-flow gaps, rather than giving up equity. Later turned down a $1M/25%-equity VC term sheet after a personal decision-making process (framed in the video through his religious practice) — years later, reviewing the VC's near-identical term sheet again for a different company, he identified the reason it would have hurt him: standard liquidation preference clauses would have prevented him from personally profiting from LCD Cycle's eventual wind-down/decline, since investors get their principal back first regardless of exit size.

## Frameworks / lessons stated explicitly
- Retail location: "do not save money on location" — a good location reduces the marketing spend you'd otherwise need; a nearby McDonald's is cited as a positive location signal (McDonald's does extensive site research).
- "Proximity bias": people already embedded in an industry are best positioned to spot adjacent opportunities within it (he recognized the parts-recycling idea instantly because of his repair-shop experience) — but that same proximity can also make people wrongly assume a market is "too saturated" or "too late."
- "Basically nothing is oversaturated and it's basically never too late" — extended analogy: e-commerce (30 years old) is still only ~15% of retail spend; if you think you're too late, treat that as a possible fear-based excuse rather than a fact. Explicit caveat: there are real exceptions (e.g., laser discs) — check for actual saturation data before assuming either way (e.g., his tree-trimming business uses a benchmark of "1 tree-trimming business per 10,000 residents" as a normal saturation ratio to compare a target city against).
- Talk to customers directly and regularly — a round of customer calls revealed the *real* reason people bought (fast free shipping / 1-day delivery radius) rather than the assumed reason (the lifetime return policy).
- Cash flow ≠ profit, especially in high-growth wholesale/e-commerce: a business can be profitable on paper while cash-flow negative because of fast inventory-driven growth.
- "Businesses don't really fail, founders just run out of energy" — his own explanation for LCD Cycle's decline; he believes he could have kept growing if he'd stayed focused, but was distracted testing other business ideas concurrently.
- Sometimes winding a business down slowly and profitably (rather than collapsing/bankrupt) is a normal, non-failure outcome.

## Caveats
- This is a personal narrative/lessons video, not a how-to guide for entering the iPhone-parts market today (that specific market opportunity was time- and relationship-dependent, e.g., a trust-based $17K wire to an overseas supplier).
- The China sourcing and MSA-scraping/VA cold-calling tactics reflect a 2013 tooling landscape; several named tools (the Google Maps scraper, oDesk) are outdated or renamed (oDesk → Upwork).
