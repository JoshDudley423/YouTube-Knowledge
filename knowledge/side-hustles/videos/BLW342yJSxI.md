# Q&A: How to Make Money Cloning Content with AI (+4 more)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BLW342yJSxI | Published: 2025-03-10

Listener Q&A episode covering five separate topics: viral marketing for an escape room, finding an "operating partner," a local newsletter monetization plan, crypto/NFT domain names, and productizing AI-clone chatbots for creators. Each answer is short but concrete.

## 1. Viral marketing stunts for a local business (escape room in Belgium)
- Tactic: "hidden money" style stunts — hide a small amount of cash (e.g., a $50 bill) somewhere in the city, post a video panning the immediate area with an exact time/location caption ("Brussels, 2:37pm"), first person to find it keeps it. Cited comparable Instagram accounts get views in the hundreds of thousands.
- Tactic: send mystery/puzzle boxes (locked or combination-locked) to local micro-influencers for free, without requiring them to post — spend only $200–$300 making 5–10 boxes, then measure how many recipients post organically and how much traffic/signal that generates before deciding whether to scale.
- Tactic: guerrilla flyer + QR code stunt — reference example: a flyer reading "I know you cheated on me, well now the secret is out" with a QR code that actually redirects to a local business's Instagram page (curiosity-bait linking to a business).
- General approach recommended: look at how already-successful escape room competitors (a well-established category) have grown virally and adapt proven tactics rather than inventing from scratch.

## 2. Finding an operating/execution partner ("find your Kirk")
- Framework: an idea-generator personality type (many ideas, loves 0→1 ideation, doesn't love day-to-day operations) needs an "operating partner" ("Kirk," named after Chris's own business partner/cousin) who complements them by executing and running the business long-term.
- Key insight on idea-sharing: nobody will love your idea as much as you do, so sharing your idea publicly is low-risk — the person you tell almost never has the "sweat equity"/emotional ownership to actually go build it themselves (a "99.9%" framing, not empirical).
- Practical difficulty acknowledged: convincing someone else to fully buy into and execute *your* specific idea is hard. Alternative approach recommended: find an operating partner who already has their own business idea they're passionate about, then focus your own energy on being the "0 to 1" specialist for narrower pieces inside their business — a specific customer acquisition channel, a sales channel, a new hire process — rather than needing to own the whole company idea.
- Example given: Chris's real operating partner (in a tree-trimming business) said explicitly he'd have joined Chris in almost any business — the specific idea mattered less than partnering with Chris and being "in the game."
- Also flagged as a real limitation: an operating partner who is already succeeding with one business will resist being pulled into new ventures — treated as a good sign (their current business is working), not a failure of the partnership.
- Stated principle: "outsource your weaknesses, triple down on your strengths" rather than trying to force yourself into a role (e.g., getting an MBA to become more "operational") that doesn't fit your natural tendencies.

## 3. Hyper-local newsletter/Facebook group monetization plan
- Reference accounts to study directly: "Creating Jobs" (local newsletter in Edmonton/Calgary, Alberta) and "Life of a Scoop"/Ryan Sneden's "Naptown Scoop" (Annapolis, Maryland) — cited as the top authorities specifically on local newsletter growth.
- Core growth channel recommended: Meta (Facebook/Instagram) ads targeted to the local geographic area — acknowledges CPA will be higher than a nationwide newsletter (may cost ~$2 per subscriber acquired vs. less for broader lists) but argues this is offset because local, highly-targeted newsletters can charge advertisers 2–4x normal newsletter CPMs/ad rates due to hyper-relevant targeting.
- Tactic: acquire subscribers for free by participating authentically in existing local Facebook groups (buy/sell/garage sale groups, etc.) since you're a genuine local member with built-in trust/authority.
- Tactic: buy an existing dormant local Facebook group outright — claims these are extremely cheap since almost nobody wants to buy a random town's buy-sell-garage-sale group, and most aren't monetized by their current owners; use a tool like "Group Boss" to capture emails from group members at the door/on entry, and self-sponsor your own newsletter within the group you now own (vertical integration).
- Monetization upsell recommended beyond ad/sponsorship revenue: sell a white-labeled AI/automation service suite to local businesses via GoHighLevel (GHL) — specific service ideas: an AI phone-answering service for missed calls, automated review request/response management, or a full "Google Business Profile management" package — suggested pricing $500–$2,000/month per client. Rationale for why this combination works well as a business: you're vertically integrated (your own advertiser via the newsletter), you're white-labeling existing AI tooling rather than building your own, and it leverages a trending topic (AI) that plugs into an audience already primed for it.

## 4. Premium crypto/NFT domain names — realistic value assessment
- Direct opinion given to a caller holding domain names for crypto/NFT projects (e.g., "momcoin.com," "sincoin.com"): premium domain names are generally NOT valuable to cryptocurrency/NFT projects, unlike traditional SEO-driven businesses.
- Reasoning: crypto projects spread virally through Discord, Telegram, WhatsApp, Twitter/X, TikTok, and Instagram — not through people Googling a project name and finding a matching domain, so a domain doesn't meaningfully help a coin's adoption or price the way an exact-match domain (EMD, e.g., "HVACDallas.com" or "TexasRoofing.com") helps a local SEO-dependent business rank in Google search.
- Practical advice: list existing domains on marketplaces (mentions GoDaddy, Dan.com) immediately, at minimal effort/cost, to make them discoverable to potential buyers, but don't expect proactive outreach otherwise.
- Advice against building a cryptocurrency or NFT project purely because you already own a matching domain name — the domain's existence isn't sufficient market validation.
- Alternative suggestion: if you enjoy domain speculation, focus on trending/valuable non-crypto niches instead (examples given: AI, water quality, microplastics, longevity) where exact-match SEO value is real.

## 5. Cloning creators with AI chatbots — business/partnership structure
- Recap and extension of an idea from an earlier episode ("How to Use AI to Clone Others for Profit," Oct 14 2024): build an AI agent/chatbot trained on a creator's content (pulls the last ~50 Instagram Reels' transcripts, or similar), potentially automating this pipeline across many creators simultaneously (illustrative example: 20 creators' worth of chatbots built in parallel).
- Suggested outreach approach: gather creators' contact info across multiple channels (email, phone, Instagram, Twitter, even Strava/PayPal) since they're often flooded with DMs and may miss a single-channel pitch; reach out with a working demo already built ("I built this chatbot for you, check it out").
- Distribution/monetization paths for the finished chatbot: offer it as a bonus feature inside a creator's existing paid community (Skool, Kajabi, Circle, etc.) to boost membership value/retention; sell access to it as a paid add-on; or license/sell the finished chatbot for a one-time fee or a percentage of the revenue the creator resells it for to their own audience.
- Lead-sourcing tip specific to Skool communities: Chris's own newsletter subscriber resource (TKOpod.com) reportedly contains a scraped list of "hundreds or thousands" of Skool communities along with their reported revenue, usable to identify and prioritize outreach targets by how much money a given community/creator is already making.
- Value proposition to the creator: most creators, even if personally comfortable with ChatGPT, lack the specific technical know-how (model selection — Claude vs. Perplexity vs. ChatGPT — prompt engineering, ensuring the bot draws on the *entirety* of their content rather than a partial/hallucinating subset) to build this themselves well, which is the paid value being offered.
