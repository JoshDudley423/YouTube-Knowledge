# How to Grow your Business with $1 Bandit Signs
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aeWA1P42I3k | Published: 2024-03-25

Format: live Q&A ("Kerner's Corner") where Chris Koerner takes calls from listeners about their specific businesses/questions. Five separate segments, each with distinct, reusable tactical substance.

## 1. Home services vertical integration (landscaping business, ~$3M revenue)
- Caller owned a landscaping/hardscape construction business (acquired an existing DBA in 2023), 34 employees, $3M revenue, targeting $4M this year, ~$100K average ticket size, mostly upscale residential design-build hardscape/softscape work (patios, fireplaces), with a small maintenance/snow-removal segment.
- Chris's core method for deciding whether to pursue vertical integration (e.g., a nursery or landscape supply company) vs. just scaling the core service: calculate total addressable market and your actual market share first. Use Outscraper (a lead/business-data scraping tool, ~$4) to count competitors in your service area and niche (can filter by keyword, e.g., "hardscape" only), then estimate competitors' revenue by checking employee counts (via LinkedIn/website) against an assumed industry revenue-per-employee ratio (roughly $100K/employee estimated here) to size the total local market.
- Real example from Chris's own tree trimming business: was considering costly vertical diversification (fences, painting) and had started raising money to franchise the concept nationally. Spent about an hour on Outscraper searching all ZIP codes in DFW and found 950 tree trimming businesses listed on Google Maps in DFW alone (about 1 per 9,000 residents) — using a rough revenue distribution assumption (mix of side-hustlers at ~$50K/year up to a few eight/nine-figure companies), this implied a $500M+ local market; a separately purchased nationwide industry report valued the entire US tree trimming industry at ~$26B, and dividing by population share for DFW cross-validated the same ~$500M figure.
- Conclusion/lesson: rather than spend $1M+ and endure complex multi-state legal work to franchise, it made more sense to keep operations local (DFW only, canceled planned Utah/Alabama expansion) and simply capture more share of an under-penetrated local market — implying they held only a small percentage (e.g., ~5-20%) of total local demand. General rule of thumb given to the landscaping caller: if your market share is low (5-20%), focus on capturing more of what you're already doing (especially your highest-margin work) rather than diversifying into new verticals; only pursue new verticals seriously if you've already captured a large share (e.g., 80%+) of your core market. Expected cost savings from vertical integration (e.g., owning your own material supply) are likely minimal.

## 2. "Should I quit my job to start something?" (33-year-old engineering manager, analysis paralysis)
- Caller: 33, married soon, no kids yet, ~$200K+ available to invest, prior venture-backed startup that ultimately wasn't a financial success, currently a W2 engineering manager (rates job enjoyment 6/10), renovating his own house, running a small Amazon store and a new TikTok shop experiment, drawn to home services content but suffering "analysis paralysis" across too many options (buy vs. build vs. franchise, home services vs. manufacturing/factory, etc.).
- Chris's framing: analysis paralysis at this life stage (young, capital available, no dependents yet, has already tasted the entrepreneurial lifestyle and its downside) is treated as a signal that "you haven't found the thing yet," not a character flaw — distinguished from people who talk about starting something for years but never will.
- Recommended the "Wing Walker's Code": don't quit your job until whatever you're building has the *potential* to replace your income (it doesn't need to already be doing so) — framed as: nearly any business can scale to six figures in today's internet-connected economy.
- Concrete unconventional advice: don't over-research — instead, put a small amount of money (his example: $100-$5,000) into literally any low-stakes, non-"the-one" business (his example: buy a bounce house, throw up a cheap $10-20 website, rent it out) purely to get real operational experience, including negative experiences (an unhappy customer, a bad review threat) — the goal is to get "a taste" of the downside as well as the upside before committing further, and to unlock new business ideas that only occur to you once you're actually operating something (vs. sitting purely in analysis/W2 mode). Explicitly says: pick something that doesn't have to be "the thing," just something to build momentum and business intuition.
- Chris cites his own preference for rental-equipment businesses specifically as a good "starter" category (e.g., buy a used stump grinder for ~$2,000 from Facebook Marketplace, advertise, and do jobs yourself) because if a direct-to-consumer version underperforms, you can pivot the same asset toward a B2B model (e.g., subcontracting for tree trimming companies that outsource their stump grinding) — a single contractor relationship can generate recurring weekly job flow versus one-off residential jobs.

## 3. Bandit signs for a home cleaning business (Dallas-Fort Worth area)
- Caller runs a residential home cleaning business originally started for his wife (who has health issues) using a purchased course (Rohan Gilkes' "Launch 27" home cleaning franchise-style course, referenced by name). Business is currently getting more retail/commercial referral calls (via word of mouth from other operators) than residential leads, and he's weighing focusing on commercial cleaning.
- Question: whether "bandit signs" (small yard-style advertising signs) are viable, given some municipalities threaten fines (quoted ~$500/sign) for placing them, while other business owners online claim great success with them.
- Chris's answer: he has personally never had a bandit sign fined or removed by a municipality despite putting them out for his own past tree trimming business start (a friend's business specifically), still visible around Allen, TX years later, reportedly that friend's #1 lead source (though sample size of one). Suggests bandit signs work better in more rural areas with less enforcement, or in areas where you already observe signs surviving (implying low enforcement there) — recommends spending a couple hours scouting.
- More strongly recommended alternative/parallel lead source: real estate agents and property managers, since they touch many homes regularly and consistently need reliable cleaning contractors, and can become a recurring referral pipeline even though realtors themselves aren't a "recurring" customer. Suggested method: scrape a database of realtors, use searchbug.com to identify which phone numbers are cell phones vs. landlines, then use a compliant personalized SMS tool like Porterhouse (renders messages as blue "iPhone-style" bubbles) to text them directly — texting realtors was specifically claimed to work better than cold calling or cold emailing, though calling ("realtors always answer their phone") was noted as effective too.
- General note on this cleaning business: caller reported roughly 20% of first-year customers become recurring, doubling in subsequent years — used to frame the "long game" nature of the business.

## 4. Bookkeeping-as-a-service for a specific niche (financial professional wanting to hire VAs)
- Caller has a QuickBooks ProAdvisor-certified background (via helping run a family sign company's books) and wants to start a "labor arbitrage" bookkeeping service: hire an offshore bookkeeper via Upwork to manage client accounts (transaction filing, monthly reports), then resell that labor at a markup, potentially white-labeling to CPAs who don't offer bookkeeping themselves.
- Chris's guidance: agreed the labor-arbitrage model is sound, and recommended going even narrower/nichier than an obvious category like "dentists" — since bookkeeping is a commodity, differentiation comes from picking an unusual, hyper-specific niche (his examples: gutter cleaning companies, fencing repair companies) and testing multiple micro-niches to see what resonates, since there's no market "too small" — cites roughly 32 million small businesses in the US, most without a dedicated bookkeeper.
- For fulfillment: use Upwork, specifically look for an *individual* freelancer (not an agency — Upwork profiles disclose this), filtering for a combination of strong availability, many 5-star reviews, and high responsiveness — claims this combination predicts an ~80% chance of being a strong hire; Chris states he's never had a bad offshore bookkeeper hired this way.
- For customer acquisition: hire a separate virtual assistant (also via Upwork) specifically to do cold calling; use Outscraper to build a list of prospects in the chosen niche and identify which numbers are cell phones (better contact rate). Recommended pitch: a very simple, transparent flat-fee offer stated upfront (e.g., "we offer bookkeeping for gutter cleaning companies, it's $400/month, that's all we do") — deliberately no-frills/direct rather than a sales funnel. Suggested tracking response quality on a 1-10 scale in a spreadsheet across the first 100 cold calls to validate a niche before scaling further or testing a new niche (e.g., moving from gutter cleaners to fence repair companies).
- Suggested value-prop framing for why the service can be cheap: specialized knowledge of the niche's typical transaction categories (e.g., instantly recognizing what counts as a "gutter cleaning solution" line item) plus use of offshore labor.

## 5. 50/50 business partnership structure (portable restroom trailer rental business)
- Caller (Ryan, runs a local newsletter business — "Naptown Scoop" — reaching ~18,000 of a 41,000-person town) is partnering with a friend to launch a portable restroom trailer rental business, contributing equal cash investment plus an in-kind marketing/audience contribution (valued at roughly $5,000-10,000, representing lost ad-revenue opportunity cost from promoting the new business in his own newsletter instead of selling that ad space). Considering a 50/50 equity split (his partner's suggestion) with equal profit distributions.
- Chris had previously written in his newsletter against 50/50 partnerships by default, citing four conditions where it *does* work well: (1) both partners work physically side-by-side, not remote; (2) both are fully committed with no other job or side project; (3) both contributed equal capital; (4) both bring similarly valuable (not necessarily identical) skill sets. If all four aren't true, avoid a straight 50/50 split.
- In this specific case, workload/time split appeared roughly even in theory, but Chris flagged an important risk: one partner works highly visibly in public (physical trailer deliveries/operations) while the other partner (Ryan) works less visibly from home on marketing — over time, "out of sight, out of mind" resentment can build even if actual contributed value is comparable, since visible effort is perceived as more legitimate than less visible high-leverage work like digital marketing.
- Recommended concrete practices: (1) proactively discuss and pre-agree on what happens in various future scenarios (one partner underperforming, unequal capital calls for future equipment, business model pivoting) while the relationship is still easy/fun, since hard conversations are much harder to initiate once things go poorly; (2) bring in a neutral third-party tiebreaker with no relationship to either partner for future disputes; (3) track hours worked by each partner (even roughly/imperfectly) and consider tying distributions partly to a pro-rata time-based formula — shared a personal example of a 50/50 remote partnership where he and his partner track hours and split payouts proportionally (e.g., one month he logged 2.5 hours and received ~$200, his partner logged 100 hours and was paid proportionally more, and both were satisfied) — contrasted with a prior failed attempt to formalize hourly tracking with an *unwilling* partner who resisted the idea and later contributed little, reinforcing that voluntary buy-in to tracking matters.
- Also flagged: business timelines can shift significantly (e.g., 2-5 years before this specific rental business could realistically support a person full-time, based on estimated weekend-rental volume), so equity/effort assumptions made at launch may not hold as the business's actual shape evolves.
- Emphasized that just because a course-based business model (e.g., home cleaning franchise) didn't work personally for Chris doesn't mean it can't work for someone else committed to playing "the long game" (over a stated 10-year, $10M horizon in Ryan's case).
