# The 5 Best Swim Business Ideas for 2026
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5wuzKesBuz8 | Published: 2024-07-31

Guest: Josh Scott, owner of Swim SRQ / Swim Academy (5 locations, Sarasota/Tampa, FL).

## Business ideas discussed

### 1. Lane-rental swim school (Josh's core model)
- Rent lanes/pool time from hosted facilities (gyms, country clubs, senior living, neighborhood pools, swim teams) instead of building your own pool.
- First deal: ~$500/month to lease one lane. Grew from there via word-of-mouth/Facebook using the founders' existing competitive-swimming network.
- Best partnerships are percentage-rent deals (e.g., with a national gym chain) so incentives align — the better the swim school does, the more the host earns too, vs. flat-rent hosts who may resent crowding/noise.
- Risk: hosted locations can and do kick you out (e.g., country club neighbors complained about noise/lane access after only 3 weeks despite 85 signups; assisted-living facility ended the deal after new management/insurance concerns) — even with your own insurance and a contract, host relationships can end for reasons unrelated to performance.
- Company controls costs by paying instructors far above industry standard while keeping strong margins by focusing spend on labor quality, not facility polish.

### 2. Backyard/mobile swim lessons (recommended starting point for someone with no capital)
- Best model for a solo person / young entrepreneur with $0-$100 to start: go to people's homes or your own backyard/neighborhood pool and teach.
- Can earn ~$100,000/year working ~9 months out of the year via this model, per Josh.
- Downside of driving to clients: instructor spends significant time in transit (e.g., 30 min drive for a 30 min lesson = only 4 hours of billable lessons in an 8-hour day), so charge a premium rate to make it worth it.
- Tactic: set up literally next to a franchise swim school and offer private 1-on-1 lessons as the differentiator against their 4:1/5:1/6:1 group classes — charge a premium for individual attention.

### 3. Above-ground pool "pop-up" swim school (the idea Josh is actively building toward)
- Put an above-ground pool inside a small commercial space (~3,000 sq ft, ideally industrial/office-flex with high ceilings and good street signage) instead of a $1.3M-$2.6M+ purpose-built facility (Big Blue Swim School's two-pool buildout can run ~$5M).
- Advantages: much lower capital, no digging into the ground (avoids the $50K-$100K escrow landlords often require to backfill a pool if you go bankrupt/leave during a 10-15 year lease), and the pool is a depreciable equipment asset (tax benefit) rather than a real-estate improvement that raises the property's tax assessment. If it fails, you can literally sell the pool on Facebook Marketplace and walk away.
- Target: break even/profitable around 300 kids instead of the 700 kids/week a typical franchise-model buildout needs to break even.
- Piggyback opportunity: partner with businesses that already have suitable space and foot traffic — gymnastics studios, daycare/early-education centers, trampoline parks — offering them a revenue share (e.g., ~20%) to install a pool in unused space, using their existing families as the customer base. One near-deal: a gymnastics studio owner with a 20,000-25,000 sq ft space; deal didn't close because she wasn't ready to sacrifice an existing youth program to make room.
- Josh also floated approaching childcare/preschool chains with large outdoor play areas (2-3 acre properties) — mentioned private equity groups investing in childcare are interested in this "rollup into many locations" angle.

### 4. Rent a house just for the pool (Swimply-style), then run swim lessons on the offhours
- Chris's original idea (tweeted ~8 months prior): move somewhere like Scottsdale, rent a house with a nice pool, list it on Swimply/Peerspace for hourly rental.
- Numbers cited: nice pools with party access rent for ~$200-$300/hour; weekends get ~70% of bookings and command the highest rates ($300-$400/hour); a Saturday pool party for 12 people splitting the cost via Venmo pencils out easily.
- Combined model discussed with Josh: run swim lessons Mon-Fri (and maybe Saturday morning), then rent the pool out for parties the rest of the time (including "wild crowd" late-night parties) — you don't need to own the house, just rent it with landlord approval.
- Chris's added tip: negotiate an option to buy the house at a set price (e.g., 10% above appraised value) when you first rent it — if the business becomes very profitable, the option is far more valuable than just renting.
- Constraints: HOAs commonly prohibit home-based businesses (even in Florida, which has a state law protecting home-based businesses if certain criteria are met, e.g., staff must live on-site); insurance carriers are increasingly dropping swim schools/pool-party liability coverage.

### 5. Adjacent service/agency ideas for the swim school industry (not swim schools themselves)
- Marketing/automation agency for swim schools: many swim school owners are unsophisticated about digital marketing (SMS/text automation "blows their mind"); opportunity to build/manage lead-flow automation (via GoHighLevel), SEO, and Google/Facebook ad management specifically for swim schools.
- Short-form video/content agency: create social clips (e.g., toddler swimming across a pool, backflips) for swim schools — content "sells itself," and many owners don't know how to edit video themselves.
- Note: affordability varies wildly by owner tier — PE-backed/franchise owners can pay for full agency service; small independent/backyard operators often can't afford a few thousand dollars/month, so a lower-cost/self-serve tier could unlock a bigger addressable market.

## Numbers (Josh's business)
- 5 locations, "seven figure" swim school (implies $1M+ revenue).
- Margins: 30-35% for his hosted-facility model; a comparable franchise using the same hosted-facility model runs 20-25% margins (lower due to royalty fees). Industry rule of thumb: ~20% margin is the goal for a built facility; 25%+ margin gets private equity interested in acquiring.
- Instructor pay: started at $25/hr six years ago (2018-ish), now up to ~$40/hr for instructors who can prove results — deliberately paying well above the industry standard cited in the viral tweet ($14/hr instructor vs. $350/hr revenue per lane cited by commenters).
- Group class ratio: 3:1 or 4:1 (vs. industry-standard 4:1+ at big franchises); ~35% of Josh's business is private (1:1) lessons, unusually high for a school of his size.
- Example hosted-location cost structure (refurbished outdoor pool site): $1,500/month rent + $900-$1,000/month utilities + chlorine ≈ $3,000-$3,500/month all-in overhead.
- Franchise buildout costs cited from disclosure documents: smaller franchise ~$1.3M-$2.6M to build; Big Blue Swim School (two-pool model) likely well above that, possibly ~$5M.
- Private equity has already rolled up major players (Emler acquired by PE around 12 locations; Big Blue is PE-backed) — PE firms reportedly want $1-2M EBITDA before investing, and are actively calling independent owners like Josh to build portfolios.

## Caveats / risks
- Anybody can legally teach swim lessons in many states (AZ, CA, TX, FL) — low barrier to entry means franchises struggle to differentiate and independents can undercut them.
- Insurance is a growing constraint: major carriers are dropping swim school and pool-party liability coverage; outdoor pools bring weather/lightning risk (a daily risk in Florida vs. rare in California), which pushes larger operators toward indoor pools.
- Hosted-facility partnerships are inherently fragile — Josh has both signed and lost multiple locations due to host-side politics/complaints even when performing well and fully insured.
- The industry has an incentive problem the video calls out explicitly: many swim schools (per Chris's original viral tweet) are financially incentivized to prolong lessons rather than actually teach kids to swim quickly, since recurring revenue depends on retention; Josh's counter is to teach efficiently but build a "love of the water" that keeps kids coming back voluntarily (progressing into pre-competitive/competitive swim team) rather than deliberately dragging out instruction.
- Referral programs: Josh has tried referral bonuses but found low motivation/no good tracking software — hasn't found a working referral mechanism despite wanting one.
