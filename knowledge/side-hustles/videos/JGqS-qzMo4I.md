# Comfort Is the Trap Keeping You Average. Here's How to Break Out
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JGqS-qzMo4I | Published: 2025-09-24

Interview with Ben Wilson (host of "How to Take Over the World" podcast, which profiles historical leaders/conquerors/entrepreneurs/artists). This is a mindset/leadership-psychology episode, not a specific business idea or tactical how-to — no dollar figures, startup costs, or step-by-step processes for a side hustle. Included here because it's framed by the channel as directly applicable to small business owners and side-hustlers; content below extracts the operating principles that could inform how someone runs or grows a business.

## Core framework: what separates "good from great" (per Ben Wilson's research)

- **Communication at scale is the defining shared trait** across entrepreneurs, conquerors, and artists — "you don't become great by working hard, you become great by getting other people to work hard [or believe]." Cited examples: Julius Caesar (elite writer/letter-writer who ran his empire largely through written correspondence), Napoleon (got his start via a political pamphlet, not a battle), Steve Jobs (the "bicycle for the mind" analogy to explain the personal computer's value), Elon Musk (not a strong public speaker but a highly effective mass communicator via Twitter/X and vision-casting for SpaceX/Tesla).
- Practical implication for a small business: the same 33 million-business, ~30-million-with-zero-employees US business landscape is cited as evidence that most small businesses fail to scale specifically because the owner can't communicate a vision/direction that gets others (employees, customers) to act — not because of lack of technical skill.
- **You don't need to be a strong communicator in every medium** — find the one channel (writing, a specific platform, in-person pitching) that plays to your strength and lean into it, rather than trying to be universally good at communication.

## "Insider-outsider" pattern

- High achievers across history tend to have partial access/legitimacy (an "insider" position — royal education, elite institution, family connections) while simultaneously being an outsider in some dimension (nationality, social class, geography) — cited: Napoleon (minor Corsican/Italian nobility in France), Alexander Hamilton (Caribbean-island origin who reached King's College), Picasso (Spanish-trained artist who made his career in Paris). Framed as a chip-on-the-shoulder/proving-yourself dynamic, though the guest cautions against over-attributing this to childhood trauma alone.

## Obsession/addiction as a driver, not just discipline

- Argues many great achievers are functionally "addicted" to their pursuit rather than driven purely by trauma or grit — cited: Thomas Edison reading a biography of Michael Faraday, realizing at 23 he'd accomplished less, and from that point neglecting sleep/hygiene/possessions to fund his inventing habit.
- Caution: the same addictive-personality trait that produces greatness can just as easily manifest as substance addiction or family/relationship damage if directed at unproductive things — the advice given is to be deliberate about which "addiction" you feed.
- Related dopamine framing: chase the dopamine that leads to action, not the dopamine that leads to inaction (passive consumption) — useful reframe for anyone using "I need motivation" as an excuse.

## Confidence / "strategic selective irrationality"

- Confidence is described as a necessary (not optional) trait for leadership — without it, you can't get buy-in from others to work toward your vision. Illustrated via Napoleon's Battle of Marengo (down 40,000 troops against 100,000, spent the day preventing his line from routing purely through projected confidence until reinforcements arrived and won late in the day).
- **"Strategic selective irrationality"**: successful organizations/leaders sometimes deliberately keep an economically "irrational" practice because it embodies their core values/differentiation — e.g., Costco's $1.50 hot dog-and-drink combo held below what "rational" pricing would suggest; In-N-Out's disproportionate labor spent keeping tables spotless. Applied to small business: a home-service business owner's stated intent to be "the best/most professional in [market]" can look delusional from outside but is what drives differentiated results.
- Confidence can be built via **acting as if you believe before you actually do** ("action precedes belief") — repetition of a stated vision, even when initially uncomfortable/embarrassing to say out loud, is presented as the mechanism for building genuine confidence over time.

## Bias for action

- Cited example: the Wright Brothers beat far better-funded, more "planned" competitors (e.g., Smithsonian-backed Samuel Langley) by skipping elaborate theory-first planning and instead rapidly iterating with kites and gliders in Kitty Hawk, NC before ever adding an engine.
- Napoleon's stated principle: "First I engage, then I figure out what to do" — take action to create information/contact, then adapt, rather than waiting for a complete plan.
- Directly mapped to the "minimum viable product" concept (a skateboard → scooter → bike → motorcycle → car progression, not a wheel → half-car → full car progression) — ship the smallest usable thing to build momentum and real feedback.

## Timing matters as much as ability

- "History is littered with people who had the wrong idea, or the right idea at the wrong time — which is the same as having the wrong idea." Example: Polaroid's founder Edwin Land spent years failing to commercialize polarized-material applications (windshields, aircraft) before hitting on instant photography, which then took off explosively — same underlying technology/skill, wildly different outcome based on finding the right application/timing.
- Practical framing for focus: instead of over-focusing narrowly on "one business" regardless of fit, focus on your core skill/strength ("your ikigai," your superpower) and be willing to abandon a specific venture when the timing/market isn't right — described as "serial focus" (fully committing to one thing at a time, but not being afraid to pivot away entirely when signals say it's not working), rather than either constant dabbling or stubborn persistence on a dead idea.
- Signal to watch for: if a specific effort (e.g., cold emailing) isn't generating any positive signal and you don't enjoy doing it, that's information the channel/approach or the underlying business itself may not be right — not just a call to "grind harder."

## Grit / resilience — "the Rockefeller principle"

- John D. Rockefeller reportedly got rejected by every one of ~30 target companies in Cleveland across three separate rounds of interviews before one finally gave him an internship "just to get him to stop knocking" — from there, a direct line to becoming the wealthiest man in the world.
- **The Rockefeller principle** (as coined by Ben Wilson): if you're clear on what you actually want, fail at least twice as much as a "reasonable person" would tolerate before concluding it's not going to work and moving on.
- Suggested practice for building resilience to rejection: deliberately set a numeric **rejection quota** (e.g., "I need to get rejected 20 times today" — used during a door-to-door missionary experience) to reframe each "no" as progress toward a target rather than a personal blow; combined with the "20 nos = 1 yes" statistical framing, tracking rejections becomes a leading indicator of upcoming wins.

## Energy and passion over raw intelligence

- The single most consistent trait among great achievers, per the guest, is not intelligence but **energy** — when you're doing something you love, it generates energy rather than consuming it ("a perpetual energy machine"), letting you meaningfully out-work people motivated only by money/obligation.
- Nuance on "follow your passion": the advice is reframed as "follow passion" (plural/verb, not a single fixed noun) — don't assume you have exactly one true calling; actively experiment to discover what generates energy for you (examples given: discovering enjoyment in mowing your own lawn, or taking up chess later in life).
- **"Zeal" (ancient Greek sense)**: defined as the specific pain felt seeing someone else achieve something great that you feel capable of but haven't yet done — cited as the emotional trigger behind many historical turning points (Edison vs. Faraday; Julius Caesar weeping at a statue of Alexander the Great, noting Alexander had conquered the world by an age Caesar had already passed; Alex Hormozi's reaction to seeing Kylie Jenner on a Forbes cover). Recommends deliberately seeking out role models/history that produce this feeling as fuel.
- **Anti-inspiration technique**: Johann Sebastian Bach reportedly reviewed music he considered mediocre before composing, specifically because it generated competitive energy/ideas ("I can do this better") — whereas reviewing work he considered untouchably great was demotivating. The host relates this to comparing his own e-commerce fulfillment business favorably against sloppier competitors as a motivational device, in addition to looking up to more polished competitors like ShipBob.
- General prescription: maintain three reference points simultaneously — people you respect/aspire to (greats), people whose lack of standards you want to avoid becoming (anti-inspiration), and peers roughly at your own level to compete with day-to-day.

## Vision as the ultimate scarce resource

- Closing thesis: "The greatest resource constraint in the world is vision... we have more than enough money, energy, and everything else — we just lack vision." Central example: Joan of Arc — a 16-year-old with no military experience or strategic skill who, purely through total conviction in her vision, rallied a losing French army to march directly at the English and turn the war.
- Practical suggestion for building/communicating vision in a small business: state the ambitious goal explicitly and repeatedly, even when it initially feels embarrassing (e.g., a pressure-washing business owner telling people at a dinner party "I want to be the biggest pressure washing business in DFW" rather than downplaying ambition) — conviction/authenticity in stating the vision is what makes it "infectious" to others (illustrated via a Mission: Impossible III scene where a character's genuine enthusiasm makes even a boring job — "traffic analyst" — sound compelling).

## Caveats

- This episode is philosophical/historical, not a validated business case study — contains no dollar figures, startup costs, or concrete step-by-step processes for any specific side hustle or business.
- The guest explicitly cautions against over-crediting childhood trauma narratives for high achievers, favoring the "addiction to the craft" framing instead — presented as his own interpretation/opinion, not a settled finding.
- The "strategic selective irrationality" framework is presented as a double-edged trait: useful for differentiation and cultural identity, but the video does not address how to distinguish productive irrational commitments from wasteful ones in a real business — left as an exercise for the operator's own judgment.
