# Exactly How to Start This Genius AI Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OwfkLLox9rw | Published: 2024-12-10

"HoldCo Bros" episode with co-host Nik (@NikonomicsPodcast) — brainstorming/"Mary, Date, or Kill" format covering two main ideas: (1) capturing elderly family stories using AI/video ("Silver Tsunami" series), and (2) a biohacking accountability-challenge side hustle. Ends with a ranking of 5 recurring ideas.

## Idea 1: "Silver Tsunami" story-capture / genealogical services business
### Market context
- US population 65+ is projected to nearly double from 52 million today to 95 million by 2050 (adding ~40 million people in ~25 years).
- Alzheimer's/dementia cases projected to double from 6 million to 13 million by 2050.
- Dependency ratio rising from 52 dependents per 100 working-age people today to 77 per 100 by 2050.
- Genealogical services market (Ancestry, FamilySearch, 23andMe, etc.) projected to grow from ~$5B/year to ~$6B by ~2033.

### The core idea
- Capture elderly family members' oral stories/history before they're lost, using two methods: (1) video interviews (similar to what StoryWorth does via email prompts, but with actual video — using tools like Riverside), and (2) AI-assisted digitization of handwritten journals/records (OCR/handwriting-recognition tools: Adobe Scan, Microsoft Lens, Google Cloud Vision, Amazon Textract), then feeding transcribed text into an LLM.
- End products that could be built from captured material: a chatbot trained on a specific relative's stories/answers that family members can query later; edited video "episodes"/podcast-style story compilations; physical books (similar to StoryWorth's book output); a searchable digitized-records service for families.
- Distribution/go-to-market model proposed:
  1. Partner with local assisted living/independent living/skilled nursing facilities. Recruit high school student volunteers to visit weekly and digitize residents' paper records/photos for families (can be done for free as a "senior project"-style initiative, or monetized).
  2. Partner with local photographers/videographers (they already have lighting/camera/mic equipment) to run a weekly "story time" session at a facility, capturing residents' stories on video — residents get social visits/attention they otherwise lack, families get a keepsake.
  3. Package outputs and sell back to residents/families, or convert into chatbot/book/podcast products.
- Monetization framing: could charge the facility (as an amenity that helps fill beds/differentiate the facility) or charge the family directly, or a hybrid.
- Economics discussed: one additional filled bed at an assisted living facility could represent significant profit (not just incremental — most costs are already fixed, so an incremental resident is high-margin). Average length of stay at assisted living cited as ~10 years, so acquiring even one additional long-term resident is valuable to a facility's economics. However, the real challenge is proving conversion/attribution (that this service is why someone chose the facility), which most facility owners will be skeptical of.
- Suggested test/pilot approach: offer the service completely free to one facility for a trial period (proposed 1-3 months, not a full year), asking only that the facility train its marketing staff to actively pitch/mention the service to prospective residents' families, and that they send a short anonymous survey to new residents asking whether the story-capture offering influenced their decision — this generates real attribution data to use in future sales pitches to other facilities.
- "Vitamin vs. painkiller" framework applied: this is a "vitamin" (nice-to-have, not essential), which changes the buyer psychology — a resident's adult children (who write the check but are one step removed emotionally) may balk at paying directly for it, but the same product bundled for free/included as part of a facility's fee is more likely to be chosen. Alternative fallback if direct sales fail: record stories for free, use AI to generate a short highlight "sizzle reel," and send it to family members as a low-friction upsell trigger (analogous to theme-park ride-photo upsells) — once they see the emotional payoff, conversion becomes much easier.
- Physical implementation concept discussed in depth: build a small dedicated recording "podcast studio" or purchase a prefab soundproof phone-booth-style pod (referencing commercially available office pods that have sold ~$50M worth of units) and place it in a facility's lobby. Staff need no training beyond "press the big red button"; a motion sensor could auto-start/stop recording (similar to a light-switch occupancy sensor) so there's no need to staff or schedule a photographer. Estimated cost: ~$5,000 upfront for a studio/pod build-out plus a few hundred dollars/month in software, with otherwise near-zero marginal cost per session. AI voice prompting (e.g., a conversational LLM voice agent) could substitute for a human interviewer to help an elderly guest who doesn't know where to start telling their story — cited unsolved detail: making a lone participant comfortable talking to a pod with no visible interviewer.
- Reference concept for capturing "life logs": Rewind AI, a wearable/Mac-and-iPhone app that continuously records and transcribes everything a person says/does and lets you query it via LLM later — proposed idea of "reselling" a Rewind-like wearable specifically to an older demographic under different branding as an easy, passive way to keep recording family stories throughout daily life, rather than a single formal recording session.
- Host's mother is cited as a personal example: she has tens of thousands of pages of handwritten journals spanning decades that he plans to digitize/transcribe into an LLM so family can later query specific memories (e.g., "what was Mom's day like on a specific date").

## Idea 2: Biohacking accountability-challenge side hustle
- Concept (credited as inspired by "Justin Mays," a Twitter business figure who ran this with friends/family previously): sell a health accountability challenge where participants buy a continuous glucose monitor (CGM), all use the same device/brand, and pay an upfront stake (e.g., $1,000) to keep their blood sugar within a target range for a set period (e.g., 3 months, with 1-2 allowed slip-ups). Successful participants get their full stake back; those who fail forfeit it to the organizer.
- Framed as a "you win either way" model for the organizer: if participants succeed, it's a genuine health win and the organizer earns via an affiliate commission on the CGM purchase (specifically mentioned: ~$100 per signup via affiliate link, paid by the device manufacturer, not the participant); if participants fail, the organizer keeps the forfeited stakes. Explicitly compared to an insurance/underwriting business model — the operator needs realistic odds on completion rates or the economics don't work (if everyone succeeds, no forfeiture revenue; if nobody succeeds, it could look predatory).
- Generalizable framework beyond glucose/health: an "accountability partner as a service" model — pay into a pot, get your money back (or more) if you complete a defined challenge; broadly applicable to any measurable behavior change, not just health (the hosts compare it to a course-based/newsletter-based challenge model discussed in a separate episode).
- CGMs themselves: explained as a health/biohacking tool (normally prescribed for diabetics but increasingly used by non-diabetics) that shows real-time blood sugar response to specific foods/behaviors, enabling personalized "hacks" (e.g., pairing carbs with protein, or taking a 15-minute walk after a high-carb meal reduces blood sugar spike effect by roughly 70% per the hosts' claim).

## General business-selection philosophy discussed (not idea-specific)
- Monetization approach recommended for a values-driven or ad-averse creator: rather than selling generic/irrelevant ads, monetize by building products/challenges you'd want to do anyway and let affiliate commissions or forfeited stakes fund the work — feels more authentic and audience-aligned.
- "Venn diagram" framework for choosing what business to pursue: three circles — what you're passionate about, what you're good at, and what you can actually charge money for — must all overlap; most people only consider the first two.
- Passion isn't necessarily about the specific industry/vertical — one host says his real passion is "the game of business/commerce itself" (citing Warren Buffett as a similar capital-allocator personality), not any single product category, so he's comfortable building businesses across unrelated niches (porta-potties, software, agencies) rather than "following a passion" for one specific field. Still argues you need enough genuine interest in a given venture to survive the inevitable "eat crap for a while" grind phase (sales, cold outreach, people management, etc. — whatever part of running it you personally find unpleasant).

## Idea rankings ("Marry, Date, or Kill" — both hosts largely agreed)
- **Marry** (favorite/highest conviction): the assisted-living story-capture/"Silver Tsunami" idea — both hosts loved it despite acknowledging real friction (assisted living facilities being hard to sell into, $5K pod cost).
- **Date** (liked, would pursue but not top pick): a Facebook-groups lead-brokering/automation idea (playing middleman between people who want leads and people who have leads) — seen as fast to scale with near-zero cost, though highly competitive.
- **No man's land** (mixed feelings): a local email-newsletter-to-high-ticket-service pivot idea, and (for one host) the biohacking challenge — biohacking seen as hard to scale beyond a large existing audience.
- **Kill**: a live local music venue idea (inspired by Nashville) — both hosts rejected it as too logistics-heavy and outside their skill set (not "events people").
