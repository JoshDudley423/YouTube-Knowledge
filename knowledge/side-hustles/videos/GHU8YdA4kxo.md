# The Most Overlooked Side Hustle You Can Start From Home
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GHU8YdA4kxo | Published: 2026-04-19

Interview with Raema of Gifty Gal — a corporate/personalized gifting business she built as a stay-at-home mother of four, working ~20 hours/week.

## The business idea
- Corporate gifting: curated, personalized gift boxes primarily for B2B sales prospecting (helping sales reps get cold outreach opened/answered), plus client "thank you" gifts, event invitations, and year-end holiday client gifts. Her stated niche within the category is prospecting gifts. Client base is mostly tech companies (concentrated in the Dallas area, where many tech companies have relocated/headquartered).
- Core value proposition: personalization dramatically increases response rate versus generic branded swag (tumbler + pen) — gifts are built around specific facts about the recipient gathered from the sales rep (alma mater, hobbies, sports, drinking preference, etc.), including a handwritten card. Cited example: a themed gift built entirely around a prospect's college and a March Madness/basketball tie-in (matching branded mugs for both the recipient's alma mater and the client's own logo, a coffee item, and themed chocolate).
- Framed as a service that saves the client money/time even though it "costs" $100: a tech sales rep earning ~$250K/year (~$125/hour) spending 2-4 hours sourcing and assembling their own gift would effectively spend $500+ of their own time on a $100-value outcome — makes outsourcing an easy financial decision for the client's employer.

## Numbers
- Revenue growth by year: Year 1 (2022, started with baby-shower/personal gifts, no corporate yet): $75,000 gross revenue, but only $7,800 net profit (attributed to not yet knowing how to price, and initially selling B2C using retail-bought product rather than wholesale). Year 2: gross grew by about $100,000 (net ~$25,000). Year 3 (2025, after fully pivoting to and specializing in corporate gifting with more disciplined pricing): $357,000 gross revenue, $119,000 net profit — all while working roughly 20 hours/week and being a full-time stay-at-home parent of four.
- Margin target: 50% net margin per gift is the goal (paying wholesale prices, having a reseller's license); minimum acceptable margin is 30%. This 50% figure is stated as true net (after cost of goods), not just gross markup.
- Typical prospecting gift price point: ~$100 per box (matches whatever budget the corporate client sets; personalization doesn't change the price much — it's about the same whether heavily personalized or not). Shipping is billed separately (often run through the client's own corporate shipping account to get their negotiated rates) rather than baked into the box price — she explicitly warns against folding variable/heavy/international shipping costs into a flat gift price, calling it a mistake she made early on ("don't make it into your prices or you'll sell yourself short").
- Order minimums (added after early missteps): won't do individual gifts under $50, and requires a minimum order of 10 gifts per batch.
- Example unit economics at the $50 gift tier: $50 gross gift price → ~$25 gross profit each; a 10-gift minimum order = ~$500 gross profit / ~$250 net profit for roughly 5 hours of work — about $50/hour net, described as realistic for early clients.
- Startup cost: ~$800 total, primarily an expedited LLC filing fee (this was overpaying — she recommends Bizee, a free LLC/registered-agent/EIN filing service where you only pay mandatory state filing fees, versus paying a paid formation service on top of those fees). An EIN (obtained via the LLC) is required before any wholesale account can be opened, which is required to protect margins (buying B2C/retail was the direct cause of her unprofitable first year).
- Packaging supply costs cited as a real, easy-to-underestimate line item: a basic shipping box ~$5, bubble wrap/packing peanuts ~$2; crinkle paper (packaging filler) called out specifically as more expensive than people expect and easy to overspend on when filling a large box.
- Physical space progression: started packing gifts at home (eventually outgrew the house — described as creating friction in her marriage from constant gift-bag clutter everywhere) → moved into a shared warehouse workspace model (a post-COVID e-commerce-driven category of flexible small-unit warehouse space) starting at 400 sq ft (~1.5 years after starting the business), upgraded to 600 sq ft about a year later, then added additional units, reaching ~1,500 sq ft total. Leases are technically flexible/short-term but she opts for annual leases to negotiate better per-unit pricing.

## Sourcing and fulfillment
- Buys product wholesale via sites like Faire (a wholesale marketplace for small businesses) or by contacting brands/vendors directly to ask about wholesale programs (some require a storefront or other qualifying criteria she hasn't yet met for certain vendors).
- Uses local vendors for customization/personalization (e.g., laser engraving — she invested in an in-house laser engraver — and logo/decal printing on boxes and bags), valued for fast turnaround during peak/holiday season.
- Sources custom branded boxes internationally (from China); explicitly warns that international sourcing requires planning around ~20-25 day standard sea shipping times, and that needing expedited shipping to hit a deadline can quietly erode margin if not anticipated.
- Packing technique: uses filler/crinkle paper and spacing to prevent glass items from shaking and breaking against each other in transit; treats the unboxing visual experience (box branding/appearance, not just contents) as a meaningful part of the product itself, comparing it to Apple's unboxing experience.
- Recommended cheaper shipping options (Chris's addition, not necessarily Raema's own tools): ShipStation, Shopify shipping rates, Pirate Ship, Stamps.com, or calling FedEx/UPS directly and asking for a dedicated rep — reps can often provide meaningful (~60% off retail) discounted rates even for low monthly shipping volume.

## Go-to-market / growth
- 100% organic growth via referral and word-of-mouth since inception — no paid marketing dollars invested in growth (tried Instagram ads once; they didn't perform).
- LinkedIn (not Instagram) is by far her top lead-generation channel: clients posting about receiving her gifts on their own LinkedIn exposes her to their entire professional network, generating inbound inquiries.
- Origin of her first corporate client: a single early LinkedIn post about starting the business (before any corporate specialization) was reshared by her husband (who works in corporate) to his network and by friends to theirs; this reached a sales rep at a company her husband works with, who became her first "prospecting" client and introduced her to other reps — creating a domino effect that became the entire corporate side of the business.
- Started deliberately in a lower-stakes B2C category (baby shower and personal gifts) before entering corporate gifting, both to learn the mechanics/pricing without corporate-level reputational risk, and because lacking prior corporate gifting experience let her avoid convention and design more distinctive, non-generic gifts than typical industry offerings (tumbler + pen).
- Stated framework for running the business, described as a genuinely simple 4-part loop: (1) **Connect** — put yourself out there publicly (primarily LinkedIn), respond quickly to inbound interest; (2) **Collaborate** — work directly with the client to learn their goals, budget, and who the gift needs to reach; (3) **Curate** — source products and pricing that maximize both budget fit and expected response rate from the recipient; (4) **Fulfill** — execute logistics precisely (shipping details, correct names/addresses, careful packing).

## Advice for someone starting this
- Know your target market before scaling — deliberately avoid straddling both B2C and B2B simultaneously (her own early mistake), since pricing, margin structure, and trust requirements differ significantly.
- Consider starting with lower-stakes personal/B2C gifting first to build skill and product sense before taking on corporate clients, since a major fulfillment mistake with a corporate client can damage the business's whole reputation, whereas mistakes on a baby-shower gift are low-stakes "paid education."
- Get an LLC, separate business bank account, and a wholesale/reseller license early — this was the single biggest lever separating her unprofitable first year from her profitable third year.
- Build relationships with local customization vendors in advance of peak season (they provide critical fast-turnaround capacity during holidays).
- Don't be afraid to say no to a bad-fit client or a request that doesn't make financial sense.

## Caveats
- Growth was aided by a personal network advantage (a spouse already embedded in the exact corporate/tech world she wanted to sell into) that a solo starter without that network may need to replicate through more deliberate outreach.
- Margin/profitability lessons (wholesale accounts, not folding shipping into flat prices, order minimums) were learned the hard way through an unprofitable first year — the 50% net margin standard reflects year-three maturity, not typical early-stage performance.
