# I Started These 14 Businesses. Here's How You Can Copy Them. (Part 3/4)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pXiFFRRItHk | Published: 2024-10-16

Chris Koerner runs through businesses he's personally started, with notes on how to replicate each today.

## Tree Biz Bootcamp (in-person/virtual course)
- Sold a 2-day in-person bootcamp (Dallas) teaching people how to start a tree-trimming business, plus a virtual/recorded version.
- Marketing: a couple of tweets + one newsletter email → ~20 signups (~60% in-person, 40% virtual).
- Numbers: ~$45,000-$50,000 gross revenue for two days of instruction (excluding prep time); roughly half was profit (~$20k profit) plus about a week of prep work.
- Feedback score: 9.7/10 from anonymous attendee survey.
- Format: guest speakers (marketers, accountants), site visits to real jobs, equipment/checklist/subcontractor-hiring instruction.
- Replicable pattern: monetize your operational knowledge as a paid course/bootcamp, or piggyback on someone else's audience to co-create a course and split revenue.

## Fast Tree Care (tree trimming/removal business)
- Launched with a partner (James, a recent college grad) — gave him a truck (2011 Tundra) and the playbook from a prior tree business, paid him a flat monthly salary, ran the business by subcontracting out all actual tree work (they "don't own a chainsaw," just a truck).
- Key framework introduced: "binary outcome" home service businesses (tree removal, pressure washing, lawn mowing, appliance repair, window cleaning, HVAC, plumbing) — the job is either done or not, minimizing subjective customer dissatisfaction, versus "non-binary outcome" businesses (custom home building, house painting) where there are many subjective ways to disappoint a customer (wrong paint shade, primer choice, etc.).
- Takeaway: when picking a home-service niche, favor ones with a clear, binary success/fail outcome.

## Blue Metric (RV park / campground acquisitions, syndication)
- Koerner acts as a co-GP/general partner sourcing capital and off-market deals (using his audience), writing investor updates, marketing.
- Deal structure: syndication, not a pooled fund — raises $1-8M per individual park deal.
- Compensation: earns a small equity stake in each deal proportional to the capital he raises, plus a small cut of the acquisition fee at closing.

## Busy (business-for-sale aggregator, busy.com)
- Aggregated business-for-sale listings scraped from dozens of sites into a Google Sheet, emailed out weekly — free, never monetized.
- Some subscribers reportedly acquired businesses off these lists.
- Shut down due to a co-founder taking another internship and Koerner's bandwidth constraints — explicit lesson: an idea with no clear monetization path tends to get deprioritized.
- Suggested monetization paths if redone: % referral fee from brokers for buyer referrals, or a monthly SaaS fee.
- Adjacent bigger opportunity flagged: connect small-town Main Street business owners (restaurants, dentists, small SaaS companies) wanting to sell with competent business brokers for a referral fee. Brokers typically charge sellers 5-10% of sale price; a referrer can often get 10-25% of the broker's fee. Example: a $2M business sale nets the broker $200k; a 25% referral cut = $50k for the connector. Caveat: extremely long sales cycles (weeks to years) and high deal-fallout rate ("home sales times 10 on difficulty").

## No Greasy (powdered restaurant degreaser)
- Sold a high-concentration powdered restaurant degreaser (vs. standard liquid degreaser, which is mostly shipped water) to restaurant owners.
- Distribution: texted ~50+ restaurant owners, sent free samples, followed up for orders (executed with a partner, James).
- Margins described as "really good"; product is recurring (restaurants reorder degreaser monthly).
- Best call times for restaurant owners: before 10am or between 2-4pm (between shifts).
- Caveat/hard part: restaurant owners are habituated to buying everything (food, supplies, cleaning products) from one or two big distributors (US Foods, Sysco) — breaking that single-source habit is the main obstacle, even though the product itself is superior.

## Lead Texter (SMS lead-gen for home service businesses, 2019-2020)
- Generated leads for home service business owners (roofers especially) via cold texting, then sold the leads.
- Caveat/main obstacle: regulatory — can't text people without opt-in or people on the Do Not Call list; found workarounds but was worried about long-term scalability, similar to how cold email has restrictions cold SMS doesn't fully avoid.
- Reframed opportunity today: cold SMS as an underused marketing channel — cites ~97% open rate on SMS vs ~20% on email, and double-digit response rates vs ~5% for email.
- Market framing: best customers for marketing services are home service businesses that are busy/successful but not so big they're hard to reach, and have budget ($1k-$4k/month) to scale further. Cites 30M+ small businesses in the US (~10% of the population). A related current (undisclosed-detail) project, "Repeat Leads," does this same lead-gen work for home service owners.

## Easy MLS (flat-fee MLS listing automation)
- Targeted FSBO (for-sale-by-owner) home sellers who weren't listed on MLS/Zillow/Redfin; automated texts/emails offering flat-fee MLS listing (~$99-$399) to get 10x more buyer eyeballs (still requires paying 1-3% buyer's agent commission separately).
- Fully automated funnel: text/email outreach → automatic payment → auto-sent Typeform for listing description/photos.
- Caveat/failure point at the time (5 years ago): too much manual friction remained for the customer (writing description, taking professional photos, answering calls) relative to the low price charged — friction/value mismatch.
- Update: Koerner is now more bullish on this idea because AI can now write the listing description and remove most of that remaining friction (his estimate: could remove ~80% of the friction).

## "Rocking chair" idea-sourcing framework (not a business, but a technique)
- Framework for generating business/product ideas: browse BizBuySell listings (including logged-out/incognito, which surfaces the most-clicked "hot" listings) to see real financials/margins of small businesses for sale.
- Case study: found an e-commerce business selling wooden rocking horses/chairs with ~70% margins, 100% customer acquisition via Facebook ads. Reverse-engineered by building a similar-audience/similar-price-point site (stock photos from Google Images), running paid Facebook ads to validate demand, then refunding any actual purchases (treating it as a demand test, not a real sale).
- General technique: pull the executive summary/financials from BizBuySell listings and look for "asymmetric" opportunities — outsized margins or clear demand > supply signals — even if you don't buy that specific business.

## The Koerner Office podcast/newsletter (content business)
- Newsletter running ~1 year (55-56 editions) at time of recording; podcast launched Feb 2024.
- YouTube monetization: ~$50-$150/day (~$2,000-$3,000/month) after ~3 months of monetization eligibility.
- Reinvests revenue into equipment/editing rather than take-home pay; runs on a cheap setup ($300 camera, $59 Amazon lighting).
- Explicitly turns down nearly all sponsorship/ad offers to preserve audience trust/attention.
- Replicable pattern: build in public, talk about what you're doing consistently; monetization compounds slowly through content quality and consistency.

## Consulting/advising
- Advises business owners weekly for a monthly retainer fee; helps with growth/operations.
- Explicit caveat: personally low-energy/unsatisfying for him relative to building his own businesses, because his real passion is the "zero to one" phase; turns down most advising opportunities despite it being a working revenue source.

## Speed to Ship (internal shipping-rate-shopping software)
- Built in-house (by his brother-in-law) originally for a 3PL business and later Texas Snacks (his resale business): scans a package, weighs it, and picks the optimal carrier (UPS/FedEx/DHL/USPS) factoring in both actual weight and dimensional weight, saving "thousands of dollars a month" on shipping.
- Never externally commercialized despite competitors now selling similar SaaS tools.
- Lesson: internal tools built to solve your own operational pain point are a potential SaaS product if other businesses share the same pain.

## Mexican Baseball merch/streaming (undeveloped idea)
- A LinkedIn contact connected to Mexican baseball team owners pitched building a centralized "MLB.com"-style e-commerce/merch hub for Mexican baseball (2nd most popular sport in Mexico, fast-growing), plus a possible streaming tie-in. Still in progress/unlaunched as of this recording — flagged as opportunistic ("find a well-connected person in a fast-growing niche sport/market and centralize its commerce").

## Better Parks (RV park turnaround entity, separate from Blue Metric)
- Entity formed with a partner to buy/turn around a specific RV park in Waco, TX (park-owned homes converted to tenant-owned, raised rents, installed cameras).
- Originally considered offering this turnaround service as consulting to other RV park owners for a flat fee or equity stake — never executed.
- Caveat: ran into city/regulatory issues with the Waco park and is trying to sell it, "front and center" disclosing the issues to the buyer, taking a valuation hit as a result.

## Busy Juice (DIY caffeinated water-additive beverage)
- Personal-need-driven product: wanted a gallon of water/day with caffeine but without artificial dyes (was using a Mio-style drink mix loaded with Red Dye 40).
- Made his own 3-ingredient version; has a landing page (busyjuice.com) but nothing for sale yet — shelved due to lack of experience in physical food product manufacturing.
- Stated rationale for entering a crowded beverage category anyway: universal daily need (everyone drinks liquids) means even a "decent" product in a saturated category can find buyers.

## Sunbeam ABA → Sunbeam Pause (attempted autism clinic acquisition, pivoted to pet cremation)
- Attempted to acquire an autism therapy clinic in Tucson, AZ with two partners; spent ~$12,000 on due-diligence/paperwork, flew out, but backed out after red flags (seller wouldn't disclose full financials).
- A tweet about the planned acquisition got 500k views and strong interest from accredited investors (raise was essentially ready) — deal died anyway when diligence turned up problems; illustrates that capital/interest isn't the bottleneck, deal quality is.
- Pivoted the entity/energy into Sunbeam Pause, a pet cremation logistics business in DFW: they operate a refrigerated van, pick up deceased pets (mostly dogs, some cats/hamsters) from veterinary clinics, and deliver them to a cremation facility — essentially a logistics middleman between vet clinics and a cremation provider.
- Described as low operational complexity and profitable from day one; growth focus is on acquiring more veterinary clinic customers.
