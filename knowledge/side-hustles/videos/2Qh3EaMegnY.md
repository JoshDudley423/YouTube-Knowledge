# These Genius Business Ideas Are Hidden All Over America
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2Qh3EaMegnY | Published: 2025-08-15

Chris Koerner and Nik (Nikonomics Podcast) discuss a series of real, observed "hidden in plain sight" business models across the US.

## Idea 1: "Weddings as a Service" (no-venue destination wedding coordination)
- Real example: Gulf Beach Weddings (Fort Myers, FL) started with $20,000, now does ~$3M/year.
- Model: no owned venue — the operator has deep local knowledge of which beaches are legally permitted for weddings, which have the best sunset angle, least seaweed/wind, etc.
- **Package/pricing**: $1,500-$3,500 per wedding package (rentals: ~100 chairs, ~100 photos, wedding arch, officiant/vows, etc.); does 100+ weddings/year at roughly $3,000 average → ~$3M/year revenue.
- Context stat cited: outdoor weddings now make up ~two-thirds of all US weddings, up from ~40% a decade ago — cited as driving related niches like luxury portable wedding-trailer bathroom rentals.
- **Generalization**: replicable in any state with a well-known destination draw (mountains, lakes, canyons, national/state parks) by becoming the local "white glove" fixer who knows logistics, permits, and timing — same model could extend to bachelor/bachelorette trip planning in destinations like Las Vegas.

## Idea 2: Reselling merchandise from beloved regional tourist attractions that lack e-commerce ("Texas Snacks" model)
- Origin case study (context, not this video's main subject): Texas Snacks / Chris Turner — built a resale business around Buc-ee's (a cult-following Texas gas station/travel chain) by buying merchandise at full retail in-store (no wholesale relationship) and reselling online at scale — hundreds of thousands of units sold, full warehouse operation.
- **New example identified**: Big Texan Steak Ranch (Amarillo, TX) — famous for a free 72oz steak if you finish it. Gets ~500,000 visitors/year (~75% tourists); has attached RV park and motel; sells ~250,000 t-shirts/year (mostly in-store); estimated single-location revenue of $16-20M/year, with speaker's overall estimate (including merch/RV/motel) at $30-40M as a full business — one of the highest-grossing single-location restaurants in the US. (Caveat: they discovered mid-episode that Big Texan actually already sells merchandise online, invalidating this specific target.)
- **Better example found**: Lambert's Café ("Home of the Throwed Rolls" — servers literally throw dinner rolls to customers), locations in Foley, AL and Branson, MO (possibly a third); ~$25/person family-style dining, lines out the door; reportedly "tens of millions of dollars per year per location"; sells branded t-shirts and merch but does NOT sell online.
- **Proposed execution model**: approach the target restaurant/attraction with a 3PL (third-party logistics)-style pitch — "put it on your website, we'll handle all on-demand printing and fulfillment, essentially a licensing arrangement" — no need for the restaurant to build any infrastructure themselves.
- **Sourcing method suggested**: use an AI "deep research" prompt to systematically identify other beloved regional/tourist attractions (restaurants, roadside attractions) across the US that have strong brand loyalty/tourist traffic but no e-commerce presence.
- Other examples referenced in passing: Winslow, AZ's "Standin' on the Corner" park/crater attraction (privately owned); Mayberry-themed attraction (Andy Griffith Show filming location) offering a $40 branded car ride experience.

## Idea 3: Themed prop/vehicle rental tied to famous movies/TV shows, filmed-location tourism
- Real example: an entrepreneur bought the "Uncle Rico" van from Napoleon Dynamite on eBay for $12,000 and now rents it out as a standalone business; same owner also owns and rents out a DeLorean (Back to the Future).
- Related resource named: propstore.com — marketplace to buy movie/TV props for exactly this kind of monetization.
- Applied to Breaking Bad (filmed in Albuquerque, NM): the show's three iconic vehicles (Pontiac Aztek, station wagon, the RV) could be bought/replicated and rented, or used as the basis for a themed tour business ("Breaking Bad tour of Albuquerque") requiring essentially zero overhead — "you just need knowledge, you just need to watch the series."
- The actual "Breaking Bad house" sold for $4M in 2025 despite a fair-market-value of only ~$500-600K (pure attention/tourism premium); the prior owners had complained for years about tourists throwing pizzas on the roof (referencing the show) instead of monetizing the attention (e.g., paid guided tours, since the house is not in an HOA).
- **Roof/attention-based advertising analog cited**: a separate guest interviewed on the podcast (over a year prior) lives directly in LAX's flight path and rents his rooftop for advertising to businesses — tracked via a dedicated link, reportedly worth "hundreds of thousands of dollars a year."
- **General framework proposed**: identify a state-specific pop-culture or landmark association, then find or create a monetizable prop/experience/tour tied to it (movie props, themed glamping sites, guided tours, licensed advertising space).

## Idea 4: Immersive/experiential "adventure" businesses (tank driving, live-fire ranges)
- Real examples: DriveATank.com (Minnesota) and a similarly named/branded competitor DriveTank.com (Texas) — rent time driving a tank, crushing cars ($850 add-on cited for a "double car crush"), and firing various weapons (including a 50-cal, described "unlimited" packages) under one roof; a comparable Orlando-based competitor has appeared in Mr. Beast and Dude Perfect content and is judged to be much larger as a result.
- Comparison data point: DriveATank's own YouTube channel had only ~85 subscribers/7 videos despite being founded in 2012 — cited as a clear case of a real, differentiated business under-leveraging free organic content/video marketing; the competitor with celebrity-creator exposure has 116,000 Instagram followers vs. 13,000 YouTube.
- **Suggested growth-hack pitch for an entrepreneur**: approach an underexposed business like this and offer to film/manage a YouTube channel for free customer-acquisition value, in exchange for ownership of the channel itself — framed as a path to a "$100K/month YouTube channel" built on someone else's existing (undermarketed) real-world business.
- Referenced comparable business (heavy equipment "play" experience for kids near a mall in Houston) reportedly generating "over $1 million a year of net profit per location" — used to argue similar adult-oriented "play war"/experiential businesses have real unit economics.
- Rough cost cited: a functional used tank (a T-72, unverified/uncited source) estimated around $50,000.

## Idea 5: "Goats as a Service" — livestock brush-clearing/wildfire-prevention middleman
- Premise: goats (unlike cattle) preferentially eat underbrush/weeds rather than grass, making them effective, fast land clearers when fenced onto overgrown lots.
- **Numbers**: fire departments/municipalities reportedly pay ~$500-$1,000/acre for goat-based brush clearing, vs. an estimated ~$28,000/acre for manual clearing by hand (cited source: CalMatters.org, a California nonprofit news outlet) — framed as a massive cost arbitrage.
- Cited real examples: Nevada City, CA ran a public "GoatFundMe" campaign, raising $30,000 to acquire goats for wildfire-fuel reduction; Oakland, CA reportedly spends ~$500,000/year on goat grazing services.
- Wildfire-prevention use case is concentrated in California, but general land-clearing/brush-clearing demand for goats exists nationwide.
- **Proposed business model — pure middleman, no owned goats required**: source goats by renting/borrowing them directly from local goat farmers (validated via a live Facebook Marketplace search for "Boer goat" — found many active goat farms within a 90-mile radius of Dallas, most of which are not currently monetizing grazing services); use the farmer's own animal trailer to transport goats to the job site, fence them temporarily, return them after the job. Charge the land clearing customer ~$500/acre; pay the goat owner a smaller fee for the loan of the animals.
- **Suggested customer acquisition (validated playbook borrowed from a comparable business)**: reference case — a forestry mulching business owner (Cincinnati) uses Meta/Facebook ads exclusively, spends ~$23 per lead, closes 49% of leads, with an average job size of $3,100 (i.e., ~$46 ad spend per $3,100 customer acquired); his targeting approach: run ads to zip codes containing large rural land tracts (targeting people 35+ who show interest in "Tractor Supply" as an interest/behavior signal, a proxy for landowners). Suggested applying the identical Facebook-ads playbook (same targeting logic) to a goat-grazing business, with the added benefit that "goat munches down a tree" video content is likely to spread even more virally than mulching-machine content.
- Government/municipal contracts (e wildfire prevention) are noted as a slower sales cycle due to competitive-bidding requirements — the speakers recommend skipping government contracts initially and instead going direct to private landowners via paid social ads.

## Idea 6: "50 states in 50 days" personal business-launch challenge (Koerner's own announced project, credited to "Kamal")
- Concept: Koerner plans to travel with his family through all 50 US states in 50 days (as they previously did for a 50-states-in-60-days family trip) and attempt to start a small, low-cost, low-barrier "business" (target: generate at least $1 in revenue) in each state within about 8 hours, then give the business/revenue away to a local person to keep running.
- Example prior proof point cited: Koerner started a business "in 56 minutes live on YouTube" (referring to the garage-door AI voice agent story recounted in a separate episode, not detailed here).
- Idea sourcing approach discussed: tailor each state's micro-business idea to something locally unique (e.g., a themed pop-up near a specific national park entry point; "shed hunting" for elk antlers as a real, lucrative seasonal activity in Wyoming/Montana that could be turned into a resale business) rather than repeating one generic idea (e.g. Facebook Marketplace flipping) in every state, on the reasoning that uniqueness drives more viewer/audience interest per state.
- Distribution/marketing inspiration explicitly cited: YouTuber Ryan Trahan's real "50 states in 50 days" charity series (raising money for St. Jude's, offering paid shoutouts for $5,000+ donations, with each video getting 4-10 million views) — cited concrete sponsor-ROI data points from that series: an electric bike company donated $100,000 (plus $10,000/day incentive) in exchange for exposure across an estimated 250 million total series views, calculated by the speakers as an extremely cheap CPM (~$10 CPM) compared to traditional media (a cited comparison: a local carpet cleaning business owner stopped radio ad spend after seeing no measurable ROI).
- Suggested extension ideas discussed but not committed to: partnering with local guest entrepreneurs in each state (pitch-to-launch model) submitted via an open application process, or exclusively recruiting from Koerner's own paid community (TKOwners) to reduce flake risk; reaching out to state tourism boards for potential funding/co-marketing since tourism boards often have dedicated marketing budgets; pre-selling RV rental bookings live during the trip as a "negative cash conversion cycle" proof-of-demand stunt for a hypothetical RV rental business.

## Idea 7: Unclaimed/abandoned asset liquidation and resale (the "Unclaimed Baggage" model)
- Case study: Unclaimed Baggage Center (Scottsboro, AL) — the only entity in the US licensed to purchase and resell unclaimed airline luggage after a 90-day unclaimed period; founded 1970 by Doyle Owens with a $300 loan and a borrowed pickup truck; family-owned since 1995 (son Brian Owens and wife Sharon).
- **Numbers**: receives 5,000-7,000 pieces of luggage per day; operates out of a 50,000 sq ft facility; roughly splits inventory into thirds — discarded as trash, donated to charity, and resold; over 1 million shoppers/year; notable resold items have included Rolexes and a reported 40-carat emerald; prices are NOT deep-discount despite the "unclaimed" framing — because of captive tourist demand, they can price close to (or above) typical thrift-store levels.
- **Generalized framework proposed**: identify other categories of "left-behind/abandoned/discarded assets" with a similarly monopolistic or underexploited resale channel. Brainstormed (unvalidated) analogs discussed: school lost-and-found clothing/items thrown out at semester end (proposed: a "lost and found as a service" software or liquidation service for schools); items left behind in rental cars; items left in hotel rooms; items left in Airbnbs (suggested approach: contact large-scale Airbnb property management companies and offer to buy their left-behind items in bulk at a discount, since their only current alternatives are discarding them or ad hoc reselling).

## Meta-commentary on paid customer acquisition (Facebook/Meta ads for home services)
- Cited Meta's own reported quarterly results as context: ad revenue up 21% YoY, ad impressions up 11% YoY, average ad price up 9%, 3.5 billion daily active people across Facebook/Instagram/WhatsApp, with AI-driven ad-conversion improvements of +5% (Instagram) and +3% (Facebook).
- Discussion of why some home-service business owners wrongly conclude "Facebook doesn't work": often due to under-testing (one bad agency experience, no real AB testing, wrong creative format) rather than the channel itself failing — analogized to writing off golf entirely after one bad round.
- Recommended tactical differences: Google Ads suit home services better for immediate high-intent conversion (searcher is ready to buy now); Facebook/Meta ads work better as brand-awareness/retargeting (planting seeds, requiring an estimated ~7 touches before conversion) — recommend video ad creative (never static images) and using instant lead-gen forms; pixel-based retargeting of website visitors is called out as one of the most effective use cases.
- Founder-run/founder-appearing Facebook ads reportedly convert better than generic agency-produced ads for local home-service businesses.
- Suggested approach to hiring a marketing agency: learn the channel yourself first before hiring an agency (so you can evaluate their work), or explicitly tell a prospective agency upfront "my goal is to learn this and fire you in 3 months, please Loom-record everything you do" — framed as a legitimate way to fast-track learning while still getting paid work done, acknowledging some agencies will decline this arrangement but many will still accept it.
