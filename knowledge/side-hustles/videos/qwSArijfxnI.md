# How He Started a $72,000/Month Event Venue Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qwSArijfxnI | Published: 2024-11-21

Interview with Alex Nelson ("the wedding venue guy" on Twitter), owner of The Midnight Gem, a modern-design wedding venue in Swisher, Iowa (between Cedar Rapids and Iowa City).

## Origin and validation
- Idea originated from being wedding *clients* themselves (married 2018) and noticing local venues booked out 1.5-2 years in advance, charging $6,000-$7,000+/night — realized it was "a full-on operating business," not just a real estate rental play.
- Validation method before committing real capital: called every wedding venue in the target area, posing as a prospective couple, to ask pricing and how far out they were booked. Explicitly recommended as a replicable market-research technique — "not many businesses where the market research is so binary" (venue owners get this question every day, so it doesn't raise suspicion) — check whether venues book Fridays/Saturdays only or also Sundays, current pricing, and how full their calendar is.
- No formal market study — largely gut feeling plus being embedded in the target demographic (same age/cohort as their own client base) and identifying a design gap (surrounding venues were all "farmhouse/barn" style; they built an all-black modern building to differentiate).

## Concrete numbers
- Total all-in cost to build and open: ~$2.9M (land, building, furniture, fixtures, equipment).
- Timeline: land under contract Dec 2020, officially opened Oct 2022 (~2 years); good chunk of that delay was construction/price escalation (pricing quotes moved from $1.5M to $2.1M over the build process due to COVID-era cost inflation).
- Financing: mostly self-funded — founders worked multiple full-time remote jobs and lived frugally to save; used an SBA loan (10% down); learned that money spent on pre-construction consulting/design/renderings could be rolled into the equity counted toward the SBA down payment. Also received $80,000 total from both sets of parents ($40k each) once demand was clearly proven (near the end of the process, not as seed capital).
- Pre-opening demand signal: booked almost 100 weddings before construction was finished, based on architectural renderings alone (roughly half of bookings were sold purely from renderings, before a walkable structure existed) — collected deposits of $5,500 (Fridays) to $6,500 (Saturdays), roughly 50% down, totaling ~$300,000 in deposits before the doors opened.
- Revenue ramp: 2022 (partial year) ~14 weddings, roughly $150k revenue. First full year (2023): ~$780,000 revenue (as best transcribed), NOI (excluding debt service) ~$330,000 — roughly 40% net margin excluding financing costs. Projected 2024: ~$850,000-$900,000 (reinvesting into the building and hiring more staff, expected to normalize/improve margin the following year).
- Revenue mix: alcohol sales account for ~40% of total revenue; without alcohol sales, the business "probably wouldn't be profitable" at its current staffing/service level.
- Liquor license cost in their state (Iowa): ~$900/year — described as cheap; contrasted with states like California where liquor licenses are capped/auctioned and expensive. Suggests venues in restrictive states instead partner with a caterer who already holds a liquor license for a referral fee/revenue share (10-20% of the caterer's related revenue) instead of holding a license directly.
- Refund/make-good rate: roughly 1-3% of weddings require a meaningful refund/compensation due to an operational mistake on the day (sizable payouts, since a wedding day can't be redone) — described as emotionally costly even at a low frequency.

## Structural business model notes
- Framed as a genuine "operating business," not just a real-estate play — the owner explicitly wavers between describing it in real estate terms (NOI) vs. business terms (EBITDA) depending on audience, but leans toward viewing it as more of a full operating business over time.
- Scaling ceiling: fundamentally date-constrained (fixed number of Fridays/Saturdays per year) — can't scale revenue 5x without building an entirely new physical venue, which requires the same multi-million-dollar, multi-year buildout again.
- Ancillary/upsell revenue ideas discussed: on-site overnight accommodations (cabins) would let a venue capture rehearsal-dinner-night and Sunday-brunch/second-day revenue in addition to the core wedding day, and would justify a rental premium — the guest's own county zoning restrictions currently block him from adding short-term rental units next to his venue.

## Adjacent/tangential business ideas discussed
- **Third-party venue management model**: manage the wedding-business operations of an already-existing beautiful property (a barn, estate, or other space not currently used for weddings) on a per-event fee/profit-share basis (e.g., "$2,000 per wedding hosted") rather than owning the real estate outright — property owners often don't want to run a wedding-business operation themselves. Caveat: still generally requires capital investment to make a space "wedding-ready" (bridal suite, groom's suite, ceremony space, weather contingency/plan-B space) — simply having square footage that can hold 300 people doesn't mean you can charge premium per-night rates; premium pricing depends on supporting the full day (typically 8am-midnight, hair/makeup prep through reception).
- **Micro-venue / house-to-venue conversion**: converting a large residential property (5-6 bedrooms, acreage, near a metro area) into a wedding venue — cites a real example of an Airbnb-listed house near Dallas renting for ~$800/night, with about half of renters booking it specifically for weddings. Feasibility factors: zoning/permitting (biggest hurdle), parking capacity, weather (outdoor-reliant markets like Texas are easier than Iowa), and typically still needing added infrastructure (a permanent tent or an interior space that holds 100+ people) to reach venue-level per-night pricing rather than simple house-rental pricing. Suggested conversion approach for a 5-6BR house: dedicate 1-2 rooms as bridal/groom suites (or build a separate shed as a groom's suite), keep remaining bedrooms as overnight lodging for the wedding party, and add an outdoor ceremony structure (gazebo/pavilion) plus tent rental for the reception.
- **Wedding rental/vendor niches** (lower capital, faster to start than owning a venue): trending rental items/services cited as having real demand — decorative arch rentals, luxury portable restroom trailers ("Beer Barrow"/"Beer Donkey"-style units), coffee carts/trucks, food trucks (increasingly used as full caterers, not just late-night snacks), photo booths/360-degree spin booths, wedding decor rental inventory. Framed as accessible entry points: can start with a single rental item/piece of equipment and grow the inventory over time, similar in spirit to a home-service business with low startup capital.
- **High-volume event/logistics businesses tangential to weddings**: cited example of a local party bus company that started ~1.5 years prior and scaled to 30 buses running ~30 events/night — illustrating that some wedding-adjacent vendors can do far higher transaction volume per night than a fixed-location venue (which is limited to one event per date).
- **Themed recurring ticketed events hosted at the venue on non-wedding nights** (to monetize otherwise-idle inventory/space): cites real examples of both traveling "Candlelight Concerts" (renting the venue space for ~$150-ish nightly fee to run ticketed themed concert events, selling ~200 tickets at ~$60/each, sometimes running two shows/night) and "Bingo Loco" (upscale themed bingo events) as proof that "take a common event and add a stylistic twist" can generate outsized revenue (cited estimate: $15,000-$30,000 in revenue from a single one-night event without needing large attendance, or a national touring operator doing ~400 events/year at ~$30,000/night). Alex has hosted third-party touring events like this at his own venue (mostly a pure space-rental deal with minimal operational involvement) and considers building an in-house version of this concept (rather than just renting space to outside touring companies) as a viable "Plan B" if core wedding demand ever declined, since it isn't constrained to Friday/Saturday wedding dates the way weddings are.

## Caveats
- Direct venue acquisition (buying an existing venue rather than building) is described as currently difficult — sellers often price using inflated/unrealistic combined real-estate-plus-business valuations that don't match what the actual business cash flow can support (e.g., "the business can't generate enough to justify paying for this $5M building").
- Zoning and short-term-rental/event-space permitting are significant, sometimes prohibitive obstacles for the house-conversion or micro-resort approaches.
- Business carries high emotional/reputational stakes — a bad wedding day cannot be redone, unlike almost any other service business; described as "one phone call away" from a very bad outcome, unlike a typical job.
- Trend risk: recurring themed-event concepts (Candlelight Concerts, Bingo Loco) succeed partly because they're novel; sustaining interest requires continually finding new concepts, and repeating similar events indefinitely may eventually see declining novelty/ticket sales (untested concern raised, not something Alex has fully hit yet).
