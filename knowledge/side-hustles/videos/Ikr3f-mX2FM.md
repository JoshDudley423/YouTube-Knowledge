# Why THESE Are the Best Businesses for Beginners
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ikr3f-mX2FM | Published: 2025-02-20

Half-monologue (Chris's thesis on home service businesses), half-interview with Doug Steel, owner of Park Cities Power Wash (Dallas-Fort Worth pressure washing / window cleaning / Christmas lights / gutter cleaning company).

## The business idea: home service businesses generally

Why Chris favors home services as a beginner-friendly category:
- **Binary outcomes**: low-complexity, "cut and dry" jobs (e.g., tree trimming: cut down the tree, remove it, get a 5-star review) vs. more judgment-heavy work like custom home building/remodeling — easier to execute consistently and get right the first time.
- **High demand + can be made year-round**: pair a seasonal service (e.g., Christmas lights) with a complementary seasonal one (e.g., landscaping) to smooth revenue across the year.
- **Recurring revenue potential**: things keep breaking; even non-obviously-recurring services (pressure washing, not just lawn mowing) tend to generate repeat/follow-on work.
- **Preferred ticket size**: $500-$5,000 per job, described as a high-ticket, high-margin sweet spot.
- **Low barrier to entry — a double-edged trait**: means lots of competitors, but most are unsophisticated (no real website, don't answer their phone) — a real online presence and basic marketing sophistication can outrank them ("selection bias" — you only notice the competitors who rank on Google, not the much larger pool of unsophisticated operators who don't).
- **Recession resistant, easy to upsell, and scalable.**
- **Demand-testing method** (referenced from an earlier stump-grinding experiment): call 10-20 local operators in a given home-service niche and see how many answer the phone, what they quote, and how booked out they are — an hour of this per niche across 5 niches tells you a lot about local competitiveness before committing.
- **Macro argument for home services vs. chasing "shiny" internet/AI/crypto businesses**: population (335M in the US, 8B globally) and the number of homes keep growing while the proportion of entrepreneurs choosing home services over internet-native businesses has shrunk — pushing prices up due to supply/demand mismatch. Cited anecdote: a 1950s-era "Dirty Jobs" episode showed septic tank pumping priced at $70/job; the same service costs roughly 10x that today, "at least."

## Case study: Doug Steel / Park Cities Power Wash

- **Origin**: sold a hood-cleaning business in Arizona ~12 years before this recording; got bored, started pressure washing informally for neighbors, then formalized into a business starting September 2016.
- **Getting the first customer**: organic — a neighborhood contact (possibly via early website). First job: pressure washing a front walk, front door area, back patio/pool deck.
- **Early pricing method**: backed into an hourly target (~$100/hour, based on what he'd eventually need to pay an employee) and estimated job duration to set price — did not use square-footage-based pricing (common in the industry) because it didn't account well for job-specific complications (e.g., a fancy house with brand-new copper gutters needing extra care).
- **Customer acquisition in the first 5 years**: almost entirely word of mouth + a basic website; heavily emphasized collecting reviews early, before online reviews became a mainstream growth lever. Did very little paid marketing.
- **Revenue trajectory** (approximate, self-reported): Year 1 partial/weekends ~$75,000 → then roughly doubled annually for 4-5 years: ~$150k → ~$300k → ~$450k → ~$550-600k, then growth leveled off. In the video's year, expecting ~$800-900k after an acquisition of another company.
- **Service-line expansion timeline**: pressure/soft washing was the core for the first 5 years (gutter cleaning included early on, low demand). Added **window cleaning in 2021** (5 years in) after noticing pressure washing left customers' windows spotted — trained via an industry convention session on water-fed pole systems (pure-water-filter poles with brushes) plus traditional squeegee technique, and hired someone with existing window-cleaning experience to help train the team. Added **Christmas lights ~2 years before this interview**, driven by customer requests and the rise of easy-to-source LED light products; trained by a local distributor/training company (in July, in 110°F heat) that also taught pricing and sourcing.
- **Full current service lineup**: power washing, soft washing, window washing, Christmas lights, solar panel cleaning, gutter cleaning, graffiti removal; roof cleaning offered selectively (sometimes requires renting a lift).
- **Regional variation in what to specialize in**: roof cleaning and general washing demand is bigger near the coast/humid areas (Florida, Houston) due to algae growth. Window cleaning for storefronts is described as a strong, low-risk line since it's a near-automatic recurring need for many businesses.
- **Getting first customers for a single-service-line specialist** (e.g., storefront-only window washing, or solar-panel-only cleaning): cold emailing/cold texting/direct outreach recommended. For solar panel cleaning specifically: adoption in the DFW area is a "very low percentage" niche, so targeted lead generation (a purchased list, or an AI/Google Earth tool that identifies which houses actually have solar panels) is recommended over broad direct mail to a whole zip code.
- **Margins**: window cleaning has the highest margin of Doug's service lines (mostly labor cost, can be priced somewhat competitively while still profitable); pressure washing margins are also good.
- **Pricing/costing benchmarks he tracks**: aims to keep labor at under 30% of a job's cost; job costing tracks technician hours, burden/taxes, chemicals used, and machine hours (proxy for fuel cost). Net margin target: starting solo operators will see gross ≈ net; as the business scales (multiple trucks, a shop, admin/production managers, bleach delivered by the truckload), overhead grows and compresses net margin — a 20% net margin the owner can take home is called "a pretty good number" at scale (compares to large HVAC/plumbing/electrical franchises that often run ~5% net but at much larger revenue volumes).
- **COVID tailwind**: business "blew up" during COVID because home-service/construction workers were among the few people allowed to keep working, and people were home more, increasing demand — an unusually favorable period that may not repeat.
- **Emerging opportunity flagged**: permanent architectural LED lighting (brand example: "Jellyfish Lighting" — app-controlled, color-changing, weather-resistant permanent lights installed in soffits/eaves, usable for Christmas, Valentine's Day, Easter, or plain warm white year-round). Priced by linear foot (exact pricing not specified by Doug — he doesn't install them yet) and typically requires an electrician for permanent wiring. Suggested acquisition tactic floated by the host: canvass door-to-door in November/December specifically to houses already displaying (temporary) Christmas lights — those homeowners have already demonstrated they value the aesthetic — and pitch them on switching to permanent lighting as a high-ticket (non-recurring) upsell.

## Advice for someone starting a home service business

- Don't overspend on "guru" training programs, expensive rigs, or all the fanciest equipment starting out (Doug is explicitly skeptical of high-priced pressure-washing training/rig-selling programs). Also don't go too cheap (e.g., a basic Home Depot consumer power washer) — a middle path recommended: a decent commercial-grade unit, a few thousand dollars, portable enough to run out of a pickup truck.
- DIY chemical mixing starting out: a 2-gallon sprayer with pool chlorine from a hardware store works to start (more expensive per unit than bulk delivery, which larger operators eventually switch to); rough dilution guidance given — up to 50/50 bleach-to-water for tough flatwork, much weaker (roughly a quarter-to-half gallon of bleach per 1.5 gallons of water) for house siding, which is more delicate.
- Insurance: shop around via industry Facebook groups for broker recommendations; described as inexpensive when starting out. Cautionary note — a formerly dominant insurer in the space (JD Walters) changed ownership, audited/re-priced policies after years without rate increases, and some existing policyholders either faced steep increases or lost coverage entirely — a reminder not to assume your current insurer/rate is stable long-term.
- Prioritize getting a brand established and visible over taking on every large project immediately; you don't need to spend tens of thousands of dollars to get started.

## Caveats / risks

- Numbers from Doug are explicitly self-reported/approximate ("I think," "maybe," "we'll say") rather than precise financial statements.
- COVID-era growth is flagged as a uniquely favorable period unlikely to repeat, and current competition ("these days I'm sure it's different") is higher than when Doug started in 2016.
- Solar panel cleaning demand is described as low-incidence/niche in his specific market — success depends heavily on being able to actually identify and target the small subset of homes with solar panels.
- Permanent lighting (Jellyfish-style) segment is discussed as an opportunity Doug does not currently operate in — his cost/pricing comments there are secondhand/approximate, not from direct operating experience.
- General warning about industry "gurus" charging significant fees for training/rigs that may not be necessary for a beginner.
