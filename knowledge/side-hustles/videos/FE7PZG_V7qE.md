# The Simplest Business in a Box Anyone Can Start
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FE7PZG_V7qE | Published: 2026-05-08

Interview with Marc Hyman (lawyer by background, sunburstnitrocoffee.com) about a mobile iced/nitro coffee cart business built from a gutted Home Depot tool cart, designed as a low-cost "business in a box" he sets other people up in (divorced moms, military spouses, disabled veterans, immigrant visa clients).

## The business idea
- Sell iced coffee (and nitro coffee) from a converted Home Depot Husky tool cart at events, festivals, farmers markets, car washes, gas stations, night markets — instead of an expensive coffee truck or espresso machine setup.
- Key insight: after ~9am, ~95% of coffee sales are sweetened/iced drinks where the underlying coffee quality doesn't matter once cream/syrup is added — so there's no need for a $30,000 espresso machine, grinder, and milk frother. Pre-brewed coffee (bought in bulk, shelf-stable for 3–6 months) delivered via keg or "bag-in-box" (BIB, like restaurant syrup) works just as well and tastes the same once served over ice.
- Nitro option: use a nitrogen tank (rather than CO2) to dispense, producing smaller, longer-lasting bubbles and a fizzier mouthfeel — trendy ("nitro brew" like at Starbucks).
- Prior business he ran the same way: a mobile "tap truck" serving beer at weddings/events, evolved into repurposed armoires/furniture fitted with beer taps (cheaper, more portable, more visually appealing at weddings than a truck, and could be dropped off unattended, e.g. at a frat house).

## Concrete numbers
- Cart build cost: ~$2,500–$3,000 total ("under a thousand bucks" was cited elsewhere in the episode for a bare-bones build): $600 tool cart, $200 tap/dispensing system (mixes/calibrates gas and coffee — the most expensive component), nitro tank a few hundred dollars once (refill ~$30 every 2 months), plus ~$500 if paying someone to assemble it.
- Cost per cup: ~$1.50 coffee + ~$0.30 cup/lid/straw + ice (~$3/25 lb bag, sometimes given free by gas stations to draw in bulk buyers) ≈ ~$3/cup all-in.
- Sells 24 oz cups for $8.50 (or a smaller cup for $6.50 when undercutting); roughly $5 profit per cup — described as not the highest margin business, but very easy to run and universally wanted.
- Throughput: pours in ~30 seconds vs. 3–5 minutes for a traditional espresso-based coffee cart/truck; can serve ~1,000 people/day with 3 people working the cart.
- Revenue/profit by event size:
  - Small event (~80 people): ~$400 net profit for the day.
  - Larger local festival (2,000–5,000 attendees, e.g. an Avocado/Lemon/German/Israeli festival): $800–$1,200 net.
  - Best-ever event: a 2-day, 100°F festival, $8,000 net profit total — beat a $150,000 pizza-truck competitor parked next to him (his all-in setup cost under $1,000).
  - Typical hourly profit range: roughly $100–$400/hour depending on event size and location quality.
  - Car washes (the full-service, hand-wash type with 15-20 staff, not automatic tunnels): tested and performing very well — $500–$600 net in a single afternoon shift (11am–4pm) "just for showing up." One car wash owner he met washes 500-1,000 cars per weekend per location and owns 25 locations in LA.
  - Working day length: 4–8 hours, typically 10am–4/5pm (coffee demand drops off after ~6pm; some switch to decaf or other drinks in the evening at night markets).
  - Downside risk if a location bombs: minimal — just gas money and whatever site fee was paid (~$150); unsold coffee doesn't spoil, ice/cups are reusable/storable. He cites one Tractor Supply pop-up that only did $100 in sales.
- Revenue-share model when he sets someone else up: he fronts the cart, product sourcing, and location; operator runs it; they split profit roughly 30/70 or negotiated depending on who supplies what (truck, labor, etc.). Two of his early operators (a woman, and an E2-visa client) each cleared about $400 net for a day's work.

## Process / how to get set up
- Build: buy a Husky tool cart at Home Depot, pay a metal shop to cut out the internal guts, keep the sturdy shell/wheels/lid.
- Sourcing product: found a local coffee-keg supplier (originally targeting restaurants) and switched from heavy 5-gallon steel kegs (~40 lb, hard for a smaller-framed operator to handle) to bag-in-box (BIB) delivery — lighter, still gas-pump dispensed, no taste difference once iced/flavored.
- Location strategy ("location, location, location" — both which event, and where within the event):
  - Best economics: places with an inherently captive, large, recurring crowd and no or few nearby food/drink competitors — car washes, gas stations, air shows, remote/rural festivals, EV/supercharger stations (his suggestion).
  - Farmers markets: recurring weekly commitment; slow to build trust/regulars but reliable once established.
  - Counterintuitive finding: being co-located with other food/beverage vendors (a "food court" effect) outperforms being the lone vendor off in a merchandise-only area — people in "shopping mode" aren't thinking about food, but people already near food vendors are primed to buy. Compares this to auto malls and fast-food strip clusters.
  - Arrive early to scout foot-traffic flow before committing to a spot.
  - Signage matters a lot: initial "nitro coffee" / fancy signage confused people; switched to a simple, giant "ICED COFFEE" sign. Prints large PVC signage via FedEx Office for about $100 each; treats sign design as a fast, cheap iteration loop.
  - How he gets access to venues/host sites: avoids "cold front-door" asks (e.g., asking a gas station "what will you charge me"); instead builds relationships (via referrals, past favors, LinkedIn, conferences) so the venue owner invites him in, often framing it as helping an at-risk operator (divorced mom, veteran, military spouse) rather than a straight commercial pitch.
  - Some events/venues charge a booth fee ($150–$500 depending on the event); others (an outdoor "night market" model common in Hispanic/Chinese immigrant communities, or a favor-based relationship with an event promoter) charge little or nothing, or offer preferential access due to an existing relationship.
- Payment/tracking: virtually all payment now goes through the operator's Stripe or Square account (very few cash transactions even among younger/broader demographics); revenue splits are tracked and settled from there — no dedicated POS software, largely honor-system among his partner-operators.

## Caveats / risks
- Permitting varies by situation: operating inside an enclosed space that's technically part of another business (e.g., a car wash waiting room) may avoid street-vending permit rules; setting up on a public thoroughfare draws the most regulatory scrutiny and may require a proper mobile vendor/catering permit. He admits he initially operated without worrying about permits ("cowboying it") and is now moving toward full compliance/proper signage/wrap as he scales.
- Margin isn't huge per cup (~$5 on an $8.50 cup after ~$3 cost) — the appeal is volume, speed of service, and low overhead/capital risk, not high per-unit margin.
- He frames this explicitly as gig/side-income, not a career-replacing business: "nobody... based their ability to put their kids through medical school on this" — a plan B / weekend supplement, not guaranteed to scale into a full-time living for every operator.
- Event selection risk is real (cites his own Tractor Supply flop, $100 in sales) — success depends heavily on correctly reading date/season/location fit, learned through trial and error.
- Requires being physically present and comfortable working outdoors/talking to strangers; he notes this model doesn't suit people who'd rather work a desk job.
