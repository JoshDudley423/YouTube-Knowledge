# How to Profit Big from the AI Wrapper Gold Rush (Part 2/2)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l4i20N1B7ek | Published: 2025-03-17

Conversation between Chris Koerner and Sam Thompson (Creative OS, Unlimited Content) about building "AI wrapper" businesses (products built as a thin, focused interface on top of commodity LLMs like ChatGPT) and live demos of ChatGPT's "Operator" browser-automation agent for business/arbitrage use cases.

## The business idea(s)
- **"AI wrapper" businesses generally**: build a narrow, branded, easy-to-use interface on top of an existing commodity LLM (OpenAI, etc.) rather than trying to build/train a custom model. The core thesis: frontier AI models are becoming commodities, but "interface loyalty" (users forming a habit around a specific branded tool) is the real, durable moat — comparable to how ChatGPT won on ease of use, not necessarily best-in-class model quality (cites a tweet from Greg Isenberg making this argument about DeepSeek/GPT wrappers).
- Concrete example floated: a simplified, large-text "ChatGPT for seniors" product (nicknamed "Lifehelper" in the conversation) sold via Facebook ads to an older demographic for something like $60/year — deliberately unsophisticated, just a rebranded ChatGPT interface targeted at an underserved audience that doesn't know ChatGPT exists.
- Sam Thompson's own examples: "Creative OS" (sells proven ad-creative templates, positioning itself as the go-to tool people return to for Facebook ad management — an intentional "interface loyalty" play on top of what is otherwise a commodity, i.e., templates you could technically find elsewhere) and "Unlimited Content" (AI-powered blog/SEO content service — swaps out underlying AI vendors as better tools emerge, without customers caring, since the deliverable/value stays consistent).
- A DIY niche cold-emailing tool concept: pull contact data for a specific niche (example given: Shopify store owners doing under $500k/month, a segment more likely to want freelance/agency help) into a searchable database with one-click AI-drafted cold email sending, and sell it cheap (~$200/year) to freelancers/boutique agencies who want low-volume, high-relevance outreach rather than a $300-500/month enterprise cold-email stack (Apollo, Instantly, etc.). Back-of-envelope model: if ~1,000 freelancers/agencies pay $200/year, that's $200K ARR on a low/negligible marginal cost, low-team-size product.
- General "drop-shipping software" model proposed: spend a small amount (~$800-$1,000) to build a niche single-purpose AI-wrapper consumer app (a prayer app, cooking app, workout app, productivity app, etc.), run Facebook ads to it, and see what sticks — accepting that the winning niche often emerges from ad-performance data rather than being planned upfront (example given: discovering an unexpected demographic/geographic segment converts well, then building a more targeted spinoff app for that segment).
- Referenced a Shopify affiliate arbitrage tactic another operator used: get people to sign up for Shopify's $1 trial via an affiliate link (paying ~$100 commission per signup at the time), remove all friction by offering to build the person's Shopify store for them for free (via a partnered AI website builder) and driving the whole funnel through a "swipe up" Instagram Story / ManyChat automation — reportedly profitable enough that the operator abandoned selling his own e-commerce/dropshipping courses to focus entirely on this affiliate funnel.

## Numbers
- Cited (secondhand, from an Isenberg tweet): a Silicon Valley "AI wrapper" sales-agent company raised $50 million; describes multiple "wrapper" companies (agent products built on top of existing LLMs) raising eight-figure rounds despite having no proprietary model.
- Shopify affiliate example: ~$100 commission paid per signup at a $1-for-first-three-months trial price point (approximate/secondhand, "middle to end of last year").
- B2B cold-email tool concept: ~$5,000-6,000 buy-in to build, targeting freelancers/agencies at $200/year each; modeled at $200K ARR if ~1,000 buyers sign up, with a VA able to run/manage it for ~$1,200/month, framed as a sellable asset at "half a million to a million dollars."
- Consumer wrapper-app model: framed as an asymmetric bet — risk ~$5,000 to build an app, worst case lose it, best case build a ~$30K ARR asset "worth a hundred grand."

## Process / steps (live-demoed use cases for ChatGPT's "Operator" agent)
- Logged into a Beehiiv newsletter account, scraped poll responses (good/bad/great/top-tier ratings) embedded in past newsletters into a spreadsheet automatically — a task previously manual/tedious enough to require delegating to a human assistant.
- Searched Facebook Marketplace nationwide for a specific pizza-oven model (Gozney), compared listed prices against known market price, and logged underpriced listings with links into a spreadsheet — explicitly floated (but not actually executed) as the basis for an AI-run retail-arbitrage/dropship loop: agent finds an underpriced item on Facebook Marketplace, gets seller's verbal agreement to a price, lists the same item on eBay at a markup using the seller's photos, and once it sells on eBay, pays the original Facebook seller and has them ship directly to the eBay buyer — never taking physical possession of the item (a modernized, AI-run version of the classic "buy-now eBay iPhone arbitrage" trick both hosts did as teenagers).
- Had the agent gather wedding catering quotes: prompted it to contact 10 caterers via TheKnot.com for a hypothetical 50-person Dallas wedding, including picking a plausible date on its own initiative and using "an excessively friendly tone" — it found a way to submit inquiries without needing to create an account, and caterers began replying with real quotes.
- Had the agent search Zillow for "for sale by owner" homes listed significantly below Zillow's own estimated value (a real-estate-flipping lead-gen use case) — found a candidate listing in about 12 minutes.
- Had the agent message sellers on AliExpress (using homepage-featured products as a proxy for "trending/viral" items) requesting free product samples — successfully got outreach sent and samples offered (earbuds, in this case).
- Had the agent search Google Flights for the cheapest non-stop round-trip fare from DFW to LAX or JFK within 6 months, under $100 — found a $69 fare in about 2 minutes, framed as a free/automatable alternative to a paid flight-deal-alert service like ScottsCheapFlights ("a $20M business" charging ~$100/year for something this can do free on a repeating schedule).
- Ran multiple Operator sessions simultaneously (in separate browser tabs, using the Arc browser) to parallelize tasks (e.g., one tab searching DFW-to-Orlando flights, another DFW-to-Seattle) — confirmed this works, opening the idea of a "team of agents" managed like employees inside a single browser workspace.
- Used Operator + the Facebook Ad Library to identify long-running (90+ days) competitor ads in a niche (fitness, then AI cooking apps) as a signal for which ad creatives/angles are actually profitable (long ad runtime and/or high estimated spend, available via the EU version of Meta's Ad Library, is used as a "soft indicator" that an ad is a winner) — used at Sam's company (Creative OS) to build "inspiration boards" for ad creative research.
- Practical Operator usage notes from the demo: explicitly instruct it not to ask for confirmation before each action ("stop asking, just do it") and to "move faster" — both measurably sped up task execution; it handles login by having the human take over the browser tab momentarily to sign in, then resumes; it behaves somewhat human-like (occasionally clicks the wrong thing, correctable via user demonstration, which it can then mimic).

## Tools / platforms named
- ChatGPT Operator (OpenAI's $200/month plan tier at the time) — the core browser-automation agent used throughout the demos.
- Arc Browser — used to run multiple Operator sessions in parallel tabs/workspaces.
- Beehiiv — newsletter platform whose poll data was scraped.
- Facebook Marketplace, eBay, Zillow, AliExpress, Google Flights, TheKnot.com, Facebook Ad Library (EU version for spend estimates) — target sites for various agent-driven use cases.
- Sensor Tower — mentioned as a tool for seeing how many downloads competing consumer apps are getting.
- Apollo, Instantly — named as existing enterprise cold-email/lead-gen tools that a cheaper niche wrapper product would compete with (by serving a smaller/cheaper segment they underserve).
- GoHighLevel — mentioned as a source of ready-made templates/white-label infrastructure for building a digital-service-agency business without building software from scratch.
- Zapier — cited as already having saved "thousands and thousands of dollars" of payroll via basic automation, framed as the precursor layer to full AI agents.

## Caveats / risks
- Both speakers frame this space as extremely early and volatile: "the next breakthrough model is always two weeks away," meaning any technical/model-based advantage is temporary — the durable asset is the user base/habit ("interface lock-in"), not underlying AI capability.
- Explicit human-oversight boundary stated by Sam Thompson: his company currently draws the line at not sending AI-generated client communications without a human reviewing them first, though he expects that boundary to loosen over time as trust in agent reliability increases.
- Retail-arbitrage/dropshipping-via-agent idea and several others were explicitly discussed as *not yet actually built* — presented as plausible/interesting ideas from live experimentation, not proven business models with track records.
- Consumer-app "spray and pray" wrapper strategy is explicitly framed as trial-and-error (accepting you may need to iterate niches/audiences based on ad performance data rather than planning correctly upfront) and inherently speculative — described as an "asymmetric bet," i.e., expected value reasoning rather than a guaranteed return.
- The agent (Operator) still has real technical limitations noted live in the demo — e.g., getting stuck on a date-filter UI element requiring the user to intervene and demonstrate the correct action manually, and being unable to actually complete a flight booking (found the flight but could not book it).
