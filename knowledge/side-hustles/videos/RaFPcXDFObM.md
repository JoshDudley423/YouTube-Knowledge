# 7 Low-Cost Business Ideas for Beginners in 2026
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RaFPcXDFObM | Published: 2026-02-20

Seven food-industry business/side-hustle ideas, ranked #7 (least favorite) to #1 (favorite).

## 7. Grill cleaning
- Market math (Dallas-Fort Worth used as a representative US metro): 2.4M households, ~8 competing grill-cleaning businesses. If only 1% of households pay $250/cleaning, that's 24,000 households, ~3,000 per existing business = $750,000/year revenue per business.
- At ~50% profit margin (mostly gas + labor costs), that's ~$375,000/year owner profit at just 1% market penetration.
- Volume: 10 grills/day covers that revenue if working all year; a 3-person crew doing 3.3 grills/day each is more realistic than 1 person doing 10.
- Not an urgent service, so routes can be planned efficiently (no need to drive an hour between jobs).
- Efficiency trick: pick up grates from multiple houses in a day, soak overnight in harsh chemicals at a ~1,000 sq ft shop, scrub, return.
- Customer acquisition: Facebook ads, flyers, direct mail, Google ads, Google Business Profile.
- Google Trends for "grill cleaning": slow upward trend nationally, faster and less seasonal in warmer markets (e.g., Texas); seasonal peak every June elsewhere.
- Upsells: pressure washing, oven cleaning, grill tool sales, pizza oven sales/cleaning, outdoor kitchen deep cleaning, driveway/sidewalk/house/roof/gutter/window cleaning (you're already onsite with chemicals and a pressure washer).
- Caveat: assumes people will pay $250 for a service on a $100 grill -- author argues even a small % of the market makes the math work.

## 6. Mini pancakes (Dutch pancakes / poffertjes)
- Appliance-specific: a poffertjes/mini-pancake griddle, buyable on Amazon for $70-$700 (example listings seen: $78 and $179 with 5-star reviews).
- Sold like frozen yogurt: base of mini pancakes + toppings (fruit, chocolate, etc.), sweet or savory (think crepes).
- Price: $12-$14 per serving.
- Margins: ~80% gross, ~60% net if you run it yourself; drops to ~40% net if you hire someone.
- One operator makes as much as a two-week paycheck in ~4 hours of work.
- Setup: front yard/neighborhood or busy street corner; a few minutes to assemble.
- Marketing: posts to Facebook, Nextdoor, Instagram announcing hours (e.g., "here 5 hours Saturday morning"); no retail location needed.
- Google Trends: near-zero search interest before 2009, growing steadily since.

## 5. Dehydrated fruit / dehydrator-as-asset businesses
- Example: David Blake sells high-quality dehydrated fruit (orange, pear, apple, lime -- in that popularity order), buying produce at full retail from grocery stores.
- Numbers: $50,000 revenue this year part-time; on track for $100,000 net profit next year. Started with 1 dehydrator, now has 10, no employees. Started at a farmer's market.
- Dehydrator vs. freeze dryer distinction: dehydrators remove ~80-95% moisture, 1-5 year shelf life, cost as little as $50 (Amazon); freeze dryers use vacuum-sealed sub-zero temps, remove ~99% moisture, preserve food up to 25 years, higher nutrient retention, cost $2,000-$5,000.
- Tip: buy a dehydrator with a 30-day money-back warranty to experiment risk-free.
- Related example (freeze dryer, not dehydrator): a man in Idaho buys colostrum from dairy farmers at $3.20/gallon, freeze-dries it, sells the powder to other dairy farmers at ~$20/lb. 1 gallon liquid yields ~2.5 lbs powder, sold for ~$46 total (net ~$43 profit per gallon processed). One cow needs ~1 lb colostrum/year, so a 100-cow farm (a small operation) needs ~$2,000/year of this product.
- General framework: buy an asset (dehydrator, freeze dryer, skid steer, stump grinder, ditch witch, backhoe) that becomes the backbone of a rentable or service-based business.
- Caveat: expect obstacles like food handling permits -- author says don't let that stop you, "every business has obstacles."

## 4. Luxury picnic experiences
- Example: a woman charges $440-$700+ per picnic package (higher for premium ingredients), takes ~2 hours to execute, doesn't own land (uses public parks/lakes) and doesn't need a van (can rent a van/trailer/U-Haul/Home Depot truck).
- Setup: table, rug, food, plates, silverware at a park/pond/lake/national park.
- Growth model: start near-free while building an Instagram portfolio documenting the work, then raise prices as demand increases.
- Customer acquisition: organic short-form/time-lapse video content; once a video performs well, boost it on Instagram targeted by zip code to 25-55 year-old couples locally.
- Typically no food handler's permit or chef needed since food is simple (fruit, veg, sandwiches, hummus) rather than cooked.
- Framed as a date-night alternative to a $300-$500 restaurant dinner.

## 3. Frozen grapes in fancy packaging
- A guy buys grapes (cost ~$2), packages them like Ben & Jerry's pints, sells for $8-$10 (2-5x markup over plain grapes). Now sold in hundreds of grocery stores; also does mangoes, strawberries, blueberries.
- Framed as "you can just do things" -- ignored people telling him it was "just a snack, not a business."
- Marshmallow Challenge anecdote used to argue bias toward action over over-planning: kids (avg. tower height 26 in) who just experimented rapidly beat executives (avg. 10 in) who spent most of their time planning.
- Approachability caveat: hard to replicate exactly (getting into grocery stores is not accessible to most), but the same product could be sold via a booth/cart/farmers market/friends-and-family instead.

## 2. On-your-honor bakery/farm stands (3 sub-examples)
- Unstaffed stand, mostly sourdough/bakery items; customers pay via QR code/Venmo on the honor system.
- Economics: typical food-business labor cost is ~30%; even if theft losses hit 30% of product (they don't come close), the model still breaks even or wins vs. paying staff.
- Example: a woman near Frisco, TX makes "hundreds of dollars a day in profit" from a front-yard stand (6-ft folding table) with no employees; morning line formed at 7:30am while she was asleep after baking all night.
- Related "reciprocity" tactic: give away free coffee/muffins to your neighborhood (works for any business, e.g., roofers/HVAC) to build goodwill and use the law of reciprocity to generate high-ticket service leads.
- Author's own example: wife sells cookie bars from a mini-fridge on the front porch, on the honor system, never stolen from.
- Caveat: won't work in every neighborhood, but "would work in most."

## 1. Watermelon-based products (3 sub-concepts, top favorite)
- **Wopsicles** (watermelon on a stick, like a popsicle): a teenager sold these at a parade after shoulder surgery sidelined his landscaping work. Cost ~15-20 cents each (7 watermelons + 2 bags of ice), sold for $1 each; sold ~50 in a single day. Best venues: parades, farmers markets, anywhere with foot traffic.
- **Watermelon "sandwich"**: watermelon slices as "bread" with cheese filling, ready-to-eat; trending in Iraq. Sells for $2-$8 depending on country/market; ~75 cents of every dollar is profit.
- **Watermelon slushy**: cut top off a mini watermelon, blend the flesh inside with a hand mixer, add a straw, sell for ~$9; mini watermelons cost $2-$3.
- Testing method suggested: try 5 high-foot-traffic locations at the same day/time across 5 different weeks to find the best selling spot.
- Caveat: seasonal, location-dependent, but very high margin and low-barrier -- framed as a good first business to learn sales fundamentals before moving to something bigger.

## General framing
- All ideas pitched as low-cost, approachable entry points -- either weekend side hustles or scalable full businesses.
- Recurring theme: bias toward action/testing over excessive planning or seeking validation (marshmallow challenge, frozen grapes story).
