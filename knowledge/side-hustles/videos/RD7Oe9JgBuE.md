# Q&A: How Do I Grow My Local Business? (+5 others)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RD7Oe9JgBuE | Published: 2025-01-13

Listener Q&A episode — six separate questions answered. Not a single business idea; a grab-bag of tactical advice applicable across small businesses/side hustles.

## Q1: Private jet photo studio — how to pick a location
- The idea: a studio set that looks like a private jet interior, rented out (reportedly $40-$200/session) for people to take flex photos for social media. Only a few exist in the US (New York, LA, maybe Atlanta).
- Before launching in a new market (Dallas), reverse-engineer the existing ones: find their locations, visit in person if serious, note whether customers walk in spontaneously or need an appointment, check pricing and booking systems, and use the Wayback Machine (web archive) and WHOIS lookups to see how long their website/social presence has existed.
- Recommendation: build the actual studio inside a 20-foot shipping container (cheaper and portable — pay ~$500 for a flatbed truck to relocate it) rather than a fixed retail lease. Place it in a walkable outdoor shopping area (e.g., Legacy West, Watters Creek in DFW) with permission, wrapped in branding so passersby immediately recognize the offer.
- Core insight: proximity converts far more than people expect because this is an impulse purchase, not a planned one — the "extra narcissistic" customers will drive an hour, but the mass market needs the offer directly in front of them. A portable shipping-container format lets you A/B test locations (something impossible with a 5-20 year retail lease) — cites a friend's 40-foot shipping-container escape room business that did exactly this. "Location wins always — don't skimp on cost on location when it comes to a retail business like this."

## Q2: Starting a tree-trimming (or any local home-service) business while working a 9-to-5
- Model referenced: subcontracted-crew home service business (crews do the physical work; owner doesn't own equipment, e.g., no chainsaw) — same model discussed elsewhere on the channel.
- For someone with a full 9-5 (calls, Zooms) who can't go quote jobs during the day: personally quote jobs yourself for the first few months regardless (needed to learn the industry and avoid being taken advantage of by subcontractors), but schedule quoting around the day job — before 9am, after 5pm (even evening quotes with a work light/"Mag light"), or batch all quoting into Saturdays or a single afternoon/day off every two weeks.
- Anything doable from a computer during work downtime (Thumbtack lead management, SMS follow-up, arranging realtor referrals, marketing) can be handled alongside the day job.
- Once the business matures, crews can be trained to quote jobs directly without the owner, as long as the owner remains the sole customer-facing point of contact (to prevent subcontractors from cutting the owner out and taking cash directly).
- Framing: having a 9-5 is a real constraint, but "constraints breed creativity" — it forces more deliberate scheduling rather than blocking the business outright.

## Q3: When to form an LLC for a new business experiment
- Koerner has "countless" LLCs, each costing roughly $300 to form plus $50-$100/year to maintain — he does NOT file one for every business idea he tries (has started 70+ businesses).
- His two triggers for finally forming an LLC: (1) confidence the business is a real, ongoing thing (not just a one-off test) — i.e., you're reasonably sure you'll still be doing it in 6 months to years; and (2) you've already generated real, repeatable revenue from strangers, not just friends-and-family money. Hiring an employee/contractor is also cited as an automatic trigger to get an LLC.
- Early low-stakes tests (his example: selling pizzas at a farmers market "for funsies") don't need an LLC unless required by specific regulation. Only once a test proves durable demand (e.g., you could hire someone at $20/hour and still profit $500/market) does forming an LLC become worthwhile.
- If an early test/launch goes poorly (which is common), either abandon it without ever forming an LLC, or keep experimenting informally and only incorporate later if it proves out.

## Q4: Investing $50,000 passively in an existing e-commerce store, run by someone else
- Koerner's blunt take: he dislikes the phrase "passive income" — it exists only after a large amount of prior hard work created the capital/asset (e.g., his RV park investors enjoy passive income only after years/decades of saving the capital to invest); buying and passively owning an operated e-commerce store is "not passive income by any stretch," even with a hired operator.
- Selection-bias problem with this plan: a truly great e-commerce operator willing to split profit with an outside investor on a modest-size deal ($250K-ish business, funded by $50K down + 80% seller financing, netting ~$80K/year profit at "3x profit" valuation) is unlikely to exist — a genuinely skilled operator would already be working for a well-funded DTC brand or building their own; someone willing to take this deal is more likely inexperienced, meaning the "passive" investor ends up effectively training/supervising them, undermining the entire point of passivity.
- SBA financing is explicitly discouraged for a deal this small — the hassle of an SBA loan is only worth it for a seven-figure business, not a $250K one.
- Fulfillment/logistics matters: using a 3PL cuts into thin e-commerce margins; self-fulfillment requires warehouse space and staff, which further destroys "passive"-ness. A purely digital product business would function more like a SaaS or agency, not the pure e-commerce play the investor described wanting.
- Marketplaces named for buying/selling existing Shopify-type e-commerce businesses: Quiet Light, Flippa, Acquire.com, and Empire Flippers ("Bisby Sell.com" transcribed — likely referring to a similar business brokerage). Shopify itself used to run its own marketplace for buying/selling stores but shut it down.
- Bottom line: technically possible, but very hard to actually find the intersection of "good enough operator" + "willing to give up equity/control" + "deal small enough for a $50K check" — the caller should reconsider the plan rather than pursue it as described.

## Q5: Cold email follow-up cadence (online personal training targeting real estate agents)
- Niche example praised: online personal training specifically targeted at high-performing male real estate agents.
- Initial cold email structure recommended: subject line max 2 words, at least 2 points of personalization, pitch kept under 3 sentences in the body, no links (to avoid deliverability issues that could taint A/B results), and the email must end with a question — a question functions as an explicit call-to-action/response trigger. Never end with just a comment/statement.
- Follow-up cadence: first follow-up 2 days after initial send, second follow-up 3 days after that (day 5 total), third and final follow-up on day 7 total — then stop entirely. Never follow up more than 3 times; response/conversion likelihood drops roughly 80% with each additional follow-up beyond that.
- For a personal, high-touch offer like this, prioritize getting the prospect on a live phone call as quickly as possible rather than trying to close over email/text, since it's a considered, personal purchase.
- Tool mentioned: the caller was using "uptics.io" (a cold outreach/CRM automation platform) for sequencing.

## Q6a: Monetizing two large inflatable event walls (25ft x 25ft x 9ft), Cleveland OH
- General framework for any weird/novel product/asset: go up the supply chain to the original source — contact the manufacturer or distributor directly (call by phone, not email) and ask who their actual customers are (event planners? brides? conference centers?), how those customers use the product, whether anyone rents these out already, what the going rental rate is, and where such rentals are typically listed (e.g., Peerspace, Facebook Marketplace, The Knot). This upstream-supplier interview approach is presented as the single highest-leverage research step ("the 80/20 of it") for figuring out how to monetize an unfamiliar product.

## Q6b: Scaling a dog-training business from neighborhood-famous to city-famous (Washington DC, 11 years in business)
- Identify the biggest, most well-regarded competitors in the target market (the ones "doing it all right" — always busy, strong social presence, clear offers/pricing) and reverse-engineer them.
- Tactics to research competitors: pose as a prospective customer and call them directly to ask how they find customers, how long they've been in business, etc. Use a free Chrome extension called "TikTok and Instagram Sorter" to sort a competitor's social posts by oldest-first or most-viewed, revealing how their offers/content evolved over time and which posts performed best. Use the Wayback Machine (web archive) and WHOIS to see how a competitor's website evolved historically, including tracing when/how they scaled from neighborhood-known to city-known.
- Reality check: fast-growing local competitors usually are NOT doing anything exotic — the "80/20" is almost always simple Facebook or Google ads with a good offer, paired with a decent photo/video and maybe a coupon, scaled aggressively; or, for local services, heavy spend on a single lead-gen marketplace (examples given: Angie's List — cited spending as much as $30,000/month by a competitor — Thumbtack, or Sniffspot/dog-walking marketplaces), sometimes exclusively, rather than diversified marketing.

## Closing advice for 2025 (mindset, not a business idea)
- Observation from Koerner's own experience advising many people: he has never spoken to anyone who was on the fence about quitting a job or starting a business, did it, and later regretted having done so — even people who ultimately failed (including bankruptcy) generally don't regret the attempt over a long enough time horizon. People tend to regret things they didn't do/things they quit (his own example: quitting wrestling in high school), not things they did.
- Framework/phrase highlighted: "Now, not how" — attributed to Mitchell Cohen (head of content at AppSumo, working for Noah Kagan), credited in Kagan's book as the source of its first chapter title. The idea: avoid analysis paralysis; take direct, forward action (e.g., buy the domain name, collect the first dollar of revenue) rather than waiting to have the "how" fully figured out.

## Caveats / risks
- Private jet photo studio idea is explicitly described by Koerner personally as "dumb," "cringe," and something that "makes me a little sad" as a product/behavior — he frames the advice purely as "if they're going to do it anyway, here's how to monetize it," not as an endorsement of the underlying demand being admirable.
- The e-commerce "passive income" investment plan is directly discouraged by Koerner as likely to fail to find a workable counterparty at the stated budget; he calls it a "hard truth" meant to save the caller "heartache."
- Cold email follow-up numbers (80% drop-off per follow-up, exact day cadence) are Koerner's personal heuristic based on his own experience, not data specific to the caller's real estate/fitness niche — he explicitly says the advice could vary by industry and that he's making assumptions without knowing the caller's exact offer/pricing.
- LLC formation guidance is general, non-legal advice from personal experience (cost figures are California-specific in the caller's case and vary by state); Koerner does not present it as formal legal or tax counsel.
