# Secrets to Earning from Facebook Communities
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0ZKgF6M0jW4 | Published: 2024-12-01

"HoldCo Bros" episode (Chris Koerner + Nik of Nikonomics Podcast) covering three separate business ideas: custom concerts, AI-powered local newsletters, and Facebook-group email/lead capture.

## Idea 1: "Custom concerts" (pay-to-request live music events)

- Inspired by Nashville's Broadway honky-tonk bars, where a musician on stage has a QR code / Venmo bucket ("$20, I'll play whatever you want"), and patrons pay per song request.
- Proposed business: replicate this as a standalone ticketed/experiential event (not tied to a bar) in any city — rent a venue for one night (don't own/lease long-term), bring in a talented local band with a large song repertoire, and let attendees bid/pay to request songs (could be a silent-auction style bidding system where the highest bidder's request goes to the top of the queue; door fee of $5-10 also suggested).
- Comparable/reference business models cited: "candlelight concerts" (a company bringing in a pianist + fake candles, described as a $20-50 million company) and "Bingo Loco" (a bingo + dance party + music event that reportedly grosses $50,000-200,000 per night).
- Suggested go-to-market: find one great band in a market to prove the concept, then "tour" the concept city to city like a touring cover band; market via Facebook ads (cited as how Bingo Loco and candlelight concerts scale).
- Suggested partnership/piggyback strategy: partner with existing Bingo Loco-style event operators to run custom-concert as an add-on/extension after their scheduled event ends (e.g., "$20 more, stay another hour"), splitting/covering the door fee.
- No revenue/cost figures given for the concert idea itself (unlike the referenced comparables).

## Idea 2: AI-powered local newsletter (as a wedge into an AI automation agency)

- Premise: local/home-service business owners generally know little about AI ("Twitter bubble" effect — AI is huge in tech circles but has barely reached small local businesses).
- Recommended launch sequence: knock on doors of local businesses, offer a free AI demo/service (e.g., help write their first contract, or any small demonstrable value), and use that interaction to get them to opt into a local newsletter — rather than trying to close paid work immediately.
- The newsletter serves two functions: (1) builds an audience that can eventually be monetized via local ad sales, and (2) captures leads who later convert into paid AI-automation project work once they're "ready."
- Monetization suggested for the eventual AI-automation service: $1,000-3,000/month recurring for Zapier/Make/OpenAI-API integration work — not ad-based.
- Scaling idea (syndication model): rather than each local operator writing unique newsletter content, one parent entity writes a single AI-newsletter template, and 100 different local "franchisees" in 100 different markets each send the same (or lightly localized) content to their own local subscriber list — since it's private email, no one notices the duplication (no public SEO exposure). Each local operator's job becomes: (a) recruit local subscribers and (b) handle the local customer interface/service delivery; back-end content and (per the hosts' own related project, "Repeat Leads") fulfillment can be provided/white-labeled by the parent entity.
- Recommended niching strategy: pick either a narrow business vertical (e.g., CPAs — go deep enough to build genuinely custom automations for their specific workflows) OR a small geography (rule of thumb: cap around 6,000 households / ~20,000-25,000 people — cited a real neighborhood example, Cross Creek Ranch in Houston, with 6,000 homes / ~19-20K residents / ~600 business owners assuming 10% of Americans are business owners). Going broader (e.g., "Boise") is deemed too large to service directly; broad geography only makes sense if the real business model is pure newsletter ad sales rather than a local service funnel.
- General newsletter economics claim: customer acquisition cost of ~$1-2/subscriber against an LTV of ~$50/subscriber (a 25:1 to 50:1 LTV:CAC ratio, vs. a "great" ratio elsewhere typically being ~3:1).
- Caveat on subscriber quality: co-registration ("co-reg") signups — where someone opts into a newsletter incidentally while signing up for something else — are low-quality and churn fast; organically opted-in subscribers are far more valuable to both the business and to advertisers. More niche/local newsletters get higher open and click rates.
- Reasoning for email over social reach: email is a channel you own; Facebook/Instagram/Twitter can change algorithms any time and you don't own the relationship or guarantee delivery. A direct email relationship is called more "intimate"/higher-intent than a social media follow.

## Idea 3: Facebook Group email capture as a lead-gen business

- Tool: **Groupboss** (groupboss.io) — syncs with a Facebook Group and lets the group owner/moderator add custom join-questions (e.g., "what's your email address?") to capture emails from new members as they join; emails flow into a Google Sheet, which can then feed Beehiiv/Substack/etc. via Zapier. Facebook itself doesn't want groups collecting emails, but a moderator can ask for it via custom join questions.
- Personal example: Chris started a niche "RV park off-market" Facebook group with zero promotion; Facebook's own algorithm grew it organically to ~2,000 members in 6 months, yielding ~1,100 opted-in emails (of arguably real-estate-investor caliber) at $0 acquisition cost.
- **Faster alternative — buy an existing Facebook group**: most niche Facebook groups are unmonetized, so many moderators will sell for a modest one-time payment (example figure: ~$1,000). After buying, simply change the join questions to start capturing new-member emails going forward (retroactively getting emails from existing members is much harder/less successful).
- **Service/arbitrage model without owning any groups**: reach out to owners of large, active Facebook groups and offer to implement the email-capture automation for them, in exchange for either (a) a flat implementation fee (e.g., $500), or (b) a per-email finder's fee (e.g., $0.10 per email address collected going forward) — framed around the stat that acquiring an email address typically costs advertisers ~$2, so a group with 100,000 members represents up to ~$200,000 of latent email value.
- **Mortgage-broker-specific arbitrage example (the "hook" of the episode)**: mortgage/real-estate leads often cost lenders $50-300+ per lead. There are "thousands" of real-estate-investor Facebook groups (e.g., ~20 in a single market like Plano, TX; "hundreds" within a narrower niche like self-storage/Class B industrial). Play: identify buyers of these leads first (e.g., search LendingTree.com to find which specific loan officers/brokers are already paying for leads, then find them on LinkedIn), pay Facebook group moderators a small per-email fee (e.g., $0.50/email) to implement the Groupboss-style capture, mark the emails up when reselling to the identified buyer (example figures discussed: buy at $5/email, sell at $30/email), and only purchase emails once already pre-sold to a buyer — pure "middleman" model with a live shared Google Sheet to track email counts owed.
- Adjacent idea: target people already commenting/self-promoting in Facebook groups (e.g., "local CPA," "local insurance broker" who spam their services in comment sections) — offer the group owner a per-email fee to implement capture, turning what used to be free organic self-promotion into a paid data acquisition channel for that same service provider (cited real example: "Dan Magazu," who runs a business built around commenting in Facebook groups to generate leads, as the kind of person who'd pay for this instead).
- Legal/compliance caveat raised explicitly but NOT answered on the show: whether cold-contacting people whose emails were captured via a Facebook group join-question (versus explicit opt-in to marketing) is compliant with CAN-SPAM — the hosts acknowledge this is a real gray area and say to "do it the right way" (e.g., explicitly disclose in the join question that the person will receive periodic emails) rather than giving definitive legal guidance.
- Additional monetization path for Facebook groups directly: sponsored posts pinned in large niche groups (example cited: a Hawaii-tourism Facebook group with ~150,000 members reportedly charging local tour operators $500-1,000 per pinned promotional post) — described as an unregulated "black market," not something Facebook officially supports.
- Suggested newsletter-as-retention-tool for group owners: recommend the group owner start a weekly "in case you missed it" email newsletter (top 3 posts of the week) to counteract low organic reach on any single post (estimated ~1/50th of members see any given post); this newsletter can then be independently monetized via a paid sponsor slot — pitched as a clean, opt-in-compliant way for e.g. a mortgage broker to get a free ad placement (e.g., free ad for the first 6 months in exchange for setting up the whole system) without directly cold-contacting captured emails.

## Caveats raised across the episode

- The Facebook-group email arbitrage model works best when there's a genuine, valuable use for the captured emails (e.g., a real buyer already lined up) — without that, it's just data collection with no guaranteed monetization.
- Groups vary hugely in quality/moderation; check engagement levels before buying a group or striking a per-email deal with its moderator.
- No hard revenue numbers are given for the Facebook-group lead-arbitrage business itself (only illustrative price ranges); it's framed as a nascent tactic rather than a proven, scaled business in this episode.
