# How to Flip High Ticket Items in 2026
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aegGV_J71oA | Published: 2024-07-13

Guest: Shannon Jean, serial entrepreneur (Mac Rescue/Rescue Systems, Tech Restore iPhone repair/parts business, Poshmark handbag reselling, RV park co-GP, author).

Core idea discussed: flipping/deal-making (auction liquidation, wholesale, resale of handbags, electronics, vehicles, furniture) as a scalable path from small capital to large returns, and finding "asymmetric supply and demand inefficiencies" in your local market.

Key business/flipping concepts and numbers:
- Auction flip example: attended a Sprint (cellular carrier) facility liquidation auction in Kansas with only ~6 other bidders present; bought roughly $300-400K worth of equipment (bidding "we'll take it all" even without having the cash on hand up front — buy the deal first, find money later) and flipped it for $4-5 million using multiple channels simultaneously: wholesale to other dealers, eBay (parts division), a repair division (using scavenged parts), and a consumer resale division. Belief: "if the deal is good enough you'll find the money."
- Component/parts stripping: often components are worth more disassembled than the whole unit, but takes longer to sell — worth doing when you have the labor to spare.
- Laser printer flip: bought damaged Apple color laser printers (contaminated internal oil trays) for ~$500 each from an insurance company doing replacements, had an internship program repair/clean them, resold for $5,000 each (vs. $10,000 new).
- Handbag/luxury goods flipping (via Poshmark): took about a year of research to find a reliable supplier; first-year sales of $500,000; over four years grew to roughly $4M in total sales with about $552K net profit — run solo, working about an hour a day once systems were automated.
- Key handbag-flipping method: check eBay "sold" listings (not "for sale" or "completed" — must be actual sold price) to verify real resale value, and check sales velocity (how fast items actually move) before buying inventory in bulk.
- Sourcing philosophy: don't approach vendors asking to buy their best products — ask "what do you have that no one else will buy?" Build the relationship first without expecting immediate profit; profitable access comes later once you're seen as a reliable buyer. Guest had to vet 13 bad vendors in China before finding one good one; flew to China twice to vet suppliers and negotiate financing terms for his iPhone parts business.
- Sold iPhone parts sourced from China to repair shops for roughly double what those shops could get by ordering directly from Alibaba — the premium was justified by removing "friction" (fear of being scammed, unfamiliarity with sourcing).
- Applied same logic to importing designer handbags from a Japanese auction house: consumers wouldn't buy directly from an unknown Japanese auction house, but would pay $500-600 (vs. his ~$300 cost) buying from him on Poshmark/Vestiaire/eBay because of his ratings/reviews/press features (San Francisco Chronicle, Business Insider) building trust ("that's the delta").
- Government/institutional liquidation sources named: govdeals.com and GSA auctions (gsaauctions.com) — cites live example of GSA reselling FEMA/RV trailers (2020-2021 models) for $5-6,000 that could be repaired minimally and flipped for $12,000+ or rented via rvshare.com; suggests raising capital from friends/family ("Uncle Mike") once you have a concrete researched deal with comps.
- Emphasizes location/logistics as critical: unsexy, bulky, non-shippable items (trailers, furniture) have far less competition than shippable/sexy items (electronics, cell phones) because buyers must be physically present, eliminating national competition. Recommends targeting boring/unsexy niches over "sexy" ones like consumer electronics, which have thin margins due to oversaturation ("you're better off selling shampoo").
- Example: a full truckload of Costco return dining furniture (B-grade, meaning likely near-A-grade due to Costco's conservative grading) sold at auction for just $250 despite a Costco retail value of about $42,000 — profitable for anyone locally positioned to pick it up, though shipping cost/friction from the buyer's location is a key constraint.
- COVID-era opportunistic buying: bulk purchase of oversold home exercise equipment and dining furniture as prices crashed post-pandemic due to falling demand.
- Real estate deal-sourcing technique: narrow inventory via specific criteria (e.g., asked how many 6-bedroom rental houses existed in an area — found only 50 out of thousands of listings) to target under-competed niches; look for extra "friction points" that suppress buyer competition, such as sellers living out of state, properties with an unusual/off-putting backstory (e.g., a reported accident), or properties nobody else wants for idiosyncratic reasons (e.g., a house a government agency wanted to acquire — bought at half asking price, held 5 years, sold to the US government for 3x purchase price).
- Recommends starting small and compounding: e.g., $1,000 to $1,500 to $3,000 to $6,000, reinvesting profits repeatedly rather than seeking one big score.
- Uses AI (mentioned generally) to help parse auction/liquidation data and to generate lists of similar auction/liquidation sites once you've found one good source (example: used AI to find sites similar to GSA auctions, leading him to discover US Treasury auctions).

Caveats/risks raised:
- Buying inventory sight-unseen from far away for shipping (e.g., cell phones on GovDeals) puts you in direct competition with buyers nationwide and kills margin — physical presence/local pickup is the edge.
- Consumer electronics/tech flipping has thin margins due to heavy competition ("it's sexy, so it attracts so many people").
- It took a full year of dedicated research to find a reliable handbag supplier — this is not a fast/easy business to start.
- Ordering unfamiliar goods (e.g., iPhone/computer parts) from overseas carries real risk of receiving misdescribed/defective batches; only takes one bad batch to feel the pain of an unreliable vendor.
- Success in this space requires tolerance for tedious, time-intensive research (compared to time spent on entertainment) and persistence contacting reluctant business targets (e.g., repeatedly trying to get meetings with Best Buy and GameStop for returns/liquidation deals — described as needing to "be a pest").
- Guest emphasizes that most people online promoting easier digital/agency businesses aren't describing this style of work — physical flipping/deal-sourcing is a different, hands-on skill set (sourcing, sales, marketing, packaging, photography, customer service).
