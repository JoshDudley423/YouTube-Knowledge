# I Told an AI Agent to Make Me Money. It Did.
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1PwTrINNzOA | Published: 2026-05-30

Two-part interview with Brandon Doyle (founder of AI agency getdavid.ai) filmed a month apart: part 1 brainstorms business ideas an autonomous AI agent ("OpenClaw") could launch; part 2 reports real results. Concrete, high-signal case study with real dollar figures.

## The tool: OpenClaw (AI agent framework)

- An AI agent framework that can be connected to any underlying model (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, etc.), given a credit card/tool access, and interfaced with wherever the user already spends time — iMessage, Slack, email, Telegram, browser, etc.
- Brandon runs his personal instance ("Zach Morris") entirely through iMessage on his phone, connected to a Mac mini running 24/7 in his office, with output delivered via text, Google Docs, Google Sheets, or Gmail.
- Explicit strategic point (echoed by host): put the agent interface inside whatever app/channel the user (or a client) already lives in daily (Slack for Slack-heavy teams, email for email-heavy teams, iMessage for individuals) — minimizing friction drives usage, and usage drives results ("the more you use it, the wealthier you get from it"). When building this for clients (Brandon's AI agency), they deliberately match the agent's interface to the client's existing habits.

## Idea 1 (launched, moderate success): AI-generated custom bedtime stories — "DreamTales" (dreamtales.io)

- Concept: parents create a profile for their kid(s) (name, favorite animal/color, themes like princesses/cars/superheroes), and the service emails a new, personalized bedtime story every night at a set time (7:00 p.m.).
- Origin: Brandon had manually done a version of this for years with his own 4 kids by retelling Marvel movies with characters renamed after his kids — recognized this as tedious to sustain and hard to generalize (not everyone knows enough movie plots to draw from).
- Deliberately NOT built as an app — delivered via email specifically because people already have too many apps and won't install a new one for a niche use case; meeting users in an existing habit (inbox) beats asking for new app-install friction.
- Build process: built essentially "one-shot" via a detailed voice prompt to OpenClaw via iMessage (e.g., "build a custom bedtime story site, charge ~$9/month, generate at least 365 unique stories per year, spin up the website, create ad creatives"). Manually had to buy the domain.
- Pricing: launched at $9/month for 1 child (later considering restructuring to $9 for 1 kid / $19 for up to 4-6 kids, from an initial $24-for-3-kids tier) with a 7-day free trial.
- Marketing: ran Facebook/Instagram ads — one video ad made personally by Brandon (dad-of-4 talking to camera, ~20 seconds) and several AI-generated ad creatives (still images of moms/kids) made by OpenClaw; let Meta's algorithm handle targeting.
- **Results after ~1 month**: roughly $300/month MRR (~33-34 customers) against ~$400 total ad spend — breakeven on ad spend in month one, but since it's recurring revenue it compounds forward. Brandon's own live-talking-head video ad outperformed the AI-generated creatives (attributed to a human/relatable connection with a dad-of-four persona), though he thinks an AI avatar tool like HeyGen could get "pretty dang close."
- Scaling thought: believes the strongest version of this business would be founder-led by an actual mom/creative-writer who authors story rubrics herself (with AI assisting behind the scenes), since the buyer base is mostly moms and would connect more with an authentic creator-author framing than a faceless SaaS tool.
- Framed explicitly as a side/demo project for Brandon's AI agency — not his main business, but valuable as a concrete proof point to show AI-consulting clients "we eat our own dog food."

## Idea 2 (launched, the big win): AI-agent-built local business websites + physical postcard outreach — the standout result of the episode

- Step 1: prompted OpenClaw to scrape Google Business Profile listings across Utah for local home-service businesses (ended up mostly landscaping, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roofing) — pulled business name, contact name, phone, email directly from each listing. Found/targeted 350 businesses, a mix of some with poor existing websites and some with none at all.
- Step 2: prompted the agent to build a simple website for each business using only publicly available data (from their Google listing), telling it to use whichever platform/model kept token costs low — it opted for cheaper Chinese LLMs to hold cost down and hosted the sites at temporary/test URLs (not yet on custom domains).
- **Cost**: roughly "a handful of cents" in token cost per website — total for all 350 sites came to only ~$10-20.
- Step 3: prompted the agent to send a physical direct-mail postcard to each business with a QR code, using the **Lob** API (lob.com, a direct-mail-as-a-service platform) — the agent identified and used this tool on its own when asked if postcard automation was possible. Postcard design: front reads "We built you a free website — scan the QR code to see your new site," QR code links to the business's new site; back has the agency's ("David AI") contact info.
- **Postcard cost**: a little over $1 each; ~$400 total for 350 postcards.
- **Delivery/response data (via Lob analytics)**: 340 of 350 successfully delivered (9 lost in mail); 68 recipients (~20% of delivered) scanned the QR code.
- Step 4: handed the full contact list to a part-time human sales rep, instructed to call everyone but prioritize/call hardest on anyone who had scanned the QR code, pitching: the website is free if they sign up for the agency's cheapest marketing retainer package (a few hundred dollars/month, packages ranging ~$300-700/month).
- **Results after ~1 month**: ~$8,000/month in new MRR, from roughly 17-20 unique customers (average ~$400/month each; a couple of larger accounts near $1,200/month). Of the 68 QR-code scanners, roughly 14 (~20% of scanners) converted to paying customers — i.e., of 350 targeted businesses, about a 20% scan rate and then a ~20% close rate among scanners drove the large majority (~90%) of closes; the remaining ~10% of closes came from cold-call contacts who hadn't scanned.
- Total cash cost of the whole experiment: roughly $400 (postcards) + $10-20 (AI tokens) — a few hundred dollars all-in against $8,000/month in new recurring revenue.
- Explicit reasoning for why this outperformed a pure digital/email approach: compounding multiple "hard," higher-friction, higher-signal actions (physical mail costs real money and time; building an actual working website per prospect; a live phone call) each individually raises perceived legitimacy/effort in the recipient's eyes, and stacking several of these signals compounds conversion multiplicatively rather than additively — contrasted with the "easy" default most people take (cold email/scraper outreach), which the hosts argue produces far lower response because the effort signal is low and recipients are inundated with similar low-effort pitches. Analogy drawn to MrBeast's early "give a homeless man $10,000 instead of $5,000" insight: visibly higher stakes/investment drives disproportionately higher engagement, not just proportionally higher.
- Planned next iteration (month 2, at time of recording): retarget businesses that DO already have a website but rank poorly in Google/SEO, offering a rebuilt/SEO-improved site as the new hook, and running the same postcard + cold-call playbook — explicitly likened to running this as a repeatable, monthly-refined acquisition engine.
- Acknowledged tracking gap: didn't separately track close rates for prospects who already had a website vs. those with none, so no data yet on which segment converts better (a split test the host had explicitly suggested testing).

## Idea 3 (attempted but abandoned): Facebook Marketplace arbitrage via AI agent

- Concept (inspired by a reported internal Anthropic experiment having employees use Claude to buy/sell items on Craigslist): have OpenClaw scan Facebook Marketplace listings in Utah for underpriced items (e.g., specific sneaker models), cross-reference against eBay "sold" comps to identify arbitrage spreads, and autonomously message sellers to buy items for resale.
- Elaborated concept discussed on-air: agent could compare a Facebook Marketplace listing (e.g., $78) against known eBay sold prices for the same item (e.g., $128, netting ~$103 after fees), automatically message the seller, send a deposit to hold the item, alert a human to physically go pick it up, and simultaneously list it live on eBay — potentially selling before the physical pickup even happens.
- **Outcome**: started but abandoned/deprioritized in favor of the two working ideas — Brandon cites lack of personal interest/passion in buying-and-selling as the reason, plus opportunity cost given limited time and the other two ideas already showing traction. No data on whether it would have worked; explicitly flagged as unproven, not a failure.

## Key takeaways / caveats

- All three ideas were explicitly "side" experiments for an AI agency to generate proof points for client pitches, not the agency's core revenue — but the local-business-website funnel produced a genuinely substantial standalone recurring revenue result ($8K MRR in one month) from very low cash outlay.
- The single biggest driver of the standout result was combining several distinct "hard"/effortful actions (a real, functioning built website; a physical, paid mailer; a live human phone call) rather than any one of them alone — the presenters argue stacking friction-heavy, high-signal tactics compounds close rates non-linearly.
- Legal/compliance note raised in passing: mass unsolicited text messaging is explicitly called out as non-compliant and exposes senders to FCC fines/lawsuits — direct mail and live calling were framed as the safer, more "legitimate-signaling" alternative to spammy automated outreach.
- No claim is made that this specific $8K/month result is guaranteed reproducible at scale — it's presented as an early, small-sample pilot (350 targets, ~17-20 conversions) that the operator planned to iterate on monthly rather than immediately scale to a much larger target list.
