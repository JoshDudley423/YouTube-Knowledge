# 40 Ways to Make $400,000 from Unwanted Land
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cvs_HhGD18o | Published: 2026-01-17

Solo episode filmed on the host's own rural property, running through dozens of concrete ways to monetize raw/undeveloped or "unloved" land. Very idea-dense; most items include specific dollar figures from the host's own experience or from named acquaintances' businesses.

## Camping / tent site rental
- List quiet, unimproved spots (no hookups/electricity/plumbing needed) on peer-to-peer camping marketplaces for $10-50/night.
- Platforms named: Campspot (largest), Tentrr, Campspace, Harvest Hosts.

## Vehicle/equipment parking on unused land
- Rent out unused space (even a side yard) for parking cars, trucks, boats, RVs.
- Platforms named: Truck Parking Club, Neighbor.com, Spacer, Stowit, CurbFlip, Pavement (also referenced generally as "Airbnb for parking lots").
- Heavy equipment parking specifically: local contractors/excavator operators pay $80-250/month per spot to park equipment; example math: an acre (43,000 sq ft) could generate six figures/year from truck/equipment parking. Larger operators (with multiple flatbeds, box trailers, cement trucks) are typically also willing to pay for space alone and often want it fenced.

## Portable self-storage on leased land
- Case study (friend's business, Canada): secured a 3-4 parcel plot on a busy road via a lease-with-purchase-option agreement (pay monthly rent for a year, then buy the land above asking price only if the business proves out, or walk away with the portable containers).
- Sourced pricing intel from CubeSmart and SquareFoot (e.g., 10x10 storage unit renting for ~$130/month in his market).
- Became fully booked within 6 months; now replicating the model ("copy-pasting the playbook") across multiple locations; described as a six-figure, largely passive business.
- Advantages cited: financeable via equipment loans, attractive to private investors because containers can be repossessed if the deal fails, low capital risk vs. traditional ground-up self-storage development.

## Pallet reclamation business
- Businesses collect free wood pallets from local warehouses/businesses (who'd otherwise pay hundreds/month for dumpster removal), repair/clean them (crowbar, hammer, replace broken boards), and resell for $5-12 each — described as ~100% gross margin (before gas, delivery, and labor time).
- To find customers/suppliers: door-to-door, cold call/email/text local businesses that consistently discard pallets, or find businesses needing a reliable pallet supply (shipping out more than they receive).
- Tangential idea: build furniture (e.g., a bar) from reclaimed pallets and sell it, or rent it out for events (e.g., weddings, at hundreds of dollars per event).

## Custom home / high-end short-term rental + event/photography space
- Example: a custom home on the host's land rents for $600-950/night on Airbnb; only ~20% occupancy needed to cover the mortgage (6 rental nights/month).
- Cross-list simultaneously across multiple platforms to maximize occupancy: VRBO, Airbnb, Peerspace (rents space by the hour to photographers/videographers as a cheaper alternative to a professional studio).
- ~30% of the host's renters used the property for weddings; wedding venues alone can run $3,000+ for half a day, covering a full month's mortgage payment in a single event — suggests also listing on wedding-specific platforms: Zola, TheKnot.com, WeddingWire.

## Firewood / hardwood / barbecue wood business
- Firewood sells for $400-900/cord depending on season/region.
- Hardwoods (oak, maple, pecan) are more valuable than softwoods (pine); barbecue-smoking wood can fetch more than plain firewood — host is a barbecue enthusiast and runs a firewood business himself.
- Free raw material sourcing: partnered with local tree trimming companies (including his own) to receive free drop-offs of cut trees instead of them paying disposal/dump fees — a mutually beneficial arrangement since land for storage is cheap relative to what's stored on it.
- Hires teenagers to split/stack wood using a hydraulic log splitter.
- Land with mature hardwood trees is worth marginally more (host estimates roughly 20-30%, not dramatically more) than land without, and sellers/realtors often don't account for this in pricing.
- Equipment note: bought a 27-ton hydraulic splitter for $1,500; if you lack firewood customers, you can instead rent the splitter itself out for $80-160/day on Facebook Marketplace to people who cut down trees but don't want to buy their own splitter.

## Shed-based businesses (multiple ideas)
- Buying a basic pre-built shed (host's example: 8x18 ft for $7,200 delivered) and finishing the interior (wiring, drywall, paint, furniture — roughly $1,000 more) creates what is functionally a tiny home for ~$20,000 total, versus $50,000-100,000+ for a purpose-marketed "tiny home." Framed as a pricing/positioning arbitrage (same physical structure, different market category).
- Such a finished unit can rent for $500-1,000/month, recouping investment in a couple of years (vs. a decade+ for an equivalently priced purpose-built tiny home).
- Separate idea: assemble/build sheds on-site (using purchased plans, a table saw, and a chop saw) and sell them directly on Facebook Marketplace, including higher-margin custom jobs.
- Separate related idea: shed delivery/logistics service — cited a real example of a North Carolina friend who nets ~$600,000/year (over half as profit) by tracking Craigslist/Facebook Marketplace for people trying to give away or get rid of unwanted sheds (often tied to a home sale contingency requiring shed removal), picking them up for free (or being paid to remove them) using a flatbed truck with a forklift attachment, often pre-selling the shed via photos before even collecting it, then reselling for $400-2,000 including delivery (a key differentiator since most buyers can't self-transport a shed). This operator supplements ~30% of income by contracting with shed retailers to handle their delivery logistics ($400-500/delivery), since shed sellers themselves usually aren't set up to deliver.
- Related named business: shedramps.com — a company doing $10M+/year revenue exclusively making/importing/selling shed ramps (cited as evidence of how many adjacent micro-niches exist around a single product category).

## Land subdivision (Texas-specific but broadly applicable regulatory arbitrage)
- In Texas (and similarly in many states), a parcel over 10 acres can be subdivided into smaller lots without a full rezoning process, as long as each resulting parcel remains at least 10.01 acres — avoiding a much heavier approval/septic process required for sub-10-acre lots.
- Example (host states these are illustrative/rounded numbers, "directionally correct" not verified real figures): buy 500 acres for $500,000 with strong road frontage, subdivide into fifty 10-acre lots, sell each for $20,000 = $1M revenue, minus a few thousand dollars in surveying costs.

## Land + mobile home value-add / arbitrage
- Negotiating tactic: when a seller inflates the value of an existing mobile home on the land being sold (e.g., claiming it's worth $30,000), use specific market knowledge (host's mobile-home-park background) to recognize it's worth far less (e.g., ~$10,000) and negotiate a price reduction in exchange for having the seller remove the home.
- Value-add model: buy raw land (~$100,000), buy a used mobile home (~$30,000), pay to transport/hook it up (~$5,000) — total investment ~$135,000 — resulting combined property (land + installed home) can be worth $200,000+, since you're removing the "headache" of sourcing/connecting a home for a future buyer/renter.

## Metal building / "barndominium" rental for storage or business use
- A metal building shell (host's cost cited: ~$60,000 to build, noted as more expensive today) can be rented out for $2,500-3,500/month to a wide range of tenants: storage, small businesses (auto repair/body shops, cabinetry/wood shops, metal shops), or personal uses like pickleball facilities.
- General storage demand point: even uncooled/rural covered space has consistent demand — host cited a company paying monthly to store old electronics in one of his buildings, and general interest in storing classic cars.

## Tree farm / nursery business
- Buy or grow tree seedlings (from seed or seedling); species like live oak, shumard oak, pecan, cedar elm, and maple grow well from seed with minimal input; a 3-4 year old live oak can sell for hundreds of dollars.
- Low ongoing input needed if relying on natural rainfall; can plant thousands of seedlings per acre given their small initial size.
- Sell direct-to-retail via Facebook Marketplace/Facebook ads, or wholesale to established nursery wholesalers who don't grow their own stock.
- Same model applies to blackberry bushes, grape vines, and blueberries.
- Cost-saving tip: multi-plant nursery pots from big box stores (e.g., a $200 pot with 3 trees) can be split apart and repotted individually to triple your effective plant count for the same price.
- Upfront costs: irrigation/tubing, possibly a well (an existing well on land you buy is a bonus).

## Bitcoin/crypto mining on existing electrical infrastructure
- If land has spare electrical panel capacity, mining is a possible use, but explicitly flagged as high-risk/volatile ("100% buyer beware," host has had both very profitable and loss years).
- Requires a professional electrician (standard wall outlets insufficient) and awareness of hash rate, network difficulty, and Bitcoin price cycles; also creates noise, heat, and space constraints to plan around.

## Sports/recreation facility businesses
- **Indoor pickleball club** (host's own in-progress project): converting an existing building; also plans to add a golf simulator, cold plunge, and sauna. Explicitly flagged as only viable in non-rural areas with sufficient nearby population.
- **Private sports coaching facility / sport court**: youth sports coaches (softball, baseball, golf, tennis, pickleball, basketball, football) are paid $40-200/hour for private lessons but often lack access to gyms/schools for training space. Built a combined full pickleball court/half basketball court: 70x30 ft, 5-inch concrete with rebar and vapor barrier at $6-8/sq ft (~$14,000 for concrete alone), all-in cost about $32,000 including fencing and lighting — versus an estimated $50,000-60,000 if hiring a general contractor to coordinate subcontractors (suggesting a business opportunity in being that GC/subcontractor-coordinator for sport court builds). Potential revenue cited: $8,000-20,000/month via membership or hourly tutor fees. Google Trends shows rising search interest in "sport court." Explicitly requires proximity to population centers with kids in sports — doesn't work if too rural.
- **Portable/temporary facility testing tactic**: host used a portable, rollable pickleball court (weighs several hundred pounds, like a thin wrestling mat) to validate demand before investing in permanently renovating a building — a way to de-risk a facility business before committing capital.

## Novelty/low-cost "fun" monetization ideas
- **Hole-in-one-style golf challenge game**: inspired by a $10,000 hole-in-one challenge in New Zealand; host set up his own small-scale version at a public park ($3 for one shot, $5 for three shots, win $100 for a hole-in-one from 30 ft) using ~$100 of equipment; win rate under 5%; described as "extremely profitable."
- **Disc golf course**: cheap baskets (~$100 each on Amazon); not highly profitable on its own, but can generate donations, can be monetized via optional paid access, and disc golf vending machines can sell discs directly on-site for $20-30 each. Framed as a "fun, not just profit" business idea.
- **Backyard/neighborhood pop-up restaurant**: buy a Neapolitan pizza oven ($500-1,200), advertise via local Facebook/Instagram/HOA/Nextdoor groups for pre-ordered pickup ($10-25/pizza), reportedly can net about $1,000 for an afternoon of cooking. Same model applies to barbecue (brisket, pork butt) or Blackstone-griddle cooking; total equipment investment across these formats cited at $500-1,500 (excluding a larger dedicated grill).

## Fruit orchard business
- Referenced example: "The Peach Truck," described as a $100M business selling peaches online (unverified figure as stated in video).
- Named example (friend, Isaac French): planted a 300-tree fruit orchard in Central Texas producing "tens of thousands of pounds" of fruit annually for local sale; total investment (trees, irrigation, sidewalk/cement work) was about $75,000.
- Advises researching regional variety suitability and cross-pollination needs before planting.

## EV charging monetization
- Peer-to-peer EV charging platforms named: PlugShare, EV Match.
- Tesla will install supercharging infrastructure for free on qualifying properties (generally those near highways) but does not allow marking up the electricity price directly — property owner can only monetize adjacent foot traffic, not the electricity itself, under a Tesla-funded setup.
- If you install your own charger (optionally paired with solar), you can charge EV owners directly and keep the margin.

## Sawmill/lumber slab business
- Hire a portable ("Alaskan") sawmill operator (~$60/hour) to cut large logs (e.g., red oak, cedar, white oak) into 2-4 inch slabs; season for months to years, then sell by the board foot to woodworkers/furniture makers.
- Raw material is free if sourced the same way as the firewood business (tree trimming companies happy to drop off large logs to avoid dump fees) — land is used mainly for low-cost storage/seasoning space.

## Decomposed granite (DG) / crushed granite reselling
- Buying retail from a distributor (example named: Living Earth) costs roughly $50-70/cubic yard in Texas; buying directly from an Oklahoma quarry costs roughly $20/cubic yard.
- Business model options: (a) act as a lead-gen middleman/dropshipper — build a simple lead-capture landing page (using a tool like Replit or Lindy) and run paid ads, connecting buyers to the quarry without holding inventory, or (b) buy in bulk, store on cheap land, and resell directly with pickup or delivery.

## Septic tank repair/installation business
- Described as unglamorous but highly profitable: 20-40% net margins, largely because so few people want to enter the field, creating a persistent supply shortage and pricing power for providers (host was quoted $15,000 for a system he suspected could be repaired for much less).

## Tree relocation/transplanting middleman business
- Uses a "tree spade" (skid-steer attachment or standalone machine, ~$4,000 to own, or you can broker deals using someone else's) to remove and relocate mature trees rather than having them cut down.
- Business model: connect landowners who need mature trees removed (often for free, avoiding a "shameful" cut-down cost) with buyers who want an already-mature tree cheaper than nursery price (e.g., buyer pays $1,000 instead of $2,500 retail) — the broker/operator captures the spread between the two prices, either owning the equipment or subcontracting someone who does.

## Fiber optic cable installation and reselling internet access
- Host paid $500 for contractors with a "ditch witch" trencher to run a private fiber line to his house from an existing fiber-served road.
- Framed as a broader micro-industry: laying fiber optic cable for underserved rural areas creates very sticky customers (new homeowners inherit the internet subscription when a house sells) with a described real example of an operator making six figures/year with low overhead once the up-front trenching cost is paid.
- Adjacent business: buying/renting out ditch witches to fiber-laying contractors.

## Pond/lake cleaning service (real case study)
- Case study: college students in Chicago (interviewed separately by the host; business name "J&D Lake Services") started by hand-raking seaweed/weeds out of a lakefront homeowner's water using $20 Home Depot rakes, earning ~$500 for a few hours of labor on the first job; word-of-mouth from neighbors grew it into a six-figure/year business while still in college.
- A local equipment owner later leased them a $200,000 amphibious lake-cleaning machine with no money down, seeing their traction — illustrating that equipment financing/partnerships can follow after demonstrated demand rather than requiring big upfront capital.
- Positioning/marketing angle used: "no chemicals, all natural."
- Customer acquisition suggestion: use third-party property/address databases to identify homes with ponds or lake frontage rather than manual searching, then cold-outreach with a "no one's ever offered to clean your pond before" pitch; can charge hundreds of dollars per hour.
- Explicitly described as physically difficult, unglamorous work (e.g., leeches) but a "brand new industry" with minimal existing competition.

## Pool rental (Swimply-style) business
- Platforms named: Swimply, Swimmy, Resort Pass — let pool owners rent out their backyard pool by the hour/day, similar to Airbnb for pools.
- In warm markets (Phoenix, San Diego, LA), some operators buy/rent a house purely to run this business (not to live in), adding revenue via catering, drinks, lifeguard service, and pool toys; some reportedly earn six figures/year.

## Culvert (drainage pipe) buying/reselling business
- Host bought two 30-inch diameter culverts for his own property ($900) and separately found city-surplus culverts being discarded for free (simply asked and was allowed to take them), then resold them on Facebook Marketplace for a combined total in the hundreds to low thousands of dollars.
- Identified demand signal via Google Keyword Planner / Google Trends showing active search volume for culverts, attributed partly to the post-COVID rural/farm-lifestyle property boom.
- Business model: source from manufacturers or municipal surplus, store cheaply on land, list on Facebook Marketplace (boost with paid ads if organic traction is low), offer pickup or paid delivery (extra $200-300).
- Named this idea "Culvert City."

## RV pad / single-pad "micro RV park"
- Installing even a single 30-50 amp RV plug lets you list a spot on RV-focused booking platforms: Campspot, The Dyrt, Boondockers Welcome, Campendium, Harvest Hosts.
- Can scale incrementally by adding more pads/plugs, then a septic system or city sewer connection, eventually becoming a full RV park — host states he has been buying RV parks for 7 years.

## High-traffic road frontage monetization ("attention arbitrage")
- Host bought a narrow, awkwardly-shaped lot on a busy road for $160,000 (below its ~$300,000 value at the time) specifically because of heavy traffic (~22,000 cars/day) most buyers viewed as a negative; the lot has since appreciated to $750,000, outperforming comparable properties, attributed to the road-frontage advantage.
- Practical monetization of traffic: parking a wrapped business truck or a for-sale vehicle roadside increases visibility/selling price purely from additional "eyeballs" — analogized to how a tweet from a large account (e.g., Elon Musk) can sell an identical item for a much higher price purely due to greater audience size (fixed supply + higher demand = higher price).
- Case study (host's own prior business, "Presto Cars," a concierge car-buying service): bought cars at wholesale prices from Manheim/Adesa dealer auctions, parked them roadside with a for-sale sign leveraging the high-traffic lot, and resold at a markup (example: bought for $13,000, sold for $17,000); doing this once a week was cited as generating about $16,000/month net profit, with real operational headaches (cars running out of gas, breaking down).
- General framing: for a residential-zoned lot with high traffic that most buyers see as a negative (noise, privacy loss), it can instead be monetized via billboards, vehicle sales, signage, wrapped vehicles, farm stands, or informal roadside sales.

## Financing/legal notes for land-based businesses
- **USDA Rural Development Loan**: 0%-down, no PMI, government-backed loan available in eligible rural areas (map available online) with decent-but-not-perfect credit; interest rates lower than FHA; can only be used to build/improve a structure on the land (stick-built, modular, or manufactured homes appear to qualify) — cannot be used for raw undeveloped land with no plans to build.
- **Master lease agreements**: used by the host on some RV park deals — make lease payments to the landowner while keeping all cash flow above the lease payment; can include an option to purchase the property/business later (1, 3, or 5 years) at a predetermined price if performance justifies it — a way to reduce risk before committing capital to a full purchase.
