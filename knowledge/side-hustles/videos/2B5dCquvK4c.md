# If We Had to Make $1M Fast, We'd Start These Businesses
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2B5dCquvK4c | Published: 2025-08-05

Chris Koerner and Nik (Nikonomics Podcast) brainstorm/pitch multiple business ideas under the constraint: "build a $1M business in 6 months." Rapid-fire idea format; several distinct ideas below.

## Idea 1: RV rental arbitrage
- You can finance an RV over 20-30 years because it's classified like a home; a $35-40K Class C RV (motorhome) can have a monthly payment of only ~$200-300.
- Rents out for $200-500/night depending on market; one or two rental nights covers the monthly payment.
- **Validation process** (used by Koerner personally, pre-purchase): compare occupancy/demand on RV Share and Outdoorsy across RV classes in your local market (search near-term and a year out, track results in a spreadsheet) to identify which RV class has the most demand before buying. Koerner's own research pointed clearly to Class C RVs in his (Dallas-Fort Worth) market; he emailed the manufacturer to confirm the trend, which they corroborated.
- **Numbers to scale to $1M-adjacent target** ($300K/6mo mentioned as one bar): keeping one RV rented ~50% of the time generates ~$7,000/month topline, ~$5,000/month profit if self-managed (renter pays gas/mileage/damage repairs; your cost is just the loan payment + storage, ~$150/month per RV to store, e.g. $1,000/month for 5 RVs). 5 RVs at that rate ≈ $25K/month profit; 10 RVs ≈ $50K/month.
- Financing multiple RVs (aiming for 10) is feasible but likely requires spreading across multiple banks/credit unions rather than one lender approving all 10; credit unions worked best for Koerner.
- **Caveat**: doesn't scale well past 1-2 units if self-managed (cleaning, turnover, customer meet-ups) — only makes sense as a real business at 5-10+ units where you can hire help; highly location-dependent (works only where local demand data supports it).

## Idea 2: "30 wrappers in 30 days" — AI app-building agency
- Inspired by a guest (Billy Howell) who has no coding background but built an agency creating AI "wrapper" apps for clients using Replit/Lovable-style tools, reportedly earning ~$60K/month.
- Suggested approach: spend 30 days building one small AI wrapper app per day around a real pain point (quality doesn't matter at this stage), then review which had the most promise, and spend the next stretch productizing 1-2 of the best ones — either listing on an app store or selling directly to local businesses with that pain point as a SaaS product.
- Key point raised: "most people listening probably know 60-80% as much as [the guest]" — the differentiator isn't technical skill but finishing the "last 10%" (deployment/polish) and doing the hard work of finding and selling to customers.

## Idea 3: Offshore staffing / recruiting agency
- Two staffing models: (a) ongoing placement/management — mark up a $1,200/month Philippines VA to $2,500/month, handle payroll, recurring revenue but requires ongoing hand-holding; (b) headhunting — place a candidate for a one-time fee of ~20-35% of their first-year salary, no recurring revenue but paid upfront and you "wash your hands of it."
- For a 6-month $1M sprint, the speaker would choose headhunting/placement fees (upfront cash) over the recurring model.
- **Sourcing/vetting**: find candidates via Upwork, vet over Zoom, must be "AI-native" (fluent using ChatGPT at minimum) as a non-negotiable qualification.
- **Client acquisition**: DM hiring managers at companies needing to hire 10-100 overseas roles; alternatively, find a small business already selling to hiring managers with an existing CRM of leads and offer them a referral fee (10-20% of your ~$4,000 average placement fee, i.e. $400-800/referral, or a $4,000 bonus for 10 placements).
- **Alternative low-cost model**: apply directly to job postings as if you were a candidate (or automate the applications), then reveal you're actually an agency that can fill the role, and go find/place a candidate on the back end.
- General framing: sitting on Upwork/Facebook Marketplace all day hunting for deals/opportunities is described as a low-capital, "AI-proof" business requiring only relationship-building skill, not money or technical skill — though the speakers note it requires a specific "hustler" personality suited to high-volume opportunistic dealmaking.

## Idea 4: Hyper-niche AI audit/savings agency for large corporations
- Target large corporations (nine-figure+ revenue) and find one specific costly inefficiency to automate/fix — examples cited: a company spending $20,000/month manually creating employee CRM profiles (automatable via HR system integration, saving ~$17K/month = ~$200K+/year); a benefits department finding ~$5,000/month in billing errors (e.g., being billed for terminated employees, wrong payroll deductions) after auditing for the first time in 7 years.
- **Pricing models discussed**: (a) charge 50% of first-year savings, sales objection = fluctuating bills feel risky to clients; (b) price a flat/predictable retainer calibrated to roughly equal that same value, without explicitly tying it to "50% of savings" — companies prefer predictable billing over variable bills even when the variable model is a net win for them.
- **Case example cited**: an 18-year-old (Ben Weiss, Swiss) built a business purely by cold-approaching Fortune 100 executives in person with the pitch "I can use AI to save your company money — if I can't, I'll donate $1,000 to a charity of your choice," reportedly closing ~30-60% of in-person pitches for six-figure value per client, without formal corporate experience — just using ChatGPT.
- **How to find these opportunities**: requires a specialized skill/insider knowledge of an industry's back-office pain points (payroll, accounting, IT, benefits admin) to identify where money is being lost, then build a "plug-and-play" audit + automation offer targeted at similar large organizations, potentially using a savings calculator as an outbound sales tool.

## Idea 5: Rolling up dormant email newsletters
- Newsletter operators frequently burn out and abandon their lists (similar to podcasters); dormant newsletters (3,000-30,000 subscribers, same niche) can be bought cheaply because the owner has already mentally written them off.
- **Sourcing**: use public directories on Beehiiv, Kit (ConvertKit), and Substack to find newsletters that haven't sent an email in 1+ month; can automate discovery with AI agent tools (ChatGPT Agent/Operator, Manus, Perplexity Comet) to build a spreadsheet of dormant newsletters by niche.
- **Numbers**: newsletter ad revenue is priced on CPM (cost per thousand *opens*, not subscribers) — a "good, conservative" CPM cited as ~$30/1,000 opens; example: 10,000 subscribers x 50% open rate = 5,000 opens x $30 CPM = $150 per send; weekly cadence = ~$600/month per newsletter from ads alone (excludes affiliate revenue or a ~1% conversion to a $10/month paid tier).
- **Buyout offer structure suggested**: pay $0.10/subscriber upfront, with an escalator to $1.00/subscriber if certain milestones are hit later; alternatively acquire on pure contingency/seller-finance (pay nothing upfront, give the seller a percentage of whatever revenue you generate going forward) — framed as low-risk to the seller since they've already abandoned the asset.
- **Scaling variant**: convert acquired weekly newsletters to daily cadence to multiply revenue (~5x send frequency), though CPM may need to drop somewhat (e.g. $50 CPM daily vs. $30 weekly) since sponsors get more inventory/scale; requires finding enough sponsors to support the higher frequency.
- **Lower-effort variant (broker model)**: instead of operating the newsletter yourself, acquire dormant lists cheaply in a specific vertical/geography, then resell/broker the aggregated subscriber list to an existing marketing agency serving that same niche — avoids the operational burden of running a newsletter yourself, at a lower per-unit payout.
- Niches with sponsors embedded in their own subscriber base (e.g. a marketing newsletter with marketing-exec subscribers) are called out as especially favorable since you can monetize by selling sponsorships to your own readers.

## Idea 6: AI automation agency using "Hero AI Workflows" (Nik's framework + Chris's real-world execution)
- **Hero workflow definition**: a single automation that is high-impact but low-complexity, easy to explain, fast to implement, with a clear before/after ROI story (example given: automating missed-call follow-up texts for home service companies, recovering ~15% of otherwise-lost leads).
- **Research method for finding a hero workflow** (Nik's 4-part framework): (1) Pain — clear operational cost/stress; (2) Access — can you actually reach decision-makers in that niche; (3) Infrastructure — target businesses should already use some digital tools but not too many (a "Goldilocks zone"); (4) Dollar savings — verify real, quantifiable savings potential.
- **Chris's live case study**: built a 24/7 AI voice-answering agent for garage door repair companies using GoHighLevel's "AI Employee" feature — built in 6 clicks / ~3 minutes, using ChatGPT to write the knowledge-base script. Chose garage doors specifically because it's high-ticket ($500-$3,000/job), has after-hours emergency calls, and (unlike plumbing/HVAC/electrical) isn't already saturated with private-equity-backed automation. Scraped every garage door repair company in the DFW area, texted them cold, and closed a paying customer in 57 minutes (9:41am to 10:38am).
- **Pricing pitch used**: offered pay-per-lead (only pay once a lead closes) as the initial hook to reduce buyer risk.
- **Business model proposed to scale to $1M**: pick 3 niches, build one hero (plug-and-play, not custom) workflow per niche, sell a $3,000 setup fee + a monthly retainer, with upsells layered in afterward.
- **Market size cited**: ~32 million small businesses in the US, most without employees, many not answering their phones at all (especially after 5pm) — described as a durable, still-early ("blue ocean") opportunity likely to remain viable for years.
- Referenced comparable prior guest (episode 133) who raised venture capital to build an AI call-answering service for small businesses — framed as validating the category, while noting a solo operator can now approximate similar capability via no-code tools (GoHighLevel) in a fraction of the time/cost.

## Idea 7: Liquidation / reselling (govdeals.com, B-Stock/Costco liquidation, Facebook Marketplace)
- Buy surplus/returned goods at ~5 cents on the dollar (via GovDeals, B-Stock, Costco liquidation channels), resell at 30-40 cents on the dollar.
- Cited example: a first-time flipper (referred to as "Dylan," Logan, Utah) bought ~10 treadmills for $500 total, resold at ~$200 each — sold out within about 2 weeks despite initial doubt.
- Recommends studying an existing expert's playbook (cited: "Shannon," featured 8x on Koerner's podcast) rather than improvising blind — start with cheap, low-risk pallets before scaling capital committed.
- Financing shortcut suggested: put initial inventory purchases on a credit card (framed loosely/jokingly, not as formal advice).
- **Adjacent variant — restaurant equipment liquidation as pure broker**: search Facebook Marketplace for "business" to find businesses closing/liquidating not listed elsewhere; specifically target failing restaurants and offer to take/place all their equipment (fryers, etc. — high-ticket, tens of thousands of dollars), then list the equipment on eBay/restaurant-equipment marketplaces and sell it *before* ever taking possession/paying for it — a middleman/broker model requiring no inventory holding.

## Idea 8: Glamping site rollup in underserved markets
- Model is directly analogous to the RV rental idea: identify a geographic market with high glamping demand but insufficient existing supply (validate by comparing Airbnb search demand vs. current listings, near-term and a year out, in target markets), then build/acquire glamping tent sites there.
- Real example noted: Island Park, Idaho had glamping sites fully booked on one side of town and zero glamping supply on the other side, an identified gap.
- **Numbers**: glamping tents obtained via a distressed/discount deal at ~90% off (~$1,500 vs. normal $15,000 retail per unit, from a liquidation-style contact). Land in the example market costs roughly $150-200K per acre or ~$500K for 5 acres — deemed a lot of upfront capital.
- **Structuring the land cost down**: lease-purchase or lease-with-option-to-buy raw land instead of buying outright (cited real example: someone leased land for portable self-storage at $3,000/month with a 1-year option to purchase, then exercised the purchase option only after the business was validated and had increased the land's value). Suggested framing for a landowner: offer to install a permanent concrete pad (~$7/sq ft, e.g. $3,500 for 500 sq ft) and septic system (~$20,000) on their land at your cost, with the stipulation that if you don't renew/buy after a year, the landowner keeps those improvements for free — makes it an easy yes for the landowner.
- **De-risking further**: skip the bathroom/septic infrastructure investment initially (most glamping sites don't include a private bathroom anyway) — launch bare-bones, use guest feedback/lost bookings as validated evidence of exactly how much a bathroom would increase demand before investing in septic.
- **Caveat**: full build-out (pad + septic + tent delivery) estimated to take ~6 months minimum, which is a tight fit against a 6-month "$1M" timeline; a lower-capital alternative is to find someone who already owns land with septic/infrastructure in place and broker/lease a deal with them rather than building from scratch.

## Idea 9 (bonus, quick pitch): B2B HVAC coil-cleaning service at grocery stores
- Pressure-washing/cleaning the outdoor coils on commercial HVAC units, marketed narrowly to grocery stores specifically (a guest on the podcast does this) — framed as extremely low customer churn (near zero, described as never losing a grocery store client), high margin, no real competition, because it's a maintenance task HVAC companies find annoying to do themselves (they have to pull out a pressure washer they don't otherwise use) so they neglect or outsource it. General principle stated: "find the pain point nobody wants to do but has to be done, and unbundle it" as a specialized recurring service.

## Idea 10 (bonus, quick pitch): "Bingo Loco"-style themed live events for other activity niches
- Reference case: "Bingo Loco" events (DJ, neon lights, prizes, alcohol/food sales) draw ~1,000 attendees at ~$40/ticket (~$40K door revenue per event) plus food/alcohol sales, traveling city to city, marketed via Instagram/Facebook ads.
- Proposed idea: apply the same "packaging/marketing, not the underlying activity" formula to other niches (e.g. portable pickle ball courts) — validate demand *before* investing in equipment by running targeted Facebook ads (to a specific demographic, e.g. ages 35-50 interested in pickleball) advertising a ticketed event at a proposed venue, see if enough tickets presell; refund and abandon if demand doesn't materialize, proceed to rent/buy equipment only if it does.
- General principle: the underlying activity (bingo, pickleball, etc.) isn't the innovation — the event packaging/atmosphere and geographic/format arbitrage (bringing an established concept to underserved secondary markets) is where the opportunity lies.

## Meta-frameworks and general advice repeated throughout
- Always validate demand with data (spreadsheet tracking of search/booking demand on existing marketplaces, or presales via targeted ads) before spending capital on physical assets.
- "Learn by building" — several speakers favor spending 30 days actually building over 30 days planning/researching, arguing you only really learn a tool/skill by doing it and iterating, with research/strategy refinement coming after initial reps.
- Buying equity/conditional-equity in an existing small business in exchange for implementing a specific high-leverage automation or process improvement (rather than either buying the whole business outright or just billing hourly/retainer as a service) is presented as a high-risk/high-reward path — cited example: a friend (Hector) negotiating a small equity stake (e.g. 5%) in a one-man heavy-equipment repair business (real cited numbers: owner nets $350K/year charging $125/hour vs. $300/hour market rate in Houston, fully booked) in exchange for adding systems/staff/pricing improvements, without buying the business outright — compared explicitly to Alex Hormozi's early playbook of taking equity stakes in small businesses and improving them (e.g., via ad spend) as a growth-equity value-add rather than straight acquisition.
