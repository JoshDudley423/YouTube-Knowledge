# How to Build a Chat GPT Wrapper (Real Success Story)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dPLAvI6wGkY | Published: 2025-02-19

Interview with Jordan Gal, founder of Hey Rosie, an AI voice-answering service ("GPT wrapper") for small businesses. Business was ~6 months old at time of recording.

## The business
- Hey Rosie is an AI phone agent that answers calls for small/local businesses -- positioned as a much better, much cheaper alternative to (a) voicemail and (b) traditional human answering services.
- Traditional answering services cost roughly $2/minute and use rotating staff (sometimes 10-50 different people depending on time/day) who often lack real knowledge of the specific business, giving inconsistent/poor answers to anything outside a handful of common questions.
- Rosie is positioned as transitional between "answering service" and full "receptionist" -- not a full human replacement, but far better conversion than voicemail, moving toward human-level competence over time as underlying voice AI improves.
- Core use cases: (1) large home-service/trades businesses (e.g., water remediation) needing after-hours call triage with conditional call transfers (e.g., "try line 1 for Billy, then line 2 for Jack" for urgent jobs) and (2) solo/small owner-operators who don't want to be interrupted by every call (e.g., a $33k concrete job lead vs. spam) -- Rosie takes the message, sends a text transcript/summary so the owner decides whether to act immediately.
- Transparency choice: businesses can choose whether Rosie announces itself as an AI upfront or not -- no single right answer; depends on audience/demographic. Feature request from a large gym franchise client: let callers opt into finishing the interaction by text instead of voice.
- Adding scam/spam detection, appointment setting, live call transfers, and custom agent training as premium features to avoid being commoditized as "just X minutes for $Y."

## Numbers / traction (as of ~6 months in)
- Growth: doubling every month for several consecutive months at time of recording.
- Churn: low single digits (monthly), far lower than the founder's own conservative ~20% projection -- attributed to how painful the underlying problem is (missed calls costs real money), so customers tolerate rough edges rather than switching back.
- Customer acquisition cost: ~$50.
- Pricing (at time of recording): $49/month for 250 minutes, $99/month for 500 minutes -- roughly $0.20/minute effective, with feature differences (e.g., appointment setting) increasingly used to differentiate tiers instead of raw minutes.
- Underlying voice AI cost: cited around $0.13-0.15/minute wholesale for voice calling -- meaning gross margins on voice AI products are notably worse than typical SaaS (compare: a previous company of the founder's, CartHook, spent ~$5,000/month on AWS while doing $500k/month revenue -- a far better margin profile).
- Customer mix breakdown, which surprised the founder: ~50% home services (HVAC, plumbing, electrical, landscaping -- landscaping specifically was an unexpected high performer), and the other ~50% is a "wild west" of general small businesses (property management, event/party businesses, and any business where the phone still matters) -- NOT limited to home services as originally assumed.
- Currently acquiring customers via paid social ads (Instagram, Facebook, TikTok) -- founder notes he'd historically never had B2B ad success before, but the broad small-business market and the algorithm's targeting made cold paid acquisition work here specifically.

## Process / how it was built and launched
- Founder's method for choosing what to build: browsed Y Combinator's public list of recently funded companies specifically to find AI ideas, filtering for criteria: (1) something customers would pay real money for with clear ROI, (2) a technology that was "not good yet" but improving fast (early voice AI at the time), (3) a big enough market to sell self-serve (rejecting Enterprise sales cycles, which he disliked from a prior company).
- Deliberately built as a thin layer ("wrapper") on top of existing AI infrastructure/voice providers rather than building the underlying tech from scratch -- explicit philosophy: "don't get enamored with the tech, get enamored with the problem and the solution," then find which new tech now makes solving that problem possible.
- Explicitly avoided platform dependency risk: a previous company (CartHook, an e-commerce checkout tool built on Shopify, reached ~$6M ARR) was effectively killed when Shopify chose to shut down the functionality it depended on -- motivated him to build Rosie without being beholden to one dominant platform.
- Onboarding flow designed for near-zero friction before signup: prospect enters their business name on the website, the tool auto-pulls their Google Business Profile (logo, info) and website content, generates a working AI agent trained on that data, and lets them literally call and hear their own business name spoken by the AI -- all within about 10 seconds, before requiring an account. Account creation happens only after this "wow" moment.
- Deliberately kept configuration options minimal (currently ships with one AI voice, a few tone settings) despite competitors offering many more voices -- rationale: avoid feature creep on things customers aren't actually asking for; simplicity is treated as a form of product value ("distillation of what matters is part of our value-add," compared to Airbnb's design philosophy of hiding complexity from the end user).
- Considered a growth-hacking tactic (not yet executed at time of interview): scrape phone numbers off Google Maps/Business Profiles, call them after-hours by local time zone, and any call that hits voicemail is treated as a qualified target for a Rosie-recorded outbound pitch voicemail ("eat your own dog food").

## Adjacent business ideas the founder identified but isn't pursuing
- AI sales coaching / "ridealong sales manager": use an AI transcription layer (mentioned tool: Fathom, for recorded calls/Zoom) on live sales conversations -- e.g., a pest control company's door-to-door salesmen -- to give the business owner visibility into how salespeople actually represent the company and close deals, without the owner needing to be present. Could anonymize transcripts so reps aren't afraid of surveillance, sending owners only coaching notes ("he could have upsold X before leaving") rather than raw transcripts.
- Mentioned wearable hardware (the "Rewind" pendant) as a potential enabler of more such ridealong-recording products in future, though noted as more consumer-focused currently.
- General framing: because AI opens new categories rapidly, going broad/horizontal (not narrowly niching to one vertical) can make sense right now, since the market itself (small businesses needing better phone handling) is large and still forming, unlike a mature market where deep niching would normally be the smarter play.

## Caveats explicitly raised
- "Just because it's a wrapper doesn't mean it's easy" -- software is still hard, product-market fit is still elusive; a wrapper approach gets you moving faster, it's not a shortcut to success.
- Voice AI COGS (cost per minute) falls fast over time -- price based on where costs will be in the future, not current costs, or margins will be crushed as competitors undercut once tech gets cheaper; expect to "eat bad margins now for good margins later."
- Avoid pricing purely on a commodity metric (raw minutes) -- once customers start asking things like "do unused minutes roll over," you've been pulled into commodity/cell-phone-plan-style competition; differentiate on features/outcomes instead.
- The founder is explicitly skeptical of the "easy wrapper app" hype around niche iPhone apps (e.g., sourdough guides) promoted heavily on social media -- describes going through the "emotional rollercoaster" of chasing shiny objects and cautions against assuming those are as easy or lucrative as portrayed; he personally passed on that category due to unfamiliarity with App Store dynamics and platform risk.
