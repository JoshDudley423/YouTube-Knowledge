# Don't Quit Your Job... Yet. Watch This First
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6XhabeLLuEw | Published: 2025-06-30

Solo episode by Chris Koerner — not a business idea video, but direct guidance on the "should I quit my job to go all-in on a business" decision. Included here because it's actionable advice relevant to anyone running a side hustle toward full-time.

## Core framework: "burn the boats" vs. the "wing walker's code"
- Two competing entrepreneurship philosophies Chris holds simultaneously: (1) "burn the boats" — eliminate your backup plan so plan A has to succeed (cites Dude Perfect's origin story: the group only broke out after the 3 members with day jobs quit them entirely to go all-in, which they identify as their single biggest inflection point); (2) the "wing walker's code" from an entrepreneurship class — never let go of one handhold until you have the next one, i.e., don't torch your safety net.
- Chris's resolution: both are situationally correct. It depends on your risk tolerance, experience in the field, whether you could realistically get your job back if the business fails, how competitive the market is, and whether you have dependents/a family to support.
- Personal examples: Chris's first "burn the boats" moment wasn't voluntary — a soured deal to sell his first business (Phone Restore) at 25 cut his income to zero overnight; the desperation/chip-on-shoulder drove him to build his next business into his first million-dollar success. Conversely, when he has one very profitable business as a safety net, side projects he's merely "tinkering with" tend to fizzle when they hit friction (example: Presto Cars, a concierge car-buying service that was working but he quit because he didn't need it to work).
- Analogy to personal habit change: Chris lost 60 lbs on keto by treating carbs like alcohol (something he'd decided since childhood was never an option, due to his LDS faith) — removing the option entirely removed the temptation. He explicitly notes this isn't a universal cure for addiction, just illustrates how eliminating optionality changes behavior.

## The actionable advice: how to "burn the boats" without reckless risk
- When people ask if they should quit a well-paying job (example used: "John," $180K/year corporate job, wants to build a franchiseable mini donut cart business) to chase a new business, Chris's answer is almost always no — quitting immediately is not what he recommends, despite people wanting permission to do exactly that.
- Instead, recommends "burning the boats on the commitment level, not the paycheck level": set an explicit revenue goal and timeline; if hit, quit; if not, stay employed. Concretely, for the example business:
  1. Months 1-3: nights/weekends learning everything about the industry before spending real money.
  2. Months 4-5: buy used/cheap equipment (e.g., a mini-donut cart off eBay) instead of financing a large buildout (e.g., don't finance $200K for a storefront).
  3. Month 6: practice/operate the equipment in your own space (e.g., garage) on your own time, perfecting the process, still employed.
  4. Month 7: test the real market on weekends at fairs/festivals/farmers markets while still employed — this is described as the real test of whether you're suited to the business.
  5. Month 9: if the numbers work, reinvest — buy a second unit, hire first employee — still without quitting the day job.
  6. Only quit once the business has already proven itself financially through this process — at that point quitting doesn't feel like "burning boats," it feels like the obvious next step.
- Explicitly contrasts this slow, methodical path (60-hour weeks: 40 at the day job + 20+ nights/weekends, no external glamour or "entrepreneur" identity payoff) against the emotionally satisfying but riskier move of quitting outright for the dopamine hit of telling people "I'm an entrepreneur now."

## Caveats / risks
- Chris is explicit that he's never personally had to quit a high-paying "big awesome job" himself, and is therefore hesitant to advise someone else to do so blindly — especially anyone with a family depending on the income.
- Burning the boats "done right" should feel like an obvious, inevitable decision by the time you make it — not a leap of blind faith or a poorly calculated gamble.
- No numeric business data in this video — it's advice/mindset content, not a specific business teardown.
