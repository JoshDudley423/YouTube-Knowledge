# How This Entrepreneur Makes Over $10m per Year Selling Cabin Kits
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lr5xbVaf5B0 | Published: 2024-05-13

Live call-in format ("Koerner's Corner" style episode 13). Chris Koerner takes four caller questions, each about a different existing business seeking growth advice. Notes organized by caller/topic.

## 1. Growing a licensed private "Acton Academy" school (Amarillo, Texas)
- Business model: a licensed/affiliate model (similar structure to CrossFit's box-affiliate model) — the Acton Academy network (started ~12 years ago in Austin by Jeff Sandefer, based on a fast-track/no-frills Acton MBA program) lets local operators run a "learner-driven" school (Socratic guides instead of teachers, self-directed students) using licensed materials, with zero curriculum control from the parent network (only ~300 Acton schools worldwide, run by a skeleton crew).
- Numbers: started 2020 (mid-COVID) with 4 families/6 students; now 20 students at $8,500/year tuition, ~$170,000/year revenue; had some family attrition (cited: middle-school-age families wanting a larger peer group than the school's small cohort could offer). Caller's stated growth target: 60 students (in a city of 200,000) — compared to Austin (population ~1M) supporting 6-7 Acton locations, several with waitlists, at a higher $15-20K tuition tied to Austin's higher private-school price norms (~$30-40K for traditional private school there).
- Funnel diagnosis: website lead magnet gets ~3-4 information requests/week, funneled through a 3-email drip (one email every 2 days) then a follow-up call attempt about a week after the last email — explicitly identified as too slow ("the biggest red flag... someone's really excited... and then they don't speak to a human for over a week"). Phone-call scheduling conversion from the funnel was very low, and even those who did schedule calls were often just asking basic clarifying questions (e.g., confirming it's a paid private school), not highly qualified leads.
- Recommended fixes: (1) shrink the intake form to just name/phone/email (remove all other fields) to reduce friction; (2) contact new leads immediately/same-day rather than waiting a week — "every second that goes by... they get colder and colder"; (3) start using Google Ads specifically (recommended over Facebook ads) because Google search captures higher-intent queries (e.g., "alternative schools near me," "unhappy with my school") — advised learning the basics directly via YouTube tutorials or a cheap ($50) Udemy course rather than hiring an agency at this stage, since an 80/20 self-taught approach should be sufficient at this scale; (4) directly call other Acton Academy affiliate operators (the network is described as unusually generous/collaborative, akin to how CrossFit box owners help each other) to shortcut common early mistakes; (5) explicitly study high-ticket direct-response marketing funnels ("cringy" Instagram course-guru style operations, e.g., a cited $12K short-term-rental mastermind reportedly doing $20-30M/year) and reverse-engineer their lead-to-call speed and follow-up cadence, since those operators have heavily optimized exactly this kind of high-consideration-purchase funnel.
- Caller's Net Promoter Score reported at 4.5/5 (can't legally operate below 4.0 per network rules), suggesting the retention/satisfaction problem isn't the product but the top-of-funnel conversion process.

## 2. DIY log cabin ("bunkie") kit business — high-ticket e-commerce
- Business: sells small DIY log cabin/bunkhouse kits ($6,000-$20,000 per kit) shipped across North America; target customer is 40-65 years old, lives in a city but owns rural/lake property, upper-middle-class, wants extra guest space for family gatherings. ~85% of buyers successfully self-assemble using provided manuals/build videos/support (described as "Lincoln Logs for adults"). Foundation can be as simple as gravel/patio stones or a full concrete pad; electrical is DIY-feasible, plumbing typically requires permits so many customers skip it.
- Numbers: currently on track for $11-12M annual revenue run rate, ~900 units sold last year, targeting ~1,000 this year (average selling price roughly $11-12K). Email list of ~350,000 (acknowledged as mostly low-intent "chaff" from a giveaway funnel) with a monthly newsletter to the full list. Refund rate on deposits is low (~1 in 30). Payment structure: 65% deposit upfront (funds production), 35% due a day or two before delivery; typical sales cycle is highly variable (as short as 1-20 days for a "hot" buyer, but can stretch out much longer — some customers reported following the brand's content for 2 years before buying).
- Primary acquisition channel: paid social (pay-per-click) feeding into an annual "win this bunkie" 30-day giveaway/contest funnel with an educational live-stream series (e.g., on foundations, staining) that warms up entrants — described as producing a large concentration of sales in the week right after the contest closes. Group sales webinars/calls have had poor attendance (e.g., 50 sign-ups yielding 1 attendee), a problem they were still trying to solve.
- B2B channel (campgrounds, wedding venues, trailer parks buying bunkies) exists organically through the B2C funnel but has not been deliberately built out — flagged as an untapped opportunity.
- Sales team structure discussed: currently a "Sherpa model" (a few long-tenured salespeople each handle a customer through the entire journey — factory tour, sale, delivery coordination) alongside a more segmented setup for other reps (outbound callers in the Philippines making ~130 calls/day purely to set appointments; separate people to host virtual/in-person factory tours; a UK-based remote closer). Caller was weighing whether to fully specialize into a "Setter → Closer → Fulfiller" (baton) model, common in high-ticket direct-response/course-sales funnels, versus continuing the generalist Sherpa approach.
- Advice given: (1) A/B test North American appointment setters against the existing Philippines-based team, since for a purchase this large the marginal wage savings from offshore staff are likely outweighed by small conversion-rate gains from culturally-native phrasing/rapport (suggested testing side-by-side call volume and close rates); (2) lean toward the specialized Setter/Closer/Fulfiller model (again referencing high-ticket "guru" funnels as the proven template) specifically because, unlike a subscription-style product needing years of ongoing handholding, a cabin kit customer's relationship effectively ends once the structure is delivered — making a hand-off/specialized pipeline more appropriate than a single-relationship-manager (Sherpa) model.
- No directly analogous competitor was identified in their specific niche (shed sellers are the closest but described as far less structured/professional); most useful comparisons drawn were to other high-ticket "outdoor living" categories like hot tubs and boats.

## 3. Shopify app offering clippable digital coupons on product pages
- Product ("Clip Coupons"): places a virtual, clickable coupon directly on a Shopify product page (rather than requiring customers to find/enter a manual discount code at checkout, which causes drop-off from forgotten/mistyped codes) — deliberately reintroduces friction (physically "clipping" the coupon) as a psychological commitment device, likened explicitly to grocery-store paper coupon aisles (host: "strategically introduced friction").
- Currently free to use (a deliberate strategy to build adoption), funded out of pocket (development, VA team, customer support, marketing) — not sustainable indefinitely; founder unsure when to start charging.
- Early performance data: observed conversion-rate lifts ranging from 2% to 10% across a small, varying sample of stores — founder hesitant to publish this data publicly given the small/inconsistent sample size.
- Growth so far: entirely organic via the Shopify App Store listing, minor App Store ads, a newly hired full-time marketer moving into social/content, with app partnerships identified as the most promising channel discovered so far.
- Compatibility issue identified: some other Shopify apps offering cart-page discounts (e.g., quantity-based upsells) can override/conflict with this app's product-page-based discount, a recurring source of "your app doesn't work" complaints — flagged as worth fixing directly in-app if feasible.
- Advice given: (1) start (or piggyback on) a niche Facebook group for Shopify store owners, following a model the host described from another founder who built a Shopify app by first starting a non-promotional Facebook group, building the product's early features "in public" within the group as the sole admin, and letting organic goodwill drive adoption over time — advised finding an underserved sub-niche of an already-popular category (the host's own example: he started a Facebook group specifically for "off-market RV park deals" because broader real estate and RV-park groups existed but that specific niche didn't); (2) get on the phone directly with existing users (offering something like an Amazon gift card as incentive) rather than relying on passive reviews/feedback forms, since users will give much more specific, actionable feedback in conversation; (3) publish the (even imperfect) conversion-lift data prominently on the App Store listing/marketing rather than withholding it over methodological concerns — host argued the upside of prominently showcasing a believable lift outweighs the small risk of being challenged on methodology, and suggested packaging it as a case study emailed to existing customers, then repurposing a condensed version for the store listing; (4) explore usage-based/percentage-of-savings pricing but flagged as riskier due to customer backlash risk from unclear/variable billing — feedback from other apps' bad experiences with usage-based pricing surprising customers at billing time.
- Host explicitly advised against hiring a full-time marketer at this early stage — suggested the growth tactics above could be bootstrapped cheaply, and that current growing pains (a surprise surge in customer-support ticket volume) suggested the real unmet need was support staffing, not marketing headcount.
- Founder also runs a Shopify development/theme-customization agency and is trying to transition this SaaS product into a full-time focus.

## 4. Handling inbound DMs/consulting requests as a growing social-media personality
- Caller's situation: has a meaningful X/Twitter following (order of tens of thousands) posting business content, and receives frequent unsolicited DMs asking for advice/mentorship — unsure whether/when to charge for time versus give it away, without seeming rude.
- Host's own tested approach and results: previously charged $200/half-hour or hour for consulting calls — found conversion (people actually willing to pay) was surprisingly low even for high-value, specific advice (e.g., "exactly how to buy a mobile home park that makes six or seven figures"); later raised the price to $1,200/hour specifically to filter out all but the most serious inquiries — literally zero people have ever paid that rate, which the host considers a fine/acceptable outcome since he finds one-off paid consulting calls inefficient and low-leverage regardless.
- Recommended approach: (1) create a low-cost digital product (host suggests calling it "a course" but really just a compiled PDF/ebook, e.g., 3,000-5,000 words) — feed relevant existing tweets/content into ChatGPT (or delegate to an assistant) to compile into a coherent short ebook, publish on Gumroad for $20-50, and use a canned/copy-paste response directing DM requesters there first, explicitly and politely stating you can't personally answer everyone; (2) build an email list/free newsletter specifically to capture interested-but-not-yet-paying followers, so that when a higher-ticket offer does exist later (an agency service, a fundraise, etc.) it can be marketed directly to this pre-qualified, already-engaged list rather than broadcast to the entire following; (3) make case-by-case exceptions for people you personally admire/want a genuine two-way relationship with, rather than applying the filtering system universally.
- Explicit acknowledgment from the host that most people who DM for free advice are unwilling to pay even a small amount (e.g., $20) for a packaged answer — the filtering mechanism's main value is reducing time spent, not generating meaningful direct revenue from the DMs themselves.

## Caveats / risks (general, across all four segments)
- The Acton Academy funnel advice assumes the underlying product (school quality/satisfaction, evidenced by a 4.5/5 NPS) is not the actual bottleneck — the diagnosis is specifically about speed-to-contact and funnel friction, not curriculum or delivery.
- The cabin-kit business's B2B upsell opportunity (campgrounds, wedding venues) was explicitly flagged as unbuilt/unproven, not a validated revenue stream yet.
- The Shopify coupon app's free-forever model is explicitly described as financially unsustainable long-term ("I can't sustain it forever"), and its conversion-lift data is based on a small and "varying" sample size, so the reported 2-10% lift range should be read as preliminary, not statistically robust.
- The host's own consulting-pricing experiment ($1,200/hr yielding zero takers) is presented as a real data point but is anecdotal to his specific audience/brand — conversion at different price points will vary by individual influencer and audience trust level.
