# This Business Makes Vending Machines Look Dumb
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YoD0hFqX9W8 | Published: 20260131

Interview with Forest Coughtry (Coughtry Wholesale), who runs a wholesale snack/jerky distribution route to independent gas stations/grocery stores in Billings, Montana.

## The business idea
- Buy snack products (jerky, candy, novelty items) at wholesale from manufacturers, then personally deliver, restock, and merchandise them at independent gas stations, grocery stores, hardware stores, lumber yards, and liquor stores — no vending machines, no contracts.
- Pitch to store owners: "I'm not replacing your existing wholesaler, I'm an addition. I'll show up every other week, place my own order based on what's selling, merchandise/rotate the shelf myself, and buy back anything that doesn't sell." This removes the store's inventory-forecasting burden entirely — framed as a no-brainer/no-risk offer.
- Differentiator vs. the big national wholesalers (who run fast in-and-out routes, ~52 stops/day, no upsell/relationship): Forest visits every other week, builds relationships, and actively upsells new product lines once trust is established.
- Key competitive edge: **carrying local/regional products** (e.g., a local Billings jerky brand) that big wholesalers won't bother stocking because it wouldn't sell nationally — "support local" is an easy sell to independent store owners, and it can't be easily undercut on price by nationals.
- Framed explicitly as objectively better than vending machines: no $3,000 machine, no $1,000 credit card reader, no swipe fees, no machine maintenance/multiple site tests — display racks are provided free by the manufacturers.

## Numbers
- Started ~1 year, 3-4 months before this interview, in Billings, Montana (moved from Colorado, knew nobody).
- First month in Montana: $20,000 in sales. By month ~6-7: over $50,000/month and growing.
- Current run rate: ~$50,000/month revenue, ~94-95 accounts (accounts = individual store locations; one gas-station chain with 10 locations = 10 accounts).
- Average order/delivery: $500-900 for gas stations; some grocery stores as high as $3,500/drop; one rural store nets $1,400/visit despite being tiny.
- Markup: 30-45% depending on category; on a $1,000 order, product cost ~$650-700, ~$350-400 gross profit.
- Gross margin ~40%, net margin ~30% (self-operated); if hiring out delivery, net drops to ~20%.
- A high-end-market operator (Breckenridge/Aspen) can hit 45% margin due to premium pricing.
- Storage: started with a 10x20 temp-controlled storage unit (~$180/mo), then two units (~$500/mo combined), later moved into a shared warehouse (~1,500 sq ft, ~$1,400/mo all-in) where staff receive/check deliveries for him (critical since Montana routes can be 2+ hours away).
- Payment terms: buys from manufacturers on net 14-30 (2 weeks to a month to pay); sells to stores on net 10 (delivers every 14 days, collects payment before the next delivery) — creates a **positive cash flow conversion cycle** (gets paid before he has to pay suppliers). Was able to negotiate 14-30 day manufacturer terms even as a brand-new distributor by "selling himself" (his story, projected store count).
- Minimum order to start with a manufacturer: as low as $2,500 (e.g., first jerky supplier), which fills about 4 display racks.
- Best single days: $6,000-7,000 revenue days (summer); ~$2,000-3,000 profit/day achievable running solo, full-time, once account base is built out.
- Churn: lost only 1 account in the whole year+ (a competitor with an existing store relationship undercut him after ~4 months) — but immediately replaced it with a bigger account.
- Route/territory: services roughly a 2.5-hour radius around Billings; averages 10-12 stores/day, 6-8 hour days; ~4 out of 10 cold pitches said yes at the very start (second follow-up visit is often when a "no" converts to a "yes").
- Estimated market share: ~2-4% of total in-store snack product space per store even where he's an established vendor, though ~80% of the "independent" stores in his route are already customers, and in some accounts he now runs the entire jerky section and ~30% of candy.
- Scale ceiling estimate (from host, agreed to by Forest): current accounts pushed to 50% in-store market share could 12x the business to ~$6-7.2M/year in a rural market; with more account growth, ~$12M/year potential.
- Standard industry pay to hire someone to run a full route: $50,000-70,000/year salary; a "full" route (in a rural area) should generate ~$800,000/year revenue, implying ~30% net margin ($240K) at max efficiency for a solo operator.
- No employees currently — family helps unload pallets on the weekly "warehouse day."

## Process / steps
1. **Before moving to a new market**, cold-called similar (non-competing) wholesale distributors in the target state to learn what sells and get referrals/insight — most business owners were willing to help.
2. Picked a specific city (Billings) partly based on those calls, ensuring no direct competition with a contact he'd made further north.
3. Called multiple snack/food manufacturers (started with 3: a beef jerky company, a mainstream candy company, and a novelty candy company) to find one with **no existing distribution in the target area** — checked via phone, not by scouting store shelves (you want a brand not yet in stores, not one already there).
4. Sourced product-company contact info via referrals from other distributors and Google searches ("what's a cool jerky brand").
5. Pitch to manufacturers before having any stores lined up: sell them on your story (does not require industry experience — "I just moved here, I don't know anyone, I'm the hardest worker you'll meet" is a valid pitch) plus a rough list of independent-looking stores found via Google/Google Maps that you'll target.
6. Simultaneously pitch stores using that manufacturer relationship as legitimacy — a "marketplace" dynamic: get buy-in from brands and stores at the same time using only your story as leverage.
7. In-person pitch tools: kept two simple sell sheets — a full price/product list, and a simplified one-page "fun facts" flyer (e.g., "cheapest pound of jerky," "#1 selling gummy worm") to avoid overwhelming a busy store owner. Always carry product in the vehicle so you can close and install on the spot before they can back out ("the salesman is also the driver").
8. Deliver every other week (every 14 days) regardless of whether the store restocks that visit — repeat visits build relationship trust and create natural upsell openings ("hey, this line isn't selling, what do you have to replace it?").
9. Get manufacturers to help open new accounts when possible — his first jerky supplier sent their own salesman to open stores directly for him early on.
10. Track and manage your own inventory/ordering carefully — because *he* places every order, he says he rarely actually has to buy back unsold product despite offering the buyback guarantee.
11. Avoid Walmart/Target/regional chain corporate accounts at first — too much red tape/buyer bureaucracy; focus exclusively on independent stores (verify "independent" by checking the store's actual branding/ownership on Google Maps street view, not the gas brand on the sign — a Shell or BP-branded station can still be an independently owned store).
12. Explicitly avoid competing on price with major national brands (Coke, Frito-Lay, Jack Link's) when starting out — you'll lose on price. Sell distinctive/local/regional products instead.

## Tools/platforms named
- A basic invoicing app (~$10-12/month) for professional-looking printed tickets (helped credibility with regional accounts) — no named brand given.
- Google Maps / Street View for scouting "independent" stores before ever visiting in person.
- Local temperature-controlled storage units, later a shared local warehouse with staff who receive deliveries.

## Vehicle/startup cost path
- Can start with **literally no money and just a car** — enough jerky for one display rack fits in a trunk; sell it, restock from your garage, repeat. Scaling path: car → hitch + open U-Haul trailer (~$200-300) → enclosed trailer → shelved enclosed trailer → sprinter van/box truck.
- Because most jerky/snack manufacturers offer 14-day payment terms, you can sell through your first batch of inventory before you even have to pay for it — effectively a no-capital start.

## Caveats / risks
- Requires being physically local/available (delivery is the core service) — not a remote/passive business.
- Slow start: getting the first account is the hardest part; expect a high initial "no" rate.
- Products with short shelf life (contrast: a separate anecdote about a fresh-bread business with 7-day shelf life which had to buy back large quantities weekly) are much harder to run than long-dated snack goods (jerky/candy typically ~2-year shelf life, so "wait it out" is a viable strategy for slow movers instead of a true buyback loss).
- Competitors with entrenched store relationships can and do undercut new distributors (lost 1 account this way).
- Rural markets like Montana have less competition but also fewer total stores; denser markets (Colorado) have more stores but heavier competition — a real trade-off, not free money either way.
- Requires driving significant distances in a large, sparsely populated state (some single deliveries are a 2.5-hour round trip).
- Scaling to true full-route capacity (~$800K/year) requires hiring, which reduces personal net margin from ~30% to ~20%; sales-based commission structures (e.g., ~6% of sales) are used to keep hired route-runners motivated to keep growing accounts rather than plateauing.
- Corporate/chain accounts (Walmart, Target, chain gas stations) are much harder to break into early on due to centralized buyer decision-making — not worth pursuing until established.
