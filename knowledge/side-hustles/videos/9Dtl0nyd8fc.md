# How to Copy Your Way to a $6M/Year Indoor Sledding Biz
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9Dtl0nyd8fc | Published: 2024-06-27

Follow-up interview with Matthew (Ayers), who previously built an Alpine Slide/coaster business (covered in episode 29), about Pigeon Forge Snow — a year-round, temperature-independent indoor snow-tubing park in Pigeon Forge, TN — plus his subsequent real-estate subdivision development and other business ideas.

## The business: Pigeon Forge Snow
- Indoor snow-tubing park, first of its kind in the US in two specific ways: (1) temperature-independent — kept at ~65-70°F inside (unlike subzero indoor snow venues, e.g., some in Dubai, which require jackets), and (2) open 365 days/year (unlike outdoor ski-resort snow tubing, which is seasonal).
- Origin/inspiration: an outdoor ski resort ~30-45 minutes away guaranteed snow tubing every year from Thanksgiving to Easter regardless of actual weather, using snow-making machines that pile up manufactured snow year-round inside a controlled area. Matthew reasoned: if they can control snow/temperature outdoors with machines, he could do the same fully indoors in a purpose-built building.
- Core technical challenge: managing meltwater ("water is snow's enemy" — melting accelerates once water accumulates near ice/snow). Solved with a water-capture system: gravity carries meltwater off the snow hill through a grate into a collection tank, pumps move it to holding tanks, and it's reused as feedstock for the snow machines. Large dehumidification systems (like those in ice rinks) pull ambient humidity out of the air, and that water is also recycled.
- The recycling system was intentionally left as a "figure it out later" item on blueprints during planning/construction — engineers and Matthew brainstormed the design together once the core snow-making concept was validated; it was one of the last technical pieces finalized, and it worked out as conceptually simple (though it required redundancy — now 3 pumps — after at least one incident of a pump failure flooding the facility).
- Hill design/ride speed was NOT nailed by an outside "friction coefficient" expert he hired early on (whose formal engineering report/calculations were "nowhere close" to correct) — instead, Matthew and friends/family physically tested the hill themselves once initial snow was blown in, timing runs and adjusting with sandbags, settling empirically on ~30 mph as the sweet spot (fast enough to be exciting, slow enough for kids). Lesson drawn: hands-on experimentation often outperforms hiring an expert, especially for a non-mission-critical variable with room to iterate.

## Demand validation before building
- Because "snow was a huge financial deal" and he wasn't willing to risk his finances without confidence, he validated demand for the competing outdoor ski resort by literally watching their public webcam of the snow-tubing hill for an entire prior season and manually clicking/counting attendance in real time (sometimes enlisting others to help count) while eating dinner, etc.
- Bonus data source: the resort's own ticketing website displayed real-time inventory (e.g., "40 of 150 sold" per time slot), letting him estimate their revenue "probably down to $100" for that season — he described this as the competitor "begging you to copy them" by exposing so much operational data.
- Estimated their revenue was "in the millions"; ticket price had risen over the years (~$25 → $30 → dynamic pricing reaching ~$45-50), and secondary evidence of high demand included black-market resale of sold-out tickets discussed in local Facebook groups.
- Built a detailed financial pro forma/budget (including calling the local electric company to estimate power usage) that he says proved highly accurate in hindsight — a rare outcome he attributes to the amount of time invested in it. Explicitly worked out a worst-case demand scenario ("what's the least amount of customers I can have and still make this work") rather than only modeling a best case, and built in a ~15-20% "miscellaneous" contingency line on every budget item, reasoning that unexpected costs are inevitable.
- General cold-research technique mentioned for reverse-engineering any e-commerce competitor's revenue: place a real order, note the sequential order number, place another order some time later, calculate order velocity from the difference, multiply out annually (adjusting for seasonality), and multiply by observed average order value.

## Numbers
- Opened spring 2018 (soft-launched snow-making 365 days before opening while building an email list/hype).
- First year revenue: "a couple million dollars," profitable from year one.
- 2020 (COVID year, city closed ~30-45 days): became the region's best year yet, as Pigeon Forge/Gatlinburg-area tourism boomed as a domestic "escape" destination during the pandemic.
- 2021: revenue in the $5-6M range (roughly double 2020, not confirmed exactly).
- 2023: revenue dipped ~10-15% from the 2021/2022 peak, attributed to broader regional post-COVID tourism pullback, not company-specific issues.
- 2024: revenue roughly flat with 2023.
- Seasonality: best ~60-day season (Thanksgiving through January) generates roughly one-third of total annual revenue; May is typically the slowest month (didn't anticipate this before opening in "mid-to-late spring," which unexpectedly launched right into a slow season).
- Capital: "millions" invested, mostly in the building and land, not the snow machines themselves (a common misconception) — snow machines were paid for in cash because he was confident in the underlying demand data and didn't want debt exposure there; a large metal building of ~35,000 sq ft ("Orindal"-type prefab) was the largest cost driver. Building height varies up to ~40 ft at its tallest point (front facade ~26 ft) to accommodate the interior hill — in hindsight he thinks it may not have needed to be quite that tall, though the exact savings from a shorter structure are unclear.
- To cut costs early on, he personally installed the sound/speaker system himself (bought components off Amazon) rather than hiring it out — flagged in hindsight as possibly not the best use of his time.
- Claims to have fully recouped the investment (via debt paydown/refinancing) within roughly 2-3 years or less, though he actively manages multiple loans at varying rates and monitors macroeconomic risk to his debt load.
- Marketing/list-building: built anticipation for a full year pre-opening via a dedicated Facebook page (started when the property was still just cleared dirt) — posted informal "getting started" photos/updates, sold limited "VIP" branded t-shirts that included a free visit voucher for the first year, letting the community "follow along in the journey" well before any grand opening.

## Franchising / replication concepts (from Matthew's own exploration)
- Spent much of 2022-2023 working with two experienced franchise-development partners to explore franchising the concept, but paused due to unrelated personal-life circumstances.
- Believes a scaled-down version could be built in other cities (e.g., Dallas) far more cheaply than his multi-million-dollar Pigeon Forge build by renting a mobile snow-making unit (a "tractor trailer"-style mobile machine) rather than a permanent facility — requires reasonable water access (more than a garden hose but not extreme) and three-phase power (the trickiest logistical requirement). Could be deployed on unused mall parking lot space, either flat ground for open play or a small plywood/scaffolding-built hill, on a seasonal lease/rev-share arrangement (e.g., renting the space and machine only Nov-Jan).
- Key build requirements for anyone trying to replicate an indoor snow-tubing venue in an existing warehouse-type building: (1) a hill — can be built with scaffolding/plywood, doesn't have to be huge (a longer, lower hill works as an alternative to a tall one, and works for younger kids too); (2) a way to get riders to the top (he uses a "magic carpet" conveyor, installable in most buildings); (3) water capture/drainage (recommended but not strictly mandatory — could instead just drain/filter without full recycling); (4) the snow machines themselves; (5) some insulation (a plain uninsulated barn-type structure would not work unless the climate is already very cold).
- Cautions that many suitable large industrial/warehouse buildings sit in areas consumers wouldn't naturally think to visit (compares to an early regional trampoline park being tucked inside an industrial park) — location/visibility is a real constraint even if the physical build is feasible.

## Other business ideas discussed (not all executed)
- **FlowRider-style stationary wave/surf simulator** placed in underused water park or mall parking lot space via a rev-share/lease deal with the property owner — framed as ideal because it's a "spectator activity" the whole family (including non-participants) can watch, take photos of, or buy a same-day photo/video package from (e.g., a GoPro running continuously, charging for delivered footage). Considered better suited to an existing water park (where visitors already have swimsuits and are in a spend-ready mindset) than a mall parking lot, where the activity would be too impulsive/inconvenient (no swimsuit on hand).
- General negotiation principle for approaching an existing business owner (e.g., a water park) with a rev-share pitch: the pitch must remove headaches for the owner, not add them — framed via a real anecdote where someone pitched Matthew a vending-machine rev-share at his own facility and he declined because he could "just buy it" himself; a good partnership pitch instead offers pure incremental revenue with no added operational burden.
- **Composted grocery-waste soil business** (real case study cited, not something Matthew built): "EOS Scraps" (Utah) collected organic waste (eventually focusing only on fruit/vegetable waste) for free/paid removal from grocery stores and restaurants (negative COGS — paid to take the input), composted it with sawdust for carbon balance in an industrial warehouse, and sold bagged soil (~$12/bag) to Home Depot/Lowe's; eventually sold to a major company. The Koerner Office host had explored copying this in Alabama over a decade earlier (built a pitch deck, had lunches with the original founders, got as far as an initial conversation with Whole Foods about taking their produce waste) but never launched it — cited as proof that a good, geographically-underserved business model idea can remain viable/repeatable many years later even after being publicly described.
- **Insomnia Cookies-style late-night delivery cookie shop**: another idea the host researched and pitched to investors in college (based on the real, then-under-the-radar national chain expanding to college towns) but never executed; Insomnia Cookies later opened in that exact market.
- **Alpine Coaster at Beavers Bend, OK** (raised as a suggestion for a "Matthew 3.0" project): ~2.5 hours from Dallas, ~2 million visitors/year (roughly 1/5-1/6 the traffic of Pigeon Forge), with zero existing coasters — Matthew declined to commit personally (uncomfortable investing in a physical venue outside his home region) but offered to consult/coach/passively invest for anyone willing to pursue it, and described a concrete on-ramp: fly to Pigeon Forge, ride the existing rides, take photos, build a presentation, and start pitching investors back home — noting his own original Alpine Coaster investment was sparked by nothing more than someone texting him a photo of a long line at another coaster, and that a small equity stake (an engineer who did survey/engineering work received 1% ownership) is a viable way for a non-capital partner to get involved.
- Idea for enhancing future attractions with a themed, sequential mechanical experience (a "Rube Goldberg machine"-style mini-golf hole with laser trip sensors triggering chain-reaction effects) — an unrealized concept he's still holding onto for a future venture.

## Real estate side venture: "The Farm at Riverbend" subdivision (Sevierville, TN, ~20-30 min from Pigeon Forge Snow)
- Motivation: severe regional housing supply shortage post-COVID (Knoxville/Sevierville area saw some of the country's highest price increases due to supply-demand imbalance); Matthew is developing raw land (buying farmland, adding utilities/roads, subdividing) and selling finished lots to a home builder (mostly one primary builder, some individual lot sales) rather than building homes himself.
- Found the underlying utility/government/zoning/permitting process far harder than physical construction work he's used to — direct quote sentiment: "this is why there's a housing problem, no one can get anything done."
- Two phases: Phase 1 (~100 lots) complete and occupied; Phase 2 (~75 additional lots) nearing completion (paving) at time of recording.
- Lot types/pricing (proceeds to Matthew, not final home price): standard lots (~1/3 acre, all utilities included) ~$50,000-60,000 each; premium lots (~10-20% of total, larger/corner/better setting) ~$70,000-75,000 each; "river lots" (ultra-premium, waterfront) sold in the $100,000-180,000 range, with ~40 river lots total in the full development. Homes built on standard lots by the builder are new-construction 3-bedroom houses in the $300-400k range.
- Total development cost estimated at "five or six million dollars," and Matthew says the project has "blown the budget" (in a negative/over-budget direction) and taken a significant personal toll ("taken years off my life") despite being financially profitable overall.
- General industry observation: "there is no job title hated more than a developer" (or custom home builder) — public perception is of easy, exploitative profit despite the actual difficulty/toll of navigating red tape.

## Meta-lessons on entrepreneurship and idea execution
- Matthew explicitly does NOT recommend his own approach ("jump out of the plane holding the parachute and try to put it on in the air" — committing to large, capital-intensive projects before every detail is solved) for others, though he says the pressure helps him perform.
- His business scale escalated over time (CrossFit gym and a frozen yogurt shop early in his career, before the Alpine Slide, ski park, and subdivision) — each felt like "the biggest thing" at the time relative to his experience level; he lost more sleep/stress over the comparatively small frozen yogurt shop than he now does at multi-million-dollar scale.
- Currently prioritizing learning to delegate (self-described as "terrible" at it) over launching new large ventures, seeking autonomous team members rather than people who need hand-holding.
- On idea timing: both host and guest agree that many viable, well-documented business ideas remain executable years or even decades after they're first identified — the constraint is usually follow-through, not novelty or "being too late." Ideas not acted on tend to "fizzle," and both hosts keep large personal running lists of untried/tested ideas.
- Encourages people intimidated by large capital requirements for a given idea (e.g., wanting to build an Alpine Coaster) to pursue smaller entry points: consulting, small equity stakes for contributed expertise/work, or passive investment, rather than assuming full ownership/self-funding is the only path in.
