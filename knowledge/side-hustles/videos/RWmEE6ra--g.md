# You're Doing Everything Except the One Thing That Works
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RWmEE6ra--g | Published: 2025-06-26

Interview with Sam Thompson (unlimitedcontent.com, hummingbirdfood.co, X: @ImSamThompson), covering three distinct topics: (1) validating any business idea via cold sales calls instead of upfront planning, (2) a live case study of Sam's hummingbird nectar e-commerce experiment, and (3) using Google VEO3 AI video generation for ad creative, UGC-style content, and new media/business models.

## Part 1: Validating a business idea via sales calls (core thesis)
- **Core claim**: launching a business is "not that complicated" — most people over-invest in pitch decks, perfect websites, and market research as "advanced forms of procrastination" instead of taking action. Framework: "impatience with action, patience with results" — launch immediately, but be patient about refining pricing/positioning once real customer data comes in.
- **The method**: get on live sales calls with literally nothing to sell yet. Run a broad, low-commitment Facebook ad (e.g., "Business owners, are you struggling with Google Maps? Schedule a call.") rather than a narrowly defined service pitch. On the call, ask open questions about what the prospect is struggling with — they'll self-report the real problem ("first-party customer research"). Tip: use ChatGPT beforehand to generate specific probing questions/icebreakers tailored to your target niche, since people often can't articulate pain points from a fully open-ended question but respond well to a narrower prompt (e.g., "what are you struggling with regarding your Google reviews system?" surfaces usable answers where "what are you struggling with?" generally doesn't).
- **Real case study**: a member of Sam's "Grow Faster" community (customer-service background in England, no local-business or Google Maps experience) wanted to start a Google Business Profile/review-management service. Told simply to "go get on sales calls," he ran ~$30/day Facebook ads and booked calls for ~$30 each. On calls, he discovered prospects didn't actually care about review management — they cared about ranking higher on Google Maps / getting more calls/customers. He pivoted his ad and landing page messaging to "we'll help you rank on Google Maps better," which dropped his cost-per-booked-call to ~$18, and he closed his first paying client within about a month from a standing start, with two more expected to close shortly after.
- **Pricing discovery, live, on the phone**: don't pre-set pricing before talking to customers — ask directly what they can afford for the outcome you're offering, using their own numbers to build the pitch (e.g., "so you're saying every organic Google call is worth about $60 net profit to you? You get 4/day, that's $120/month. What if I got you to 500 calls/month — what would that be worth?" → $30,000 → "well this only costs $800/month"). This live math ("catch the customer in their own words") is described as far more persuasive than pre-declaring a price and having to defend it.
- **Willingness-to-pay varies by customer** — one prospect (smaller market, fewer reviews) may only afford $300/month while another (bigger business, nicer area) can pay $800/month; the recommendation is to A/B test pricing live across calls (as long as you know your floor cost — in the case study, the white-labeled review-management software cost roughly $100/client, and Sam suggested pricing between $400-$600) rather than guessing a single number from your own head or from a generic ChatGPT-estimated market average.
- **Why the first stranger customer matters more than friends/family**: a sale to someone unrelated to you is real, unbiased proof the offer works ("a clean slate, a perfect case study") — friends/family sales are a "false positive" because personal goodwill, not real value, often drives the purchase (and they're unlikely to give honest negative feedback if the product is bad). For a side income or something small, friend/family/referral sales can work, but any business meant to scale meaningfully will eventually depend entirely on strangers, so it's better to build the muscle (and validate real demand) from the start.
- **10x → 100x pricing/value framework**: an early useful heuristic is charging roughly 1/10th of the value you create (e.g., charge $200 if you generate $2,000 of value for the customer) — this "bakes in" some underperformance risk since even a 50% shortfall still nets the customer a 5x return. However, the speakers argue the most valuable companies (using Apple as the example: a $3,300 MacBook Pro used 8 hours/day for 4+ years) actually deliver 20-200x ROI to the customer, not just 10x — and pursuing that higher multiple (rather than settling once you've raised price to a "good enough" 7x) produces stronger word-of-mouth, lower churn, and organic referrals.
- **Warning against "idea hopping"**: jumping between business ideas every few days without giving any one idea sufficient runway is described as a major failure pattern — commit to testing something for at least ~60 days (or long enough to actually get statistically real feedback) before abandoning it; a 3-day trial "doesn't give you anything." The specific tactical skill highlighted as hard-won is knowing when you've genuinely tested an idea enough to fairly kill it versus quitting prematurely.
- **General validation funnel recommended for ANY vehicle** (service, SaaS, e-commerce, coaching, book): run a simple one-page Facebook ad funnel — "Attention [target customer]. Stop struggling with [pain point]. Talk to an expert today." — collect email/Calendly booking, then get 15-20 people on the phone from that specific market and directly ask what form of solution they want (coaching? a book? software? a done-for-you service?) and what they'd pay. Closing even one paying customer this way (e.g., "$100/hour coaching, buy 4 hours") proves the market will pay for a solution to a specific problem — described as buying founder "confidence" as much as revenue.

## Part 2: Live case study — hummingbird nectar e-commerce (Sam's ongoing experiment)
- Origin (from an earlier, linked episode): Sam started selling hummingbird nectar (essentially bagged sugar) online, launched with Facebook ads, and made "thousands of dollars" profitably within hours of setup.
- Update discussed in this episode: he got a repeat order but paused ads due to a shipping-cost/speed issue with his original bag size; switching to smaller 15oz bags to cut shipping cost per order from ~$8 to ~$4.
- New upsell/AOV idea inspired by a competitor: a hummingbird nectar "extender" (an antibacterial additive, essentially water + micronutrient copper sulfate, costing the competitor under $1 to produce in a 1oz bottle) is sold on Amazon for $16/bottle and reportedly moves ~1,000 units/month. Sam's plan: bundle two 7oz nectar pouches + one 1oz extender tincture bottle (bottles sourceable on Amazon for ~$0.30 each) into a ~1lb kit, aiming to sell it for ~$28 (vs. the standalone competitor extender at $16), while still needing to figure out supply chain/manufacturing for the liquid extender component himself.
- Business is explicitly seasonal — tied to hummingbird migration patterns, roughly a 3-4 month active selling window; Sam plans to prep the same product line for Amazon (rather than just Facebook-ad-driven DTC) for the following summer season, framing it as needing basics like trademarks in place first, and describing it as a low-stakes side project he'd be happy to scale to "$100K/year" using nothing more than a Jungle Scout account, ChatGPT, Google, and Facebook ads.
- Suggested organic marketing tactic (not yet executed): use Google VEO3 to generate a simple 5-second "before/after" video ad — identical hummingbird feeders side by side, one showing a single bird visiting and leaving, the other showing a swarm of birds — to visually demonstrate the extender's effectiveness as a testimonial-style ad creative.

## Part 3: AI video generation (Google VEO3) as content/ad tooling and business opportunity
- **Ad-testing workflow**: use VEO3 to cheaply generate many (10-20+) variations of a UGC-style video ad (different scripts, hooks, presenters) for roughly $100 total in generation cost, then identify the best-performing hook/script from that batch, and only then invest real money hiring an actual human UGC creator to re-shoot the winning concept professionally — turning AI generation into a fast, cheap pre-testing layer before committing real ad-production spend.
- **VEO3 prompting structure that worked well for Sam** (roughly $1,200 spent testing/learning): (1) Setting — physical location, time of day, weather/lighting/vibe (e.g., "busy sidewalk in an urban neighborhood at sunset, overcast, handheld style, street interview, gritty"); (2) Characters — detailed physical description of each person (age, clothing, physical traits) plus explicit personality direction (e.g., "he is relaxed and calm and confident," "she is anxious and shy") which visibly affects how the generated characters interact; (3) Dialogue/script — exact lines for each character, with an interviewer typically kept off-camera holding a visible microphone into frame for a "street interview" aesthetic.
- **Practical prompting fixes for common VEO3 glitches**: the model frequently generates garbled/non-English fake subtitles; removing quotation marks around dialogue lines in the prompt reduces this, as does avoiding the literal phrase "TikTok-style video" in favor of "handheld video on an iPhone" (both empirically reduced unwanted subtitle artifacts, per Sam and a technique he credited to a Greg Isenberg podcast guest).
- **Use ChatGPT to co-write VEO3 prompts**, but manually tighten the AI's first-draft output — ChatGPT's initial descriptions tend to be vague/multi-option (e.g., "wearing casual earth-style clothing like a hoodie, jeans, and sneakers") where VEO3 performs better with fully specific, singular choices ("he's wearing a black hoodie, jeans, and sneakers").
- **Concrete niche business idea floated**: a hyper-targeted local ad agency model — generate one realistic-looking "street interview" style VEO3 ad featuring someone discussing a product/service (e.g., "what's the best pasta in NYC?"), overlay persistent on-screen text like "I can make this ad for your business," and run it as a Facebook ad shown ONLY to a specific professional audience (e.g., the ~40,000 restaurant owners in New York City) as a live demo/lead-gen piece — charging those business owners a recurring fee (example figure: $500/month) to produce similar AI-generated ad content for them on an ongoing basis.
- **Platform/industry signal cited**: Google has announced direct VEO3 integration into YouTube Shorts, framed as confirmation that major platforms are actively embracing AI-generated video content rather than resisting it — cited alongside real examples of AI-generated viral content (an AI otter-in-a-sink video reportedly getting ~1 billion views; AI cat-diving videos reportedly getting ~700 million views) as evidence platforms will keep rewarding this content style because it drives engagement.
- **Home-service business application already in use by Sam's business**: generating fake/AI "video review" style ad creatives for local home-service clients (example given: a garage door installation company in Cleveland) as short-form promotional/testimonial-style ad content.
- **New media/content business angle**: short-form AI video content (interviewing historical/fictional characters, e.g., an Instagram account doing "Gen Z meets Bible stories" — AI interviews with Moses at the parting of the Red Sea, Daniel in the lion's den — reportedly reaching 100,000+ followers within weeks of VEO3's release) is compared to the earlier "Vine era" media-personality path (citing the Paul brothers, David Dobrik) where short-form video skill and "market taste" for what's funny/culturally relevant became the foundation for much larger media careers/personalities. Suggested monetization directions for this kind of AI content account: parlaying an established audience/niche into merchandise or product sales (e.g., a "Gen Z Bible" or related merch for a Bible-themed AI account), though the speakers note monetization is a separate, harder problem from content creation itself.
- **"Learn the skill before the technology matures" argument**: even a seemingly gimmicky/unscalable AI content account (example: a low-effort account posting AI-generated videos like "babies almost eaten by tigers," suspected to be run cheaply from overseas using ad-revenue-funded generation credits) builds real, transferable prompting skill that will compound in value as AI video models improve (longer clips, character consistency across scenes, product placement capability) — waiting until the technology is "good enough" to start means starting the learning curve later than competitors who experimented early.

## Caveats / risks
- The sales-call validation approach explicitly assumes the founder is willing to do unscalable, personal, manual selling early on (the case-study operator plans to personally deliver the service himself rather than hire staff, since it's framed as side income, not a scaled business) — this method proves demand but doesn't by itself solve later operational scaling.
- Pricing discovered live on sales calls is anecdotal/small-sample by nature at the early stage (a handful of calls) — the hosts frame it as directionally useful "first-party data," not a statistically rigorous pricing study.
- The hummingbird nectar business is explicitly candid about mid-experiment mistakes (misjudged bag size ordered, forced mid-campaign pivot) and is described by Sam himself as not "some massive thing," just a plausible ~$100K/year side project — presented as an honest in-progress case study, not a finished playbook.
- AI-generated video content carries an acknowledged authenticity/detection ambiguity — Sam notes VEO3 output "looks real" even though viewers may sense something is synthetic, and platforms are not currently requiring or enforcing AI-disclosure labeling in any meaningful way (echoing themes from a related episode on AI content transparency) — ethical/disclosure norms in this space are still unsettled.
- The "hyper-targeted local ad agency" idea (ads shown only to a narrow niche like NYC restaurant owners) and other brainstormed applications are described as promising concepts, not validated, run businesses with real revenue numbers, unlike the hummingbird and Google Maps case studies which include real (if early/small) figures.
