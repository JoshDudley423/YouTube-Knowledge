# How to Hire an Employee Better than Yourself
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x6W4RX3CM4w | Published: 2024-06-04

Call-in advice format: Chris Koerner takes questions from 4 entrepreneurs. Less "here's a business idea" and more tactical operating/growth advice, but includes two concrete business models (assumable-mortgage matching platform, children's entrepreneurship market nonprofit) with real numbers.

## Segment 1: Jake — incentivizing a General Manager for a pool cleaning business
- Context: Jake runs a seasonal (Michigan) pool cleaning side business alongside a W2 job, transitioning to part-time W2, and wants to hire a GM/COO-type person to run day-to-day operations (starting by cleaning pools himself, later managing hired techs) while Jake focuses on growth/marketing.
- Koerner's recommended comp structure for a non-equity operating hire: below-market flat salary + a commission structure that pushes total comp above market. Example given: if the business does $10,000/month topline, market salary for the GM role is ~$70k/year — pay a base of ~$50k/year, plus a commission (e.g., 20% of revenue) that would bring realistic total comp to ~$74k in year one, so the person is clearly incentivized above the market rate from day one.
- Recommends tying commission specifically to *growth*, not just maintenance of existing revenue — even for field techs who aren't naturally "salespeople." Rationale: framing matters — a tech resents being told to "make cold calls," but will happily make an offhand remark to a homeowner like "know anyone else who needs pool cleaning?" if it's framed as casual, not as "selling." A branded shirt + truck on-site makes the tech the most likely person to actually close a referral, more so than the owner.
- Recommends a two-tier commission: a modest tier (e.g., 20% of revenue) for simply sustaining/servicing the existing book, and a much richer tier for closing brand-new referred customers (e.g., 100% of the first 1-3 months' revenue from a new referred customer, since churn is low — ~4% — meaning that customer will likely stay for years). Framing this as a specific dollar amount per referral (e.g., "$400 every time you mention us and it turns into a sale") is far more motivating to a non-salesperson than describing it as a percentage or ongoing residual.
- General principle: avoid overly simple flat-salary-only structures — "simple can screw you" whether the employee does nothing or does far more than expected; look for a structure that protects the owner from windfall payouts while remaining generous/simple enough to not create friction. Cites Charlie Munger: incentives matter more than people think, and "even if you're thinking about incentives all day, you're not thinking about them enough."
- Cautions against pure commission-only pay unless it's industry-standard practice (e.g., life insurance sales, or buying an existing "book of business" at a revenue split like 75/25 or 50/50) — pure commission-only otherwise tends to attract lower-quality candidates who have no other options ("no one else will hire me, what do I have to lose").
- Sourcing/hiring tactic: Indeed (both free and paid; recommends budgeting $400-$500 for paid job post promotion) as the most productive channel found so far for this type of hire — treat it as a numbers game.
- Unscalable but effective sourcing tactic: physically approach existing service crews you see out working (e.g., a lawn crew or tree crew) as a "curious homeowner," ask casual questions like how long they've been doing the job — a long tenure (e.g., 7 years) signals someone who is likely good at the work and could be a recruiting target.

## Segment 2: Lou — assumable mortgage matching platform
- Business concept: assumable mortgages let a home buyer take over the seller's existing mortgage (and its interest rate) instead of originating a new one — valuable when the seller's rate (some as low as 2.25%, ~70% of assumable mortgages cited as under 4%) is far below current market rates (7%+), potentially saving a buyer $1,000+/month and hundreds of thousands over the loan's life.
- Platform model: nationwide site where buyers browse homes for sale in their area that carry an assumable mortgage, click "assume this home," fill out a lead form (down payment capacity, whether they already have an agent), and get warm-handed-off to a real estate agent partner in that market; monetized via referral/commission-sharing agreements with real estate partners (whether or not the deal actually closes as an assumable-mortgage transaction — they still get paid if the referred buyer closes on a traditional mortgage through their partner agent).
- Traction at time of recording: launched February 5th of that year; generated ~30,000 leads pushed to real estate partners; revenue per completed deal in the "low single digit thousands" (a percentage of the referred agent's commission).
- Servicer friction described as the main mechanical bottleneck: assumable mortgage transfers go through mortgage servicers (not the same process as a typical purchase), servicer processing time was trending down toward ~45-60 days at time of filming; servicers were newly allowed to charge up to $1,800 per assumable-mortgage transaction.
- Biggest business bottleneck identified: education/trust friction with *listing agents* specifically (buyer-side agents are receptive, but listing agents often don't understand or trust assumable mortgages, don't realize it's a differentiator for their listing, and treat qualified assumable-mortgage offers with suspicion) — deals often fall through or convert to a traditional (non-assumable) mortgage due to listing-agent unfamiliarity, not lack of buyer interest.
- Koerner's recommended fix: build a structured, escalating-friction education funnel for agents rather than relying on one long email/explainer — mirrors his own experience A/B testing a detailed platform-migration email at Mining Syndicate (a long detailed email) vs. a short 3-sentence version with a link to more detail (most people wanted only the short version/TLDR). Suggested "packet" sequence for listing agents: a 30-second video first, then a 3-minute video with more detail, then a link to a webinar, then a link to FAQs — with Zapier automation persistently following up (with buyer or listing agents) to confirm the material was actually viewed, and tracking engagement data (e.g., which link/CTA people actually click, à la testing Linktree link order) to iteratively promote whichever asset performs best to the top.
- Cited his own Mining Syndicate experience: trust/education is the primary obstacle for unfamiliar-sounding financial products; unscalable tactics (physical facility tours, live Zoom calls) get an ~80% close rate, and a (recorded, framed-as-live) webinar with visible participant counts for social proof gets "90% of the way" to that close rate at much higher scale — suggested running an evergreen/asynchronous "always live-appearing" webinar agents can join anytime.
- General comparison drawn to Uber/Airbnb: novel-sounding models that initially seem "scammy" are normalized mainly through time, reduced friction, and repeated exposure — no shortcut beyond building trust incrementally.

## Segment 3: Ashley — dealing with a missed real estate opportunity (not a business idea; personal/mindset advice)
- Context: sold her unit in a 20-unit complex to a developer (18-month multi-owner sale process); later learned other owners received up to $250,000 more for their units (all under $1M total).
- Koerner's advice: frame the money left on the table as "tuition" paid to learn negotiation; channel the frustration into a "chip on your shoulder" that fuels future deals rather than trying to intellectually reason your way past the emotion. Admits he personally never fully "moved past" his own similar experiences — instead outcompeted the situation via later success ("success is the best revenge"). General point: everyone carries insecurities; self-awareness of what's driving your reaction is itself a predictor of future success.

## Segment 4: Connor — Children's Entrepreneur Market (nonprofit), corporate sponsorship fundraising
- Business/nonprofit model: organizes recurring Saturday "farmers markets run entirely by kids" (parents help set up/take down only) across ~26 states, aiming for national scale within 2 years; positioned as hands-on entrepreneurship education for children (marketing, sales, customer service, profit motive) that schools generally don't teach.
- Revenue streams: $20 booth fees per kid (skin-in-the-game, minor revenue), branded "I'm an Entrepreneur" T-shirt sales (also minor, carries sponsor logos), and (main long-term source) corporate sponsorships and philanthropic donations — currently ~100% philanthropy-funded per new market launch, with a goal of reducing that to 20-30% of budget as sponsorship/booth/shirt revenue scales.
- Launch playbook per metro area: seek local philanthropic seed capital (~$100,000/year needed to run 10-20 events in a given metro), use their existing email list (from their "Tuttle Twins" children's book brand — ~1 million subscriber households nationwide) to find enthusiastic locals to hire as a market manager, then attach events to existing large draws (city festivals, rodeos, etc. with 5,000-15,000+ existing foot traffic) rather than standalone locations, to guarantee attendance and sales for the kids.
- Reported economics: average kid earns ~$150 in a 3-hour market; typical market draws 60-100 kids (~$10,000 gross revenue flowing through kids per market). Running ~340 markets this year (scaling to ~2,000 within 5 years) implies roughly $3.4M/year in kid-earned transaction volume. Projected total org fundraising (donations + sponsorships combined) this year: roughly $1.5 million.
- Donor profile: average individual donor gift ~$20,000; largest single gift received was $1,000,000, sourced organically through a warm referral from someone who saw a Tuttle Twins ad. Team deliberately focuses fundraising effort on cultivating large potential donors (high-net-worth entrepreneurs/business owners aligned with free-market values) rather than spreading equal effort trying to upsell existing small donors, because the same effort applied to new, larger prospects yields disproportionately bigger gifts.
- Prior attempt at scraping political donor databases yielded poor results — political donors behave differently (fear/reactive, short-term "beat the other guy" motivation) vs. the org's aspirational, long-term-values-based donor base. More effective: identifying which corporations already publicly sponsor comparable youth/financial-literacy programs (e.g., Junior Achievement often lists its corporate sponsors publicly) as a target list, since those corporations have already signaled interest in this cause category.
- Individual donor identity/data is typically confidential (most nonprofits, including Connor's own, don't disclose donor lists), but corporate and foundation donations are often required to be publicly disclosed, making foundations/corporations more scrapable as prospect lists than individual donors.
- Corporate sponsorship outreach at time of filming: early-stage LinkedIn outreach targeting titles like "corporate social responsibility" or "community outreach," building DM sequences, using Hunter.io to find likely email address patterns for cold outreach — described as generating some conversations but no major gifts closed yet.
- Email marketing details: ~1 million email addresses (collected via book purchases/content opt-ins, mostly from moms), emailed twice weekly via Klaviyo; ~55% open rate, ~2-4% click rate; ~95% of email content is about the Tuttle Twins books/ideas (relationship-building/newsletter content), with fundraising asks sprinkled in only occasionally, and separate geo-segmented emails (30-40 mile radius targeting) sent when launching a market in a specific region.

### Koerner's specific tactical suggestions for Connor's corporate fundraising problem
- Segment the ~1M-email list by domain type: separate personal email domains (Yahoo, Gmail) from likely corporate/work email domains to identify people who work at target companies.
- For that corporate-domain subset, move outreach off Klaviyo (built for bulk newsletter sending) and onto a smaller-batch, highly personalized cold-outreach tool like Lemlist or GMass, sending from a personal email address in batches of hundreds (not millions), with heavy personalization (e.g., "I see you work at Coca-Cola and you're on our email list — do you know anyone there who handles corporate sponsorship?") — using existing list members as warm internal referral sources into their employers rather than cold-approaching corporations directly.
- Use third-party data-appending/enrichment services to add company/job-title/LinkedIn data to the existing email list (a specific vendor recommendation was attempted but not confidently recalled on camera); advises testing the same list across multiple appending vendors since pricing varies dramatically (found one ~70-80% cheaper that performed as well as pricier competitors) — test several and use whichever is cheapest with comparable quality.
- Suggests running paid ads (LinkedIn and/or Facebook) targeted at a custom or lookalike audience built directly from the existing ~1M email list, specifically for corporate-sponsorship messaging (separate from their existing consumer book-sales ad campaigns) so prospects see both the email outreach and paid ads around the same time, reinforcing the message.
