# How to Get Everything You Want Out of Life
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IhX-qhFcvlw | Published: 2025-03-19

Interview with Jordan Mix (partner at Greg Isenberg's Late Checkout) on "permissionless projects" — doing unsolicited, high-effort creative work for a specific target person/company to open doors, land clients, or get hired, without being asked and without a stated ask in return. Not a specific product/side-hustle idea; it's a client-acquisition / career-growth tactic directly applicable to freelancers, agency owners, and solo operators trying to land their first customers or a "white whale" relationship.

## Core concept: "permissionless projects"

- Definition: doing real, tangible work *for* a specific target person or company before being asked, hired, or given permission — the work itself is the pitch. Same underlying idea across three framings cited: Ryan Holiday's "canvas strategy" (be a blank canvas influential people can "paint on" by making them look good), Jack Butcher's "permissionless apprentice" (do the job before you have the job), and "you can just do things."
- Explicitly NOT about doing it for a guaranteed monetary outcome — framed as most effective when done because you're genuinely interested in the person/company, with no attachment to a specific payoff; doing it purely as a transactional scheme is said to rarely work.
- Direct business application: this is presented as **the** way to land the highest-value clients/customers/partners ("white whales") — described as more effective than cold LinkedIn DMs, conference small talk, or generic "pick your brain" asks.

## Concrete case studies (with numbers)

- **Custom sneakers for NBA player Mike Conley**: hand-made a pair of Jordan 1s from playoff towels (~40 hours of work); took a $3,000, 4-day sneaker-making course ("The Shoe Surgeon" school in NYC) to learn the skill. Posted about it on Twitter (got ~700 likes, no direct DM back despite heavy outreach to the player's agent, family, and staff). A Grizzlies broadcaster (Chris Vernon), separately trying to get access to Conley, used the viral tweet as leverage to arrange a meeting — resulting in Jordan spending a full day with Conley and teammates. No monetary ask or outcome; treated purely as an experience/relationship win.
- **Custom sneakers for Jack Butcher (Visualize Value)**: made a black Jordan 1 with a white swoosh outline and Velcro-swappable "tongue tags" referencing Butcher's famous minimalist visual sayings. Turned out Butcher lived five minutes away; the gift led to an ongoing friendship. Separately, raffled off one pair of custom Dunks as NFTs for **$8,000** — his only sneakers ever actually sold.
- **Turning Greg Isenberg's tweet into an infographic (the case that led to Jordan's current job)**: saw a tweet from Greg Isenberg that was clearly meant to be visual but wasn't; made the infographic himself (10 minutes) from a brand-new, nameless Twitter account (18 followers) and quote-tweeted Isenberg with it. Isenberg DM'd within minutes asking who made it; they were on a FaceTime call ~10 minutes after the DM. That relationship led directly to Jordan joining Late Checkout, running the "You Probably Need a Robot" newsletter (grew from 30,000 to 880,000 subscribers under him, later merged into the Startup Empire community), and becoming an operating partner at Late Checkout.
- **Cited third-party examples**: Stapleman/Stable (hat brand) sent free hats unsolicited to NBA players stuck in the 2020 bubble, which went viral inside the bubble and led to real NBA/celebrity collabs. A hat maker (referenced as inspiration for the "upside-down Dallas" hats worn by the Dude Perfect crew) gave a free hat to an unknown just-drafted backup Cowboys QB, which snowballed into the whole team wearing them and eventually an "eight-figure brand."
- **A personally-made physical book as a "permissionless project" targeting podcaster David Senra**: after Senra mentioned an obscure, hard-to-read 70-page unpublished PDF biography of Sequoia's Don Valentine and his favorite bookstore in Florida, Jordan OCR'd/typeset/print-on-demanded (via Blurb) a proper physical copy of the book and arranged with the bookstore owner to have it waiting behind the counter for Senra's next visit. **Cost: ~$9 printing + ~$10 shipping (~$20 total) and about 2 hours of his own time** (screenshotting pages during a flight using a screenshot tool with built-in OCR, running through AI to catch typos, using a free online typesetting tool). No specific ask attached — done purely to build goodwill/be remembered favorably.
- **Failed/unrewarded attempts, explicitly discussed as normal**: a polished 30-minute Loom pitch/go-to-market strategy sent unsolicited to ConvertKit got only ~40 views and no direct response — but the relationship "came back around" later (Jordan's company became a Kit customer and was invited to Kit's private mastermind). The same pitch format, reused for Beehiiv, also generated no immediate response but led to an ongoing product relationship. Also mentions ~2 years of unanswered cold outreach ("knocking doors in Eastern Europe," "50,000 rejections") before permissionless projects started working — framed as normal, expected attrition, not evidence the method fails.

## Process / how to find these opportunities (a repeatable framework)

1. **Inventory your own skills and genuine interests** — don't do permissionless projects using skills you dislike; it traps you into unwanted future work.
2. **Make a target list** — specific customers, companies, employers, or individuals you want to work with/for.
3. **Study the target closely ("x-ray vision")** to find gaps: what's on the bottom of their to-do list, what they clearly want but haven't gotten around to, what they can't see about their own business/content.
4. **Do the thing and send it, asking for nothing in return.** Be ready, if they ask why, to name either more value you can add or a specific ask — but don't lead with the ask.
5. Timing/delivery matters: in the Mike Conley case, deliberately waiting for the right, natural in-person moment (rather than handing off the gift the instant it was possible) is what turned a handoff into a full-day relationship.
6. Applies the same logic to scaling from 2 clients to 10: "find eight people on LinkedIn and do stuff that is on the bottom third of their to-do list, and they will end up paying you and becoming a client."
7. **"Third door" outreach** (reference: the book "The Third Door" by Alex [Banayan]): create a distinctive tangible artifact — Late Checkout's example: an expensively, weeks-in-the-making designed physical magazine about product design in the AI era, sent to hand-picked prospect companies instead of a LinkedIn DM or a conference hallway pitch.
8. **Publish/document the process itself as content** — recording an "over-the-shoulder"/behind-the-scenes video of doing the work (a "Loom face in the corner," no heavy editing) is framed as low-friction content that itself attracts interesting people ("it'll create a magnet"). Cited: the interviewee prompted a content creator (Riley Brown) into making a specific in-demand video by publicly asking for it, which then got that creator 50,000 views.

## Broader positioning / business-model context (Late Checkout, not directly a side hustle but informs the tactic's credibility)

- Late Checkout runs an innovation/consulting agency (LCA) for Fortune 500 companies, capped intentionally at 8-12 client engagements/year; average project lifetime value cited around seven figures, sometimes starting as a lower six-figure sprint and expanding. One case cited: added roughly $2 billion in market share for a publicly traded client.
- Their broader playbook, "audience → community → product": build an internet audience, put them in a community, learn what they want, then build/sell them the product — same framework used to launch and later fold "You Probably Need a Robot" into their Startup Empire membership community.
- General framing on career strategy: being a strong "number two" (a top operator/right-hand to a prominent founder/creator) is called an underrated opportunity compared to everyone chasing being the front-facing "number one."

## Caveats / risks

- Explicitly warns against doing this purely to "monetize" or force an outcome — states the approach reliably breaks down ("rarely works out in your favor") if treated as a transactional scheme rather than genuine interest/generosity.
- Most attempts don't yield an immediate visible payoff (the ConvertKit Loom, years of cold outreach) — payoff is often delayed, indirect, or never materializes; the model is framed as a portfolio/compounding "shots on goal" bet, not a guaranteed-return tactic for any single attempt.
- Costs cited are asymmetric-bet-sized (tens of dollars, a few hours to ~40 hours of personal skilled labor) rather than capital-intensive, but do require a real skill or the willingness to acquire one (e.g., a $3,000 sneaker-making course) — not literally "free."
- No product/service is actually sold in most of the examples — value captured is relationship/access/reputation, which is harder to quantify or bank on than direct revenue; only the NFT sneaker raffle ($8,000) and the eventual Late Checkout job are concrete monetary/career outcomes cited.
