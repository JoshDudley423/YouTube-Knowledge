# How to Start a $10k/Month Sleep Consulting Business with $0
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VDxvm4PpIwE | Published: 2024-11-25

Interview with Jayne Havens, founder of The Snoozefest, a pediatric sleep-consulting business and certification program for other consultants.

## The business idea
- Virtual sleep consulting: coach parents (usually of infants/toddlers, also older kids up to ~5) to get their children sleeping through the night, via a written customized sleep plan plus async text/voice-note support.
- Started as an unpaid hobby helping friends, then formalized into a paid service; became a certification-training business for other aspiring sleep consultants.
- Sleep consulting is entirely unregulated — no licensing/governing body — so you can legally practice without certification, but certification (cost, structure, and community below) is presented as valuable both for skill and business credibility.

## Getting started / certification
- Took an online certification course: cost ~$2,400, completed in 4–5 weeks, self-paced (no cohort), came with access to a private Facebook community for questions.
- No prior professional credential required to start; her original edge was years of informally coaching friends for free, which built local word-of-mouth/reputation before she ever charged money.

## Services and pricing
- Primary offering: virtual 2-week consultation package — a customized written sleep plan, a 30-minute onboarding call, daytime text/voice-note support for ~2 weeks, and a wrap-up call.
  - Started at $425/session, raised incrementally over the years ($425 → $500 → $550 → …) to current $750.
- Secondary offering: one-off 30-minute "ask me anything" follow-up call for repeat clients (e.g., nap transitions, post-vacation regressions) — $145/call.
- Personal monthly revenue: currently $8,000–$12,000/month from client work (down from a prior peak because she now focuses more on training other consultants than taking client volume herself).

## Time investment / margins
- Very low overhead/COGS: web hosting, Acuity (online scheduling) subscription, thank-you notecards + postage, and wholesale baby pajamas mailed to each client at contract end (~$9–10/set wholesale + ~$5 postage/packaging). Otherwise the business is almost entirely her own time.
- Time per client: sleep plan ~20–30 minutes (using templates/reusable frameworks after years of practice), onboarding call 30 minutes, wrap-up call 30 minutes = ~1.5 hours of "scheduled" time; text-support load varies heavily by child's age/daycare situation (a maternity-leave mom napping baby 4x/day can mean 4 texts/day; school-age kids need almost no daytime texting). She deliberately now prefers to work with slightly older kids (in school) to reduce real-time text demands.
- At peak volume (before shifting to training), she personally handled ~25 new client families per month (onboarding almost daily) — described as "a lot," not recommended as sustainable for most people. A comfortable steady-state volume is 10–15 families/month.
- Most people in this field do it as a side hustle alongside other jobs/homeschooling/full-time parenting; only some scale it to full-time income.

## Client outcomes / success rate
- Framed success like a personal trainer/nutritionist analogy: outcomes depend on client compliance/follow-through, not just her plan.
- Core method: teach the child to fall asleep independently, so they can self-soothe back to sleep on natural nighttime waking (not "teaching babies not to wake up," which isn't possible).
- Distinguishes "trying" cries (child working at falling asleep unaided) from "panicked" cries (child in genuine distress) — reframes "cry it out" as a normal, temporary part of learning a new skill (comparable to learning to ride a bike or use a potty), which she says reduces parental guilt and increases follow-through.
- Raising her price over time reportedly improved client success rates — clients who pay more are more motivated to follow through and not waste the investment.
- Not all cases are purely behavioral: flags that some sleep issues have a medical root cause (e.g., she diagnosed her own child's dairy allergy contributing to poor sleep, and says she can often remotely spot signs of ear infections or reflux from video of a baby's body language) — refers back to pediatricians when something looks medical rather than behavioral.
- Failure mode observed: clients who pay but never schedule the onboarding call because they're overwhelmed by the proposed plan and aren't ready to start — she doesn't pressure them, just leaves the door open.

## Customer acquisition
- Almost entirely referral/word-of-mouth from past clients — she doesn't have to actively ask because the outcome (resolving a sleep problem in ~14 days after months/years of exhaustion) is dramatic enough that clients volunteer referrals unprompted ("selling a painkiller, not a vitamin").
- She does close calls by mentioning she hopes to "never hear from them again" and to share her name with friends if so — a light, natural referral ask rather than a hard pitch.
- For the certification/training side of the business (attracting new sleep-consultant students), she uses SEO and Facebook groups (posting in mom side-hustle groups, hopping on calls with interested people) rather than Instagram/heavy social media — she explicitly avoids most social media and prefers real personal connection. Runs her own Facebook group ("Becoming a Sleep Consultant") and a podcast by the same name featuring her students' success stories as a funnel.

## Scaling / earnings range among her students
- Reported outcomes among her ~860 certification enrollees vary widely: some earn $1,000–$2,000/month part-time (still called life-changing for the earner), others $15,000–$20,000/month (one student runs 2–3 sub-contracted sleep consultants under her), and one top student reportedly earned ~$40,000/month combining 1:1 coaching with sales of a passive/DIY online course.
- No dominant nationwide franchise exists in this niche as of filming; some training programs offer to certify people and then take a percentage of their client revenue, but she believes that model underperforms because the business is driven by personal relationships/reputation, not a brand name — a franchise model doesn't "rock and roll" here in her view.
- App-based competitors exist (e.g., Huckleberry) that offer in-app paid upgrades to human sleep coaches, but no dominant national franchise as of the interview.

## Demographics / market note
- Field is overwhelmingly women — of ~860 certification enrollees, only ~6 were men (a couple sleep-apnea techs doing continuing ed, one husband-wife duo team, one stay-at-home dad).

## Caveats / risks raised
- Success is capped by client compliance — a consultant "can only be successful if the client is committed to the process"; expect some clients to pay and then not follow through.
- Certification isn't legally required, but going in uncertified means learning by trial and error on real client families ("you don't know what you don't know").
- Building the referral flywheel takes time — her early growth relied on years of unpaid work for friends before the paid business took off; there's no shortcut for the initial reputation-building phase.
- The franchise/agency model (train-then-take-a-cut) is presented as a demonstrated weak model in this niche — worth avoiding if considering scaling via hired-and-managed consultants under one brand.
