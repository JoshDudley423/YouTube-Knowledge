# 21 Business Ideas for 2026 (Part 1/4)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T-zAt4Va7Ws | Published: 2024-04-25

Chris Koerner reads through his personal spreadsheet of 70+ businesses he's personally started (broadly defined: anything he spent hours on intending to generate revenue, whether or not it became a real LLC/had revenue). This is part 1 of a planned multi-part series, covering roughly the first ~15-16 of them in no particular order. Format is autobiographical/anecdotal rather than a structured idea list, but each entry doubles as a mini case study in business models, what worked, and why things were abandoned.

## Used golf balls (age 9, Logan, UT)
- Collected lost golf balls from a ditch near a golf course, washed them with laundry detergent, sold "3 for a dollar" via a large plywood roadside sign. First taste of entrepreneurship; no real numbers, informal.

## Textbook Crook (college, University of Alabama)
- A peer-to-peer textbook marketplace (pre-Facebook Marketplace era) addressing the gap between what campus bookstores pay for used textbooks (~$15) vs. what a classmate would pay directly (~$70) for a book that cost ~$130 new.
- Built cheaply: paid $500 for an existing marketplace website template (called it ugly, logo made in MS Paint). Revenue model: $1 per listing.
- Marketing: printed two-sided color postcards, distributed on car windshields at a community college and university after restaurant shifts.
- Growth hack (legally/ethically gray, presented candidly): received an email accidentally sent to all 28,000 university students without BCC; hit "reply all" and pitched the site to the entire student body. Drove a traffic spike; university IT told him never to do it again. Business ultimately abandoned a few months later (around the birth of his first son) due to insufficient traction -- no specific revenue figures given.

## Clean Cuts (lawn care, age 21, Tuscaloosa, AL)
- Printed flyers, canvassed a neighborhood, got a mowing job -- then quit after one hour because he realized he hated mowing lawns. Lasted a single afternoon; framed as a fast, cheap way to test personal fit for a business type.

## CK Properties (single-family rental real estate, age 22+)
- Started by buying a $90,000 3BR/1BA home in Tuscaloosa, AL, funded partly via an $8,800 [transcribed as $88,000, likely a transcription/speech-recognition error for the actual first-time-homebuyer tax credit amount] first-time-homebuyer tax credit during the Obama-era stimulus; used the credit to buy a second rental (4BR/2BA) for ~$56,000 in a rougher part of town.
- Expanded to a few more properties in Huntsville, AL. Held properties 3-6 years, then began selling off in the mid-2010s.
- Caveat/lesson: managing single-family rentals remotely (after relocating to Texas) became too difficult -- cited as the reason for exiting, not a failure of the underlying investment thesis.

## Phone Restore (iPhone repair chain, started 2010) -- his "first real business"
- Origin: got a free broken iPhone, learned a fellow student was charging $60 to fix screens using a $15 eBay part (a ~10-minute repair) -- recognized the margin opportunity.
- Validated the model by researching an existing out-of-state comp ("MyPhone MD," formerly "iPhone MD" until Apple forced a name change) reportedly doing $20-40k/month from one location.
- Opened first store in Tuscaloosa, AL on a $2,500/month personal-guarantee lease in a strong retail spot (between a Subway and Batteries Plus). Expanded to 4 total locations (Tuscaloosa, Birmingham, two in Huntsville) over a few years.
- Sold the business ~2 years after full build-out; a subsequent business-partner dispute over the sale took a couple of years to resolve but ultimately settled with a "sizable check" -- described as life-changing money for someone in his early 20s, though not enough to retire.

## LCD Cycle (iPhone screen recycling/wholesale, started immediately after selling Phone Restore)
- Origin: received a cold call from a buyer wanting to purchase his repair shop's used iPhone screens for recycling (shipped to China) -- realized he wanted to be on the buying/wholesaling side of that transaction instead of the retail repair side.
- Sourced supply by mass-messaging iPhone screen vendors on Alibaba with a copy-paste pitch; ~1 in 10 vendors were willing to also handle recycling, not just sales. Learned import/export logistics (customs labeling, routing through Hong Kong) from scratch.
- Growth: $28,000 revenue month 1, $56,000 month 2, ~$140,000 month 3 (Feb 2013), closing the first year over $2 million; roughly doubled revenue annually for several years. Ran for about 7 years. Attributed success to strong product-market fit, timing, and heavy cold-calling.
- Preference noted: liked this business much more than face-to-face retail because it removed direct customer service friction.

## Presto Cars (concierge car-buying service, with business partner Nick)
- Model: became a licensed dealer to access wholesale auto auctions (Manheim, Adesa); clients paid a fee to have Presto Cars buy them a specific car at auction at dealer/wholesale rates plus a finder's fee. Target unit economics: attend one 6-hour auction, buy for 10-20 clients, gross $6,000-12,000 profit per auction day.
- Reality: revenue in the "six figures" (roughly $150,000 over a few months) but riddled with operational failures -- clients backing out, cars breaking down, inspection issues, missed target prices -- resulting in roughly breaking even or a small loss.
- Explicitly called "not for me" -- disliked cars/the space personally, and it distracted from the much more successful LCD Cycle running concurrently. Lesson drawn: opportunity cost of running a mediocre business while a great one is available is very high; don't be afraid to abandon/pivot quickly.

## Nobs Crypto (crypto token project with John McAfee, ~2020)
- Token reached a market cap in the "tens of millions" (explicitly caveated as "funny money," not real cash), with tens of thousands of token holders/community members and ~$5,000/month in actual community-related revenue. Framed as a wild, fun story more than a serious financial success (referenced a separate full episode on this).

## Repeat Leads (lead generation / scraping business)
- Sold scraped leads to buyers; mentioned as a business he intends to "reboot" because he still considers the underlying model good, though no specific numbers given.

## Ready MLS (flat-fee MLS listing automation)
- Sent bulk marketing to homeowners who had listed "for sale by owner," pitching them a fully automated ~$99-100 flat fee to get listed on the MLS via the company's broker license (bypassing a traditional agent's 3-6% commission) -- homeowner just uploaded photos/description and paid.
- Chris states he believes this business "could still be very viable today" and doesn't fully recall why it was abandoned.

## All Green Shades (Instagram-based dropshipping)
- Bought an existing themed Instagram account (all-green-colored photo content, ~20,000-40,000 followers) for roughly $500-$2,000, intending to dropship/sell green-colored products to that audience. Pursued half-heartedly for 1-2 months; explicitly called "a pretty stupid idea" in hindsight and quickly abandoned.

## Mellow Migraine (green-tinted glasses for migraine relief, with partners Grant and Max)
- Premise (from co-founder Grant, an engineer and migraine sufferer): green-tinted glasses block light wavelengths that trigger migraines. Imported glasses from China, sold via Facebook ads; product reportedly worked and had some sales, but was abandoned without a clearly stated reason.

## Spot Shave (credit-card-shaped pocket razor, with Grant and Max)
- A razor shaped like a credit card meant to fit in a wallet, solving the "missed a shaving spot and don't have a razor handy" problem. Got to a working prototype but lost momentum due to the difficulty of hardware/physical product development ("hardware is hard"). Domain still owned; idea still considered good by Chris.

## Leah Hona Home Builders (custom home building, with a builder friend)
- Partnership model: friend as the builder/tradesman, Chris as the marketing lead. Built a 5,000-word lead-generation ebook ("how to choose the right custom home builder") distributed via Facebook ads to generate custom-home leads in the DFW area.
- Never generated revenue (unlike most of his other ventures) -- custom homes are a very high-consideration, slow-sales-cycle product; both partners had limited bandwidth (the builder-partner was also a pastor). Lost momentum and was abandoned.

## Assistant Realtor (virtual assistant service for real estate agents)
- Same partner as Leah Hona. Model: real estate agents texted in ad hoc tasks (photo editing, listing description writing), which were outsourced to virtual assistants for a fee.
- Noted as "ahead of the game" conceptually (a VA-for-realtors model that's more common now) but a low-ticket item, and working with realtors specifically was difficult; eventually abandoned without significant scale.

## Halo Maids (on-demand home cleaning, "Uber for home cleaning," same partner)
- Distribution model: partnered with real estate agents to clean new listings before showings (agents referred jobs). Built a polished website, branded shirts, hired cleaners; referenced learning from an unnamed course creator ("run") on how to structure this model.
- Reality: operationally terrible -- roughly 80% of jobs had quality complaints (e.g., hair left in sinks/tubs), cleaners frequently no-showed; Chris was constantly fielding complaint calls from realtors. Shut down (not sold) after generating "thousands" of dollars from completed jobs.
- Lesson drawn: some businesses require reaching real scale to become worthwhile, and reaching that scale is disproportionately hard relative to other business types -- worth recognizing early and pivoting away if the required scale looks unreachable or undesirable.

## Mobile home and RV park acquisitions (with partners, ~25 parks over 18 months)
- Investment thesis: off-market mobile home/RV parks could be acquired at attractive prices; parks performed well operationally and the thesis proved sound.
- Ended abruptly due to an unresolved dispute with business partners (described as a major personal/professional low point) -- explicit lesson: thoroughly vet business partners before entering high-stakes partnerships; the investment thesis itself was not the problem.
- Chris still buys RV/mobile home parks today with different partners, and credits his personal brand/following (built partly around a "mobile home and RV park investing" content thesis, since that information wasn't well covered online) with bringing him investors for current deals.

## Framing / meta-lessons repeated throughout
- Chris counts "tests" as businesses even without an LLC or revenue, because in his view every real business starts as a test.
- Recurring pattern: many of his ventures used real estate agents as a distribution/lead channel (Ready MLS, Assistant Realtor, Halo Maids, the custom home builder lead-gen).
- Recurring theme: willingness to abandon a mediocre business quickly in favor of a better one already in hand, framed as "chasing the greener grass" being rational given high opportunity cost, especially for someone skilled at rapid zero-to-one execution.
- Video is explicitly presented as Part 1 of a planned multi-part series (~35 minutes covering roughly the first third of the full list).

## Caveats
- All figures are Chris's own recollection in a casual, unscripted format ("forget the timelines from here on out because they're all murky") -- treat specific dollar amounts and dates as approximate.
- Several ventures (Repeat Leads, Ready MLS, All Green Shades, Mellow Migraine) ended without a clearly stated reason; the video sometimes just notes they were "abandoned."
