# Taking a Junk Removal Business from $1M to $10M in 2026
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O1kUp_zVHZI | Published: 2024-06-10

Q&A/call-in format episode covering four separate business situations.

## 1. Junk removal + moving expansion (Brett, "Junkless," Bend, Oregon)
- Business: junk removal + residential/commercial moving, junkless.com. Started ~3 years prior with a $2,500 initial investment, grown entirely off cash flow (no outside capital) to ~10 employees (incl. 1 office staff + 1 virtual assistant for backend metrics).
- Current position: near $1M revenue/year, dominant local market share in Bend (population ~150,000) — estimated 40-50% of junk-removal jobs and 10-15% of moving jobs go through them (estimated via reverse-engineering Google search/ad data). One direct franchise competitor (College Hunks); 7 total legitimate junk-removal competitors and 3 moving competitors in Bend.
- Pricing: full truckload junk removal ~$30 cheaper than franchise competitors (industry standard full load: $750-800depending on market) — positions as "the Chick-fil-A or Les Schwab" of the category (clean-cut, good service, competitive but not lowest price).
- Moving pricing is regulated in Oregon (and many US states): intrastate moves under 50 miles must follow a state tariff; interstate is based on weight/distance — this heavily commoditizes moving pricing, making customer service the main differentiator rather than price.
- Cross-sell: ~50% of junk-removal customers eventually need a move within 6-12 months (they retarget via email — ~3,000-person list managed with "Shabber" email tool).
- Expansion plan: open in Portland (3 hours away, ~10x the population of Bend, ~1/3 of Oregon's population), targeting the west side (Beaverton/Nike HQ area) rather than closer/smaller Eugene or Salem, because West Portland demographics (white-collar, no trucks/moving equipment, no interest in DIY) skew similar to Bend's actual customer base despite the distance. Notably, per-capita competition is lower in Portland (5 legit competitors) than in Bend (7), despite Portland being much bigger.
- PPC customer-acquisition cost estimated at $15-$100 per conversion in both markets (comparable).
- Staffing plan: send an existing 1.5-year Bend team member (Ryan, with 3 years prior moving-industry experience) to run the new Portland market as ops manager, backfilled in Bend by another proven internal employee (Bryce). Cheap to start: rent a box truck (e.g., via Penske) rather than buying, so main new-market costs are marketing + one manager's salary.
- Advice given: launch both junk removal AND moving simultaneously in the new market rather than sequencing them — because momentum/first-revenue dopamine matters more than reducing initial complexity, and they've already solved the "run two services" complexity domestically; the real new complexity is entering a different market/culture, not adding a second service line.
- Compensation structure recommended for the manager relocating: avoid promising equity up front (logistically risky, hard to honor later); instead offer a revenue-share tied to territory performance that can convert to equity later (e.g., 10% rev share converting to 5-10% equity), or a straightforward performance-based salary ladder — safer than equity promises that can later disappoint if circumstances change.
- General caution given elsewhere in the episode: buying an existing logistics-heavy small business/route (e.g., a tortilla delivery route or similar seen on BizBuySell advertised at ~2x annual profit, e.g. netting $200K/yr for sale at $400K) is generally a bad idea — these tend to be "buying yourself a job" (a bad one), because the numbers only work if you personally drive the route or hire a ~$75K/year driver, and delivery logistics (missed stops, unsold returns, etc.) are a grind. Avoid buying route/logistics businesses even at attractive multiples.

## 2. Digital product from an influencer's DMs (caller: works adjacent to a 1.1M-follower fitness influencer)
- Situation: caller's wife/sister answer DMs/comments for a fitness influencer (1.1M followers, sells health foods/protein powders) using ad hoc Excel-spreadsheet canned responses; influencer's existing content (Instagram, ebooks, podcast episodes) is scattered/not centralized, website is weak.
- Idea pitched: scrape/compile the influencer's existing public content (Instagram posts, ebooks, podcast transcripts) into an organized paid course (considered Fiverr freelancers + Kajabi/Teachable), then retarget her most-engaged followers via Facebook ads.
- Key advice: don't go solo without her — partnering with the influencer (even giving up majority of revenue) will earn far more than building it alone due to distribution, and going solo raises copyright/ownership risk over her content and likeness.
- Alternative/complementary idea suggested: turn her content into a physical book + audiobook. Cites another operator who reverse-engineered Amazon's algorithm/reviews/Facebook reader groups/Goodreads to sell 20-25 self-published books, and now offers a "done for you" ghostwriting-and-distribution service to other influencers keeping only ~20-30% of proceeds. Physical/audio books have long shelf life (years/decades) and don't cannibalize a paid course — can serve as top-of-funnel for it.
- Deal structure suggested: mirror traditional publishing splits as a starting point — ~70/30 in the operator's favor (or 50/50 if negotiated down), plus ~20% affiliate kickback on sales that go through the influencer's own platforms/links; keep book and course revenue splits consistent for simplicity.
- Tactic: use AI to mine the influencer's existing content/tangential topics for book/course outline ideas she hasn't gotten to herself (she and her team are "spread too thin" across 20 employees to do this themselves) — pitch loosely ("here's a course angle, here's a book angle") and let her choose rather than committing to one path up front.

## 3. Flow Rider (inflatable/surf-simulator) business (caller in College Station, TX)
- Idea: indoor or outdoor "Flow Rider"-style inflatable surf simulator entertainment venue, aimed at families/kids, in a market with ~70,000 college students (Texas A&M) plus young families.
- Equipment cost: buying directly from FlowRider brand is prohibitively expensive; sourcing a similar inflatable, bounce-house-style unit with jets from Chinese manufacturers costs roughly $150,000 (a two-lane in-ground/inflatable version; single-lane mobile inflatable versions cost less but serve fewer riders at once). Potential patent/IP risk copying the FlowRider design — needs legal review (supplier claimed no issue, unverified).
- Unit economics discussed: charge ~$20-25 for an hour-long ride slot; with 10-15 riders/hour on a single lane, that's ~$250-300/hour revenue; on a two-lane setup with ~30 riders/hour at $20/ride, ~$600/hour revenue is plausible at full utilization. At a conservative 50% utilization in peak season, ~$300/hour is realistic. At 10 hours/day in summer, full-capacity days could hit $2,500-6,000/day revenue; a more conservative "average day" estimate of $2,000/day would pay back the $150K equipment cost in about 75 days of full-capacity operation, or roughly a year including labor costs if run only on weekends — framed as a solid ROI if utilization holds.
- Deployment options discussed: fixed indoor installation (usable year-round, avoids extreme heat issue — College Station hits 100°F+ in summer) vs. portable/inflatable version that could be rented out at malls, fairs, and farmers markets to test demand cheaply before committing capital.
- Recommended validation approach: contact the small number of Chinese manufacturers of these machines, get introduced to 3-5 of their prior customers (likely overseas, e.g. Saudi Arabia/Asia resorts), and pay them for their time to learn actual per-hour throughput, revenue, and operating headaches before buying. Also call mall leasing offices directly to gauge interest in hosting the attraction (indoor or in parking lots) — reference to a similar business ("Dig World," a heavy-machinery kids' amusement park sited on a leased 3-acre lot next to a busy mall) as a comparable low-capex leased-land model.
- Caveat: buying a $150K item sight-unseen from an unproven overseas supplier carries real product-quality and durability risk ("those things could leak, you never know"); no direct domestic competitor exists yet to benchmark demand against, so real-world testing (fairs/farmers markets with a portable unit) is recommended before a large capital commitment.
