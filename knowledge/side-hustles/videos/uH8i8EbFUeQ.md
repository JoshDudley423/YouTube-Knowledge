# The Best Marketing Masterclass You'll Hear in 2026
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uH8i8EbFUeQ | Published: 2025-02-21

Podcast-style episode (Chris Koerner + Nik of Cofounders) — primarily a marketing/sales skills breakdown (Frank Kern's 8-step sales framework) plus a method for reverse-engineering SaaS/agency business ideas, and an AI investment framework. Not a step-by-step hustle build, but reusable frameworks for selling anything and finding niches.

## Core sales framework: Frank Kern's 8-step selling process
Traced to internet marketing pioneer Frank Kern (whose techniques were later repackaged and popularized by Alex Hormozi's "$100M Offers"/"$100M Leads"). Steps:
1. **Here's what you get** — list every product/feature/component (overwhelm with value).
2. **Establish value** — assign a high dollar value to each component and sum them ("all these components combined are worth $177,000").
3. **Bonus** — add extra bonuses on top ("I shouldn't be doing this, but...").
4. **Price** — reveal the price partway through, after the value anchor is set (e.g., "not $20,000, just $4,997").
5. **Trivialize the price** — reframe the cost as trivial (amortize it, compare to "a cup of coffee a day" or cheap tuition).
6. **Guarantee** — offer a money-back guarantee ("you're stupid not to take this, there's no risk"). Works partly because most people who could claim a refund never do (compare to billions of dollars in unclaimed/unused gift cards) — cited example: an appliance-repair-type business (LCD.something) added an industry-first lifetime return policy, raised prices ~3-5% to cover expected refund cost, but actual refund rate was closer to 2%, netting them ahead while building trust.
7. **Risk reversal** — explicitly remove perceived risk from the buyer's side (e.g., a car-salesman "my manager will kill me for this price" framing that flips the buyer into feeling like they're the one taking a risk from you, not the reverse).
8. **Scarcity** — critical final step; create urgency (time-limited windows, capped spots) or people delay/forget to buy even if convinced. Example cited: Frank Kern once opened an order page for only 15 minutes, generating viral discussion/FOMO. Another example: large recurring events/festivals intentionally sell ~50% of tickets in the final week at a much higher price, knowing buyers will rationalize paying more since "I missed the cheap window."

## Negotiation tactic: anchoring
- Opening with a lowball offer (e.g., offering $500,000 on a house both parties think is worth $1M) reframes the seller's perception of your budget/leverage, so a later, more reasonable offer feels like a win for them — a psychological framing effect independent of the "true" value both sides agree on.

## Reusable idea-sourcing method: reverse-engineering SaaS/agency niches from public award/leaderboard data
- Method: search award/leaderboard pages for SaaS platforms with public partner/reseller tiers (example used: GoHighLevel's "SaaSpreneur Awards" — Silver = 100+ SaaS customers on the platform, Gold = 500+, Platinum = 1,000+). Look up the winners, then reverse-engineer their business model from their public website/pricing.
- Case study: "Rainmaker AI" (a Frank Kern-run white-labeled GoHighLevel CRM/dashboard product, sold only to his own course community) — its landing page uses a one-page, no-navigation, folksy/low-reading-level copywriting style stating "we've stopped accepting new customers," which manufactures artificial scarcity for existing fans and drives people to want in specifically because it's closed. Estimated revenue (per hosts' rough math, unverified): if ~500 customers pay roughly $400/month for the white-labeled tool while the underlying GoHighLevel wholesale cost is only a few thousand dollars total, that's roughly $200,000/month in near-pure margin — customer acquisition cost close to zero because it's sold only to an existing warm audience.
- Takeaway generalized as a business model: white-labeling an existing SaaS platform (e.g. GoHighLevel) and reselling it to your own audience/niche eliminates most customer acquisition cost, which the hosts frame as normally consuming roughly 50% of typical venture-funded company spend (the other ~50% going to salaries/engineering, which they argue AI is compressing, making marketing/distribution comparatively more valuable going forward).

## Business philosophy / pattern-recognition framework
- "If it's working, it's working for a reason — don't be afraid to copy." Recommends buying gurus' courses not to consume the content but to reverse-engineer their sales funnel (email/text cadence, video sales letters, landing page structure).
- Frank Kern's own career pivots (door-to-door credit card processing → selling credit card terminals online → pet training audiobooks, ~$14M over 7 years selling ~300,000 units at $47 each, ~100% margin as a digital product) illustrate: (1) you often don't know your final niche until you're in the process experimenting, stay flexible; (2) once you find something that converts with your existing skill set (in his case, sales ability), pursue it aggressively regardless of the original plan (same pattern cited in Amazon's pivot from bookstore to AWS being its most profitable segment).
- Paying for a course/coach/bootcamp to shortcut a learning curve in an unfamiliar niche is framed as a legitimate "cheat code," distinct from also needing standard professional services (CPA, lawyer) when buying/running a business.

## AI investment/business-opportunity framework (two strategies)
1. **"Cigarette butt" strategy** (per Warren Buffett's early investing style — extracting value from a still-profitable but declining asset): businesses/agencies built purely around implementing today's AI tooling (e.g., an "AI automation agency") have a real but finite window of opportunity, because the underlying capability will become commoditized/ubiquitous and that market will shrink.
2. **Long-term strategy**: identify what will remain valuable/scarce even after AI diffuses everywhere — generally, businesses built around human interaction and fundamental human needs (healthcare, gyms/fitness, in-person conferences/events, food, purpose, religion, face-to-face/physical touch).
- Related observed pattern ("rebundling"/what's-old-is-new-again cycle): cable → unbundled into individual streaming apps → now streaming services are "rebundling" (e.g., live sports/events folded into Amazon Prime, Netflix), predicted to keep recurring; likewise community shifted from in-person to social media and is now shifting back toward in-person events/conferences/"detox" experiences.
- Practical business implication drawn: content/copy that is too polished or too obviously optimized now reads as inauthentic and underperforms — audiences increasingly self-select toward content/communication that feels authentically human (a shift the hosts frame as a byproduct of AI making "too-perfect" content ubiquitous).
- General framing: even as AI automates more digital/technical work, demand for hands-on, human-facing local services (e.g., tree trimming, home services) is expected to grow rather than shrink, since overall attention/talent is being pulled toward AI/tech, leaving more room in overlooked "boring" local service niches.
