# The Most Profitable Local Business Everyone Overlooks
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6Q6jS1-5pkw | Published: 2025-07-17

Guest: Alex Boyd (Brushworks Co, near Cincinnati, OH) — forestry mulching / land clearing business.

## The idea
- Forestry mulching: using a skid steer with a mulching head attachment to clear overgrown brush, vines (honeysuckle), fence lines, and small trees (up to 6-8" diameter) from rural/suburban properties. A niche, largely unadvertised home-service category — not even listed as a category on Thumbtack or Angie's List.
- Origin: Alex bought a 5-acre property (4 wooded), couldn't find anyone to clear it within 100 miles, and realized via informal keyword research that there was major latent demand with almost no organized competition.

## Numbers
- Startup equipment: used skid steer with mulching head, ~$90,000 total (including a used $15,000 forestry head); later added a second, new machine for ~$129,000-$130,000 (new head alone was $38,000). Forestry-spec skid steers need: high hydraulic flow, a forestry kit (~$15,000: cab fall guard, 3/4" polycarbonate window screens, debris shielding) — a standard rental skid steer (~$25K, no attachments) won't do this work.
- Rental cost comparison: Bobcat charges ~$2,700/day to rent a forestry-kitted machine, and often won't rent to individuals — only to forestry companies — which pushed Alex toward buying instead.
- Financing: new machine got 0% financing for 60 months with only $5,000 down (helped by a Bobcat sales event; financing via Ducson, which owns Bobcat); the used machine was financed at ~7.8%. Loan officers reportedly favor borrowers with a track record of running successful businesses, but strong personal/spousal credit compensates for lack of business history.
- Monthly loan payments: ~$1,400/month on the $90K machine, ~$1,900/month on the $130K machine — target is to cover the payment with a single job's revenue, keeping effective overhead "negligible."
- Shop/storage: rents a 2,000 sq ft heated shop space for $1,500/month (needed after storms/weather damaged equipment stored at home; heating enables winter operation).
- Revenue trajectory: Month 1 ~$17,000; Month 2 ~$20,000s; one machine maxed out at roughly $30,000/month (with one operator) by month 3, sustained through November (seasonal, weather-dependent — 15 months into business by time of filming). After adding a 2nd machine in May, monthly revenue jumped: one month cited at $54,000 (during a historically rainy spring that limited operating days); other months around $70,000/month scheduled through October. Projected around $500,000/year with 2 machines running ~9 months if fairly normal weather.
- Average job size: ~$3,100, roughly translating to ~1.5 days of work (billed by half-day/full-day/multi-day, not by acreage — see pricing notes below).
- Gross margin: 69.75% (already includes setting aside 15% of revenue for equipment repairs); without the repair reserve, margin is ~85%. Cost per lead: ~$23 average. Close rate: 49% (was 44% at first).
- Simple unit economics Chris highlights: ~$46 cost to convert a $3,100 customer.
- Labor: pays a subcontracted second operator $300/day for a full day's work (roughly $100K/year if working every business day) — cited as comparable to or better-paid than harder outdoor jobs like landscaping.
- Market ceiling estimate (Alex's own local area only): could support roughly 10 machines running at full utilization if capturing majority local market share; Chris's back-of-envelope: at $2,000/day/machine, 5-6 days/week, that's ~$6M/year revenue, and even at a diluted ~50% margin (as more staff are hired) that's a ~$3M/year net profit business in a single local market.

## Process / how he found and validated the niche
1. Noticed a personal, unmet need (couldn't get his own overgrown land cleared) and searched for existing local competitors — found none within 100 miles, only unbranded owner-operators posting on Facebook Marketplace.
2. Because "forestry mulching" isn't a term most consumers know, he did informal keyword discovery: asked friends/family what they would actually search for if they wanted this service (e.g., "clear my woods," "get rid of this wild rose," "destroy honeysuckle"), then plugged those candidate phrases into a keyword research tool (SEMrush, using its free trial — he "almost never" pays for these tools) to check search volume before committing.
3. Found existing tree service companies get asked about this work often but don't fulfill it — they lack the specialized machine and don't know how to sell/market the service — identifying a supply gap rather than a demand gap.
4. Bought the machine before booking a single job, but only started marketing once the machine was ordered; first ad was assembled using borrowed footage from Bobcat's own YouTube demo video before he had his own footage. Got his first job booked within 2 days of starting ads.

## Marketing playbook (the substantive part of the video)
- **YouTube as "content marketing," not audience-building**: posts raw job footage (before/after, machine in action) tagged with local keywords (e.g., "Cincinnati forestry mulching") specifically so the videos surface in local Google searches — doesn't care about view count or growing a general audience. ~80% of customers had watched the YouTube channel before hiring (some watched every video); YouTube is described as directly supporting/reinforcing the Meta ad strategy, not a separate channel.
- **Meta (Facebook/Instagram) ads as interruption marketing**: distinguishes Meta ads (interrupting people scrolling who weren't looking for the service) from Google Ads (bidding on people already searching). Meta ad creative: fast-cut highlight-reel style video — machine mulching a big overgrown area, local landmark drone shot to establish "we're local," then a problem/solution framing (e.g., "clear your land fast"); video ads dramatically outperform image ads (he doesn't run image ads at all — "people just scroll right past").
- **Precise Meta targeting, not relying on Facebook's built-in geographic/interest targeting**: manually picks specific rural zip codes likely to contain large-acreage landowners, targets age 35+, and layers in interest targeting for Tractor Supply Company / Rural King (brands associated with land ownership) — explicitly distrusts Facebook's own audience algorithm.
- Runs ads across every available placement (feed, stories, reels, audience network, etc.) rather than picking one, reasoning that the addressable audience (land-owning, 35+, in a handful of rural zip codes) is small enough that maximizing exposure across placements matters more than optimizing for one channel.
- Deliberately welcomes future competitors entering the niche ("grow the pie") because the category is under-known; more advertisers educate more of the market on what forestry mulching even is.
- Niche-community marketing: an ad specifically about clearing fence lines unexpectedly resonated with the horse-owning community (tight-knit, land-owning, motivated by needing clear paths to walk horses around fences) — drove strong word-of-mouth referrals within that community.
- Lead capture: uses Meta's native lead ads (captures name/phone/email inside the platform) rather than sending traffic to a separate landing page; leads trigger an email + Telegram notification (no CRM automation set up — considered House Call Pro's API but skipped it because it required their $350/month "Max" plan just for API access, deemed not worth it).
- Sales process: always calls the lead and books an in-person quote rather than trying to close over the phone — believes an expensive service needs a face-to-face trust moment; competitors who quote sight-unseen off satellite imagery or charge $50 for an in-person quote get negative feedback from customers.
- Ad decay: relied on essentially one core lead-ad concept for a long time, acknowledges it needs refreshing periodically (planning a new, exaggerated "'90s infomercial" style ad — a lawn mower rider getting hit in the face by overgrown brush, since "brush keeps hitting me in the face" is the #1 customer complaint).
- Organic growth: after ~13 months, organic search now drives ~36% of web traffic — grew mainly from the YouTube/X presence, not from Google Business/local directory listings (forestry mulching isn't even a category on Angie's List/Thumbtack).

## Pricing lessons
- Prices by half-day/full-day/multi-day block, not by acreage/square footage — tried switching to acreage + brush-density based quoting for scalability (so hired sales reps wouldn't need his personal experience to estimate) but close rate dropped by half; reverted, even though the actual quoted price came out the same either way. Hypothesis: customers prefer knowing exactly what a "day" costs and like the flexibility to extend a day if they want more done, rather than parsing a formula.
- Occasionally misjudges job duration (a job quoted as a full day might take 3 hours, in which case he charges a half day; a job estimated at one day sometimes runs multiple days because you can't always see 100+ feet into unclearable woods until you're cutting into it).
- 15% of revenue is set aside specifically for equipment repairs/maintenance given how hard this work is on machinery.

## Referral relationships
- Tried a referral-commission arrangement with a local tree service (sending them work the mulcher couldn't handle) — the tree service never actually paid the agreed commission despite Alex sending them an estimated 80% of their schedule; he no longer refers work to them exclusively, spreads referrals across "a few good companies" instead.
- Separately planning to build a YouTube/content-marketing package as a paid side project for an unrelated, established ($6M/year) tree service company that has been referral-only and never advertised.

## Adjacent/expansion idea raised (unclaimed niche)
- The "Green Climber": a remote-controlled, joystick-operated mulching/mowing machine (operator stands ~45 degrees off to the side, not below it) that handles steep hillsides and ditch lines a skid steer can't reach — used mainly by government/highway crews for roadside hillside clearing, virtually unmarketed to residential/commercial customers.
- Rental cost ~$1,000/day; Alex charges customers ~$3,000/day when he rents one to fulfill jobs too steep for his skid steer. Purchase price quotes varied wildly by source: ~$70,000 for smaller units, ~$150,000 for larger ones (equipment dealers were described as unusually opaque about pricing).
- Alex explicitly floated this as an open opportunity for a viewer to start (commercial parking-lot edges, steep embankments) — comparable to a door-to-door pressure-washing-style service — since "nobody's targeted it" for the average property owner/small business, only government.

## Caveats / risks
- Highly weather-dependent: rain turns ground to mud and stops operations (Alex cites one of the highest recorded rainfall spring seasons hurting monthly revenue); winter slows most residential jobs but large farm/horse properties don't mind ruts and can be worked year-round, so targeting acreage/horse-farm clients can reduce seasonality.
- Growth is capital- and logistics-constrained: a single flatbed truck can only move machines one at a time between job sites; adding a 3rd machine isn't feasible without a dedicated larger truck/trailer that can haul two machines simultaneously, or a person whose sole job is moving equipment.
- Machine size/tree diameter limits: can only mulch trees up to 6-8" diameter (softer wood higher, hardwood lower); anything bigger requires a tree service, so know when to refer out rather than force it.
- Getting into forestry mulching (or the Green Climber niche) requires meaningful upfront capital (tens of thousands of dollars) even with financing, and financing terms depend heavily on personal credit/business track record.
- Pricing structure is finicky — deviating from the "simple day-rate" approach visibly hurt conversion even when the effective price was unchanged; underscores that reducing customer confusion matters more than analytical elegance.
