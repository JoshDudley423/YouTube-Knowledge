# An Insider Spills the Secrets to Starting a $1M+ SaaS Agency
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_yMXoZVAVKs | Published: 2025-03-12

Guest: Robin Alex, co-founder of HighLevel (GoHighLevel) — an all-in-one CRM/marketing-automation platform used as the backend for "SaaSpreneur" agencies. HighLevel stats cited: ~100,000 customers, ~1,700 employees, ~2.5-3 million businesses represented, ~5 million users worldwide, 7 years old.

- Core business idea: build/run a "SaaSpreneur" agency — white-label HighLevel's tools (CRM, automations, funnel builder, texting, calendar, invoicing, etc.) as your own branded software/marketing service and charge small businesses a recurring monthly fee, without building any software yourself.
- HighLevel's own origin story: Robin ran a traditional marketing agency (25 employees, ~200 clients at peak) generating leads via Facebook/Google ads, but clients churned because they didn't know what to do with the leads once generated. He built an internal tech stack (~15 connected apps/APIs) to manage the full lead-to-close pipeline, then discovered two developers (Sean and Varun) building a reputation-management tool, showed them his internal system, and they built it into what became HighLevel v1. Pricing has stayed the same since near the beginning: **$97/month for one location, $297/month unlimited locations**.
- Business model economics that make HighLevel-based agencies profitable: HighLevel passes through backend costs (e.g., Twilio-powered SMS) near cost to the reseller/agency, who then marks it up to their own end customer — e.g., SMS costs HighLevel a fraction of a cent per message; agencies can charge a markup (roughly 4x cited) so that for every $20 an end customer spends on messaging, the agency nets around $5, without touching their base platform fee.
- Getting-started playbook (the "least path of resistance" to launching an agency), pre-2025 approach: walk into local small businesses in person and offer, for free, to install (1) a live chat widget and (2) a lead-capture form on their website — free to demonstrate value, no cost to you as the operator. After providing free value for a period, ask the business owner directly what they'd be willing to pay ("I'm thinking of charging $X — what would you pay?") as a live pricing/market-research technique; use each paying customer as a reference/testimonial to close the next one (e.g., "Sally down the street pays me this much, you can call her").
- Updated 2025 pitch (what Robin says he'd do today): same widget + form, but reframe the pitch around "AI" specifically, and replace "missed-call text-back" with a full **AI voice/answering agent**. Rationale: missed-call text-back (auto-texting a caller who hung up because no one answered) captures interest but stalls once the customer replies with a real request (e.g., "my toilet is overflowing") because no human follows up — a true AI voice agent can carry the entire conversation, collect details (address, etc.), and actually book the appointment or close the loop, producing a "significant" response-rate lift over text-only follow-up.
- Reported cold-approach success rates (from Robin's own agency-building experience): roughly **85-90% of local businesses visited already had meaningful existing website/search traffic they weren't capitalizing on** (people search for known local businesses like plumbers or pizza shops directly); of businesses approached, close rate started around **1 in 25** early on, improving significantly over time as testimonials/referrals accumulated (leading to introductions via local Chambers of Commerce, etc.). For businesses lacking traffic, the strategy shifts to partnering with or reselling a paid-traffic specialist (Google/Facebook ads) rather than doing it yourself.
- Pricing philosophy: start "embarrassingly cheap" to build initial case studies/confidence, but deliberately raise prices over time — undercharging is framed as actively harmful because low price signals low value. Cited real pricing tiers agencies charge downstream end customers: **$500 to $5,000+/month**, with some SaaSpreneurs reportedly charging **tens of thousands of dollars/month** to very high-revenue clients (e.g., pool companies) where a single converted lead can be worth $50,000-$500,000 (a pool installation project), making a $3,000-10,000/month fee easily justified by expected ROI (e.g., 20 leads/month converting 5 closes).
- Pay-per-lead model explicitly discouraged vs. flat monthly fee: businesses tend to mentally "pre-budget" a fixed lead quota, then pause once they hit it and stop consistently reordering; a predictable flat monthly fee (likened to a Netflix subscription) sustains longer, more stable recurring revenue than a usage/lead-based charge.
- Best/most lucrative verticals cited by volume and value: independent solopreneurs (insurance agents, real estate agents, mortgage brokers) are the highest-volume adopters; fitness studios next; then **home services** (plumbers, roofers, solar installers, remodelers, pool companies) are called out as especially strong because of high average ticket size (roofing average ticket cited at $15,000-50,000) and clear ROI math, making them willing to pay premium agency fees. A concrete example cited: a HighLevel SaaSpreneur running "Chiropractic leads for Spanish-speaking chiropractors" reportedly made ~$130,000/month with only 20-30 customers by going deep in a specialized, high-LTV niche.
- Customer targeting tactic suggested by the host (validated by Robin): scrape a niche's public listings (e.g., Zillow real estate agents) sorted by review count/volume as a proxy for which prospects already run a team and understand the value of leads (more likely to pay for and value additional lead flow) — described as a smarter way to prioritize outreach than blind cold-calling.
- "White whale" / franchise strategy (Robin's own recommended approach if starting today): target one franchisee within a small-to-midsize franchise network (roughly 10-40, or even up to ~200, locations) rather than large famous franchises; deliver strong results for that single franchisee, and word tends to spread within the franchise network, potentially leading either to organic adoption by many other franchisees, or in some cases the franchisor itself acquiring/mandating the tool network-wide. Concrete example given: an operator built a SaaSpreneur agency working with individual dentists inside a ~30-location DSO (dental service organization); once results were visible, the DSO's private-equity ownership bought out his agency for roughly **$6 million after only 18 months**, on the thesis that rolling it out to all 30 locations (with growth to more) could add $30 million in value — with a contractual requirement that the seller stay on ~12 months to help implement across all locations before backfilling his role. Another cited example (via HighLevel's "SaaSpreneur Awards" public leaderboard): an operator scaled to hundreds of locations from a single franchise partnership within an eight-month-old agency, reaching eight-figure revenue.
- Reported top-end SaaSpreneur revenue benchmarks: definitely eight figures annually at the high end; one reported to be approaching **nine-figure valuation on an exit**; another sold in the "high eight figures." Robin notes investor interest has shifted from valuing the underlying tech/IP to valuing the predictability of the sales/customer-acquisition model itself — meaning a scrappy, well-proven SaaSpreneur agency can be an attractive acquisition target even without proprietary technology.
- Certification/consulting ecosystem: HighLevel runs a **Certified Partner Program** (~$100/month, ~30-40 hours of training content, a self-test, then a live Zoom-proctored practical exam building a real case study) that publicly lists certified agencies in a directory and actively feeds them leads/customers; certification expires and must be renewed, intended to build long-term credibility (explicitly modeled on the Salesforce-certified-developer ecosystem, aiming to eventually make "HighLevel certified" a common job-listing requirement). A separate, growing market of third-party **HighLevel implementation/consulting firms** exists (analogous to Salesforce/NetSuite implementation shops), handling migrations, custom automations, and integrations for mid-market and larger ($100M-$1B+) companies moving off tools like HubSpot.
- Caveat noted by the guest: getting leads via the certified directory doesn't guarantee execution — many new entrepreneurs get "leads but freeze on execution due to analysis paralysis"; his advice is to take the easy, low-friction wins first rather than over-engineering the perfect setup before starting.
- General observation on why HighLevel succeeded despite conventional wisdom ("if you're everything to everyone, you're nobody to no one"): the underlying CRM/automation technology itself is described as commoditized ("like corn") with limited remaining differentiation, so the winning strategy was consolidating many previously separate paid tools (CRM, Zapier-style automation, texting platform, calendar booking, funnel builder, pipeline/Kanban board like Trello or Pipedrive) into one platform at one price — saving customers both direct subscription costs (cited example: a prospect was paying ~$300/month for Zapier alone, plus separate costs for Pipedrive, Trello, Calendly) and the hidden operational/training cost of juggling multiple disconnected tools across a team.
