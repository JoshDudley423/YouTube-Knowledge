# Everything I Know About Newsletters in 24 Min
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C1C9QK9ak18 | Published: 2025-03-03

Q&A format episode; three listener questions answered, the third (newsletter deep-dive) is the densest.

## Q1: Recovering from business failure / considering buying a business
- Listener context: built a business to ~$500k revenue, lost it almost overnight, tried multiple pivots (digital marketing, AI, e-commerce) for 2+ years without success, ended up filing for bankruptcy.
- Advice given:
  1. Consider buying an existing business instead of starting another from scratch ("entrepreneurship through acquisition" / ETA) — framed as a way to skip the line to profitability, assuming you already have general business/management skills.
  2. There are far more businesses that should be sold (owners burning out or about to fail) than there are active buyers — cited estimate that sites like BizBuySell only surface ~2% of businesses that could realistically be bought if approached directly.
  3. Tactic: post in your local Facebook community group that you're looking to buy a business, with a personal pitch (who you are, size/revenue range you want, family picture) — cited anecdote of someone generating ~a dozen leads this way and buying a seller-financed business.
  4. Raise a down payment from friends/family who know and trust you, then seller-finance the rest of a "sweaty startup" — an ugly, blue-collar business that could benefit from added technology.
  5. If buying isn't viable yet: keep the W2, spend an hour a day learning/using AI tools (Replit, Perplexity, ChatGPT, etc. — called "the most asymmetric bet you can make on yourself"), and keep testing small things on nights/weekends.

## Q2: Too many ideas, none executed ("Uber for tree delivery" caller)
- Caller already runs a tree delivery service and has a notebook full of unexecuted ideas.
- Core advice: the skill of generating ideas should be redirected toward generating customer-acquisition ideas for the one business already working, rather than spawning new business concepts.
- Validation method recommended over "asking people if it's a good idea": use free, widely available tools to test if people will actually pay — Facebook Ad Library, Facebook housing/marketplace-style landing page tests, Etsy, Facebook groups.
- Practical exercise suggested: write down every existing customer and exactly how each was acquired, then brainstorm ways to do more of what already works, instead of layering a new idea (like "Uber for tree delivery") on top of an already-working but young business.

## Q3: Financial-industry newsletter writer wanting to go independent (main segment)
- Situation: caller writes a global-markets email newsletter for their finance firm, reaching 30,000+ institutional clients (list wasn't organically built — inherited from firm's existing client base); strong positive feedback; wants to launch a personal newsletter/Substack but is under a non-compete and can't solicit the firm's existing subscriber list.
- Math floated by caller: 1,000 paid subscribers at $30/month = $30,000/month.
- Framing: any newsletter business breaks into two parts — fulfillment (writing) and customer acquisition (growth). Caller already has fulfillment solved; needs to build acquisition from scratch with a fresh audience (not poaching the firm's list) to stay clear of the non-compete.
- **Why newsletters are attractive vs. e-commerce**: e-commerce (DTC/Shopify) can be very profitable per unit economics (e.g., cited example: $75 product, $8 CAC, 70% gross margin, ~$10 fulfillment cost → putting in $1 and getting back multiples) but is operationally brutal — fulfillment, logistics/UPS disputes, damaged/lost goods, customer service. Newsletters keep the same binary, ad-driven acquisition mechanics without any of the physical fulfillment overhead.
- Caveat noted from a related episode: e-commerce businesses growing 20%/month at a 20% margin structurally are never cash-flow positive even while accounting-profitable — a hidden trap of the model.

### Co-registration ("co-reg") mechanics — the core growth lever explained
- Definition: after a user subscribes to your newsletter, a popup offers additional partner newsletters (boxes pre-checked) that the subscriber can one-click join, uncheck, or dismiss.
- Two sides of the marketplace:
  - **Selling your subscribers to partner newsletters** (revenue side): partner newsletters pay roughly $1-$10 per subscriber referred, conditional on the subscriber taking specific actions (e.g., opening/clicking X times within a set window, e.g. 2-3 weeks) — you're only paid if conditions are met, and if the subscriber unsubscribes early you may still owe/lose the fee depending on terms.
  - **Buying subscribers from partner newsletters** (cost/acquisition side): pay other newsletters $1-$3 per referred subscriber to grow your own list, again typically contingent on engagement milestones so you don't pay for dead leads.
- Illustrative math (revenue side): $2 Facebook-ad cost per acquired subscriber, worth ~$6 in expected 12-month subscriber value (a 3x ROAS) even before co-reg. Layering in co-reg: best case (all 5 partner newsletters convert at $3 each) adds $15, turning $2 into $21 (10x ROAS) — but the presenter says this is unrealistic; realistic average is closer to 1.92 of 5 partner offers converting, yielding roughly $0.50-$1.50 in extra co-reg revenue per subscriber, meaning effective CPA roughly halves (e.g., 3x ROAS becomes ~6x).
- Caveat/tension noted: there's a theory (unconfirmed by the presenter's own data so far) that subscribers who are shown 3-5 competing newsletter offers at signup may be less loyal/less likely to open the newsletter they originally intended to join, since attention gets split; conversely, subscribers acquired cheaply through someone else's co-reg popup (low intent — they clicked based on one sentence, may not know who you are) are described as lower quality/loyalty than subscribers acquired to your own ad directly.
- Presenter's own reported results: describes co-reg revenue as a "six-figure line of profit" (not revenue) recently, added ~30,000 subscribers in the described month (best month to date), keeps list clean by deleting non-openers, sustains ~55% open rate and 4-7% click-through rate.

### Practical growth/ad-testing process recommended
- Produce 5-10 short (5-15 second) videos built around a lead magnet relevant to the niche (e.g., stock picks for a finance newsletter).
- Pair with 5 headlines, 5 descriptions, 5 body texts — run all combinations (5 creatives x 15 text variants) through Meta's ad system.
- Use "Advantage+ audience" (let Meta's algorithm find the audience rather than manually targeting).
- Expect CPA to start high ($5-8) and decline as the algorithm optimizes/learns.
- Recommendation for monetizing a free list: start entirely free, then try converting an estimated 1-2% of free subscribers to paid over time once trust is established (presenter has not personally tested a paid tier).
