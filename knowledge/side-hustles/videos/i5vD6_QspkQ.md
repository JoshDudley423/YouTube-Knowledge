# The Most Overlooked Path to Wealth
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i5vD6_QspkQ | Published: 2026-08-19

Interview with Alex Smereczniak, co-founder of Franzy (a franchise research/education platform), covering four specific low-cost/high-revenue franchise concepts plus general franchise-buying strategy. Alex says he's read "thousands" of Franchise Disclosure Documents (FDDs).

## Franchise concept 1: Freight brokerage
- Model: B2B — you match shippers who need goods moved with trucking/logistics carriers, and you keep the spread (margin) between what the shipper pays and what the carrier charges. Can be run from home or a small office; no inventory, no trucks owned by you.
- Featured brand: a mature UK/Europe-based concept (15+ years there) newly expanding into the US, headquartered in Dallas.
- Numbers: average franchisee revenue is a little over $1M/year; average gross margin ~40% (~$400K gross margin on $1M rev); franchisor royalty is 30% of gross margin (not revenue) — one of the highest royalty rates in franchising after Chick-fil-A — leaving roughly 28% net margin on average (~$280K on $1M revenue) before local overhead.
- Worked example: a $10,000 shipment generates ~$4,000 gross margin (~40%); franchisor takes 30% of that margin ($1,200), leaving $2,800 to cover local costs or pocket directly if solo/home-based.
- Startup cost: $75,000-$193,000 total per the FDD; franchise fee itself is $35,000-$60,000 (this brand: ~$50,000); remainder covers technology/software fees, training, and legally-required 3 months of working capital (Alex recommends budgeting 6-9 months in practice, not just the FDD-mandated minimum of 3).
- Financing: can finance roughly 70-90% via SBA loans, meaning as little as $7,500-$40,000 of personal/borrowed cash needed on the low end of the range.
- What the franchisor provides in exchange for the high royalty: the back-end technology/software, help finding initial customers/carrier relationships, invoicing, and carrier payment handling — franchisee is "effectively just B2B sales."
- Revenue is described as recurring/sticky once relationships are built (low churn, upsell potential into warehouse/facility management services).
- Fit: best for people who are people-persons/extroverts comfortable with phone-based relationship selling and comfortable working from home; a personal anecdote is given of someone with no formal training who does well in independent (non-franchise) freight brokerage.
- Alex's "maturity score" (1-10 scale, where 10 = McDonald's/Subway level maturity): rates this concept 6-7 overall due to European track record, but only 2-3 in the US specifically since there's no established US brand in this niche yet — meaning higher risk of an unproven market fit domestically.
- Risk flagged: being new to the US, you should ask the franchisor directly why the market hasn't been served this way before, whether cultural/regulatory differences from UK/Europe could hurt performance, and check the FDD for separate UK/Europe vs. US location performance data.
- AI/automation resilience: framed as relatively AI-resistant in the near term (5-10 years) because large shipment brokering still benefits from a human relationship/accountability layer, similar to how AI hasn't eliminated demand for human wealth managers or accountants.

## Franchise concept 2: Commercial facility management
- Model: acting as a single point of contact ("one throat to choke") for commercial property owners, coordinating subcontracted landscaping, HVAC, janitorial, parking lot striping, etc. — distinct from full property management in that you don't handle leasing/tenants, only maintenance/facilities.
- Numbers: this is described as one of the highest-revenue franchise categories seen — the top franchisee in the FDD did over $50M in revenue in 2025; system-wide average is about $9-9.8M in average unit revenue, with an average gross margin around 33.5% (~$3.3M average gross margin). Revenue is high largely because it's pass-through-like (the franchisee bills for subcontracted services like janitorial/landscaping, similar to a marketplace GMV model), meaning a large majority of revenue flows out to subcontractors.
- Royalty: only 5% of gross sales (much lower than freight brokerage) — because the franchisor provides comparatively less (mainly brand awareness, some landlord/tenant relationships); the franchisee is explicitly responsible for sourcing and vetting their own subcontractor network, which Alex calls "the headache" of the business.
- Startup cost: $229,000-$410,000 total (per FDD); roughly $29,000-$100,000 in actual cash needed if financing the rest via SBA.
- Fit: ideal for people already embedded in commercial real estate/contracting relationships (leasing agents, GCs, property managers in their 40s-60s with existing contractor networks) or people with 5+ years of B2B sales leadership experience — Alex says the biggest differentiator among the highest-performing (e.g., $50M) franchisees was prior B2B sales experience, since the business is fundamentally sales leadership over an outsourced labor network. Poor fit for someone with no sales background going in cold.
- Founded in 1961 (long track record); Alex rates it 8-9 on the maturity scale — well past 50 open units, considered lower risk/more proven.
- Recommended operating approach for most franchises generally (not specific to this one): run it as an owner-operator for the first year (do the hands-on work yourself) before hiring a management layer, unless you already have significant business-ownership experience and can hire a GM from day one.

## Franchise concept 3: On-site cooking-oil filtration/recycling for restaurants
- Model: a specialized truck visits restaurants (mainly quick-service/QSR and independent restaurants with fryers), runs a hose in to filter and chemically treat used cooking oil on-site (removing solids and restoring usability), or does a full oil replacement — removing the old oil and selling it downstream (e.g., as biofuel feedstock) while selling the restaurant clean new oil. Also offers full drain cleaning/replacement and refrigeration/freezer cleaning and maintenance as additional revenue lines (4-5 total service lines at the time of recording); does not yet do hood/vent cleaning but the guest speculates they might expand into it.
- Revenue model note: multiple monetized touchpoints per customer relationship — paid for the on-site filtering/recycling service AND for selling replacement oil, in addition to the ancillary drain/refrigeration services.
- Market size context: cited stats — roughly 1 million total franchised retail locations in the US, ~400,000 of which are food-related; independently owned ("mom and pop") restaurants outnumber those roughly 2-3x, meaning total addressable restaurant count is far larger than just the ~30,000 McDonald's-scale chains — a large, recurring, unavoidable service need (oil must be serviced/replaced periodically) across a very large restaurant universe.
- Numbers: single-territory average revenue is $517,000; multi-territory operators average $1.5M+ revenue — attributed to reusing the same truck/equipment base to service a wider range of businesses (economies of scale) rather than needing proportionally more capital per territory.
- Footprint: founded/operating in the US since 2002 (UK origin); has 377 territories across 119 franchisees at time of recording (average franchisee owns ~3.2 territories); Alex estimates room for a few hundred more territories domestically — a relatively mature, still-growing system. Rated roughly 7-8 on the maturity scale.
- Startup cost: $140,000-$163,000 to start with one truck; roughly $15,000-$50,000 in actual cash if financing the rest.
- Fit: heavy B2B sales/relationship business, but requires trucks/equipment (not purely remote/home-based like freight brokerage), and services must recur on a set frequency, creating stable repeat revenue once a customer base is built.

## Franchise concept 4: Mobile commercial fuel and DEF (diesel exhaust fluid) delivery
- Model: delivers fuel and DEF directly to commercial fleets, long-haul trucking operations, local logistics companies, and heavy equipment (e.g., Bobcats) on-site so customers avoid extra stops — strictly B2B/commercial (does not serve consumers or boats).
- Numbers: average revenue is just under $5M; but margins are thin (fuel itself is low-margin, "GMV-style" economics) — roughly $1M+ in gross profit off ~$5M revenue; net margin not disclosed in the FDD's Item 19 (this is common for newer/smaller-sample brands, and Alex flags it as worth directly questioning if a mature 50+ unit brand withholds it, since that can signal a small subset of underperforming locations dragging the average down).
- Royalty: 9% of gross profit, with a favorable ramp structure — zero royalty for the first 6 months, then a step-up to $2,500/month by year two. Alex calls a royalty ramp like this a strong positive signal of a thoughtful, aligned franchisor, since it avoids taking money from franchisees during their most financially vulnerable early months (comparing it to how Take 5 Oil Change/Driven Brands structures similar ramps).
- Maturity: this specific brand is brand new to the US — zero franchise locations open in its current FDD (Alex rates it a "zero or one" on the maturity scale); founder has industry experience in mobile fueling but not franchising experience specifically, though the favorable royalty structure suggests experienced franchise advisors are involved.
- Startup cost: $172,000-$490,000 (higher due to truck/equipment needs); ~$17,000-$150-200K in cash if financed; can start with one truck/one driver and scale from there; equipment/trucks required but no fixed facility needed (can be run from home logistically).

## General financing mechanics across all four concepts
- SBA loans can finance roughly 70-90% of startup cost (Alex says up to 90% is possible but 70-80% is more realistic); franchise brand must be on the SBA registry (most are, since franchisors want this financing option available to prospective buyers).
- SBA Express loans: up to $500,000 per loan, funded in under 60 days; can be used repeatedly (e.g., for additional locations) up to an aggregate $5,000,000 SBA limit across loans/businesses — described as functioning somewhat like a line of credit for serial franchise development.
- SBA application requires a Personal Financial Statement (similar to a mortgage application) — described as tedious but not especially difficult, and reusable across multiple lender/bank applications once compiled.
- Conventional bank loans are also available depending on personal financial standing/collateral, sometimes without a personal guarantee if the franchise system is well-established; banks favor franchise concepts generally because there's more comparable performance data across many existing units versus an unproven independent business.
- Progression path for building a larger portfolio without large personal capital: start with one or two units, prove yourself as a competent operator, then unlock access to private equity investment, better conventional financing terms, and franchisor trust to expand faster (concrete example cited: an individual who grew from 0 to ~120 locations across ~7 years, whose portfolio does $350M-$500M/year in revenue, retaining roughly 30-50% equity after raising outside capital once he'd proven himself operationally).
- People can also get into franchising with no personal capital by contributing operating expertise instead — bringing proven multi-unit/multi-location management experience (GM, district manager, etc.) in exchange for equity from capital partners who have money but lack bandwidth/operational skill to run multiple concepts themselves (concrete example: Franzy itself brought on an operating partner with no prior ownership experience but strong GM/district-manager experience, in exchange for meaningful equity, to help develop 10 planned "Pop-Up Bagels" locations).

## Franchise maturity and risk framework
- Suggested "safety" threshold: franchise systems with 50+ open units are generally past their reliance on franchise fees to fund early corporate growth and have started becoming profitable off ongoing royalties — meaning they're more selective about who they let in and have already worked out early operational/marketing mistakes. Below that threshold, brands may be more dependent on selling units to anyone qualified.
- Explained trade-off curve: early on, a franchisor provides a lot of value (training, systems, initial support) relative to what they collect in royalties (near zero, since revenue hasn't started); over time this reverses — the franchisee's paid-in royalties eventually outweigh the marginal ongoing value/support they receive, which is why franchisees years into a mature system sometimes resent royalty payments ("what have you done for me lately?").
- Counter-strategy for sophisticated buyers: seek out a mature, proven brand (high maturity score) that nonetheless still has a small number of unsold or available territories in a good market — combining lower risk (large sample size of proven performance data) with meaningful remaining upside; described as hard to find without industry connections/network (attending franchise conferences, building relationships with other operators) which is how experienced portfolio-builders learn about these opportunities before they're publicly listed. One example: a large operator sometimes needs to offload a subset of overextended territories privately to someone he/she knows, rather than through public "biz buy sell" listings.
- Explicit statistic: franchised businesses have roughly an 85% survival rate after 5 years, versus roughly 50% for independent businesses — but the ~15% of franchise failures generate disproportionate "horror story" attention (loss aversion bias: negative experiences are felt roughly 3x more strongly than equivalent gains, per a cited psychological finding), which Alex says is a major reason franchising has an undeservedly bad reputation despite statistically outperforming independent-business survival rates.
- Named red flags to check in an FDD:
  - **Item 20 (transfers/closures)**: an unusually high number of "transfers" (locations resold to another franchisee, or bought back by the franchisor itself) can be used to disguise what are effectively failures/closures without them showing up as outright "closures" in the filing — worth asking directly why transfer counts spiked in a given year, especially for a young system (under 5-10 years old). A real story is recounted: a struggling franchisee who admitted in writing (at the franchisor's request) that he might have to declare bankruptcy was then forced by the franchisor to transfer the unit back to them — specifically to avoid an official "closure" appearing on the FDD.
  - **Item 19 (financial performance representations)**: watch for footnotes that cherry-pick only top-performing locations (e.g., "this average reflects only our top 10 locations") rather than the full system, or a mature (50+ unit) brand that declines to disclose net profitability at all.
  - **Franchisor reluctance to connect you with a broad, unfiltered set of existing franchisees** (not just their handpicked best performers) — recommended diligence tactic: ask for a full list of franchisees and personally track down ones NOT on any "reference list" given to you, including ones who have failed or exited, similar to independently verifying a job candidate's references by asking who their actual managers were rather than accepting only supplied references.
- General regulatory critique: Alex argues franchise buyers currently have far fewer legal protections than, e.g., home buyers (where real estate agents must be licensed and disclose compensation) despite often risking hundreds of thousands of dollars in leasehold improvements with no asset backing if the concept fails.
- Corporate vs. franchise-owned location performance bias: early in a brand's life, corporate-owned locations tend to outperform franchised ones (because the company concentrates resources there to prove the model); later in a mature brand's life, corporate locations tend to underperform franchised ones (because franchisees have more personal skin-in-the-game motivation than a corporate hired manager) — meaning corporate-location performance data should be read differently depending on where a brand is in its life cycle.
- Fit matters more than raw profit potential: Alex repeatedly stresses that the "right" franchise depends entirely on the individual's personality, risk tolerance, sales aptitude, and goals — an outgoing, competitive person suits a relationship-selling concept like freight brokerage, while an introverted but disciplined operator might do better managing subcontractors in facility management; a mismatched personality/franchise fit leads to failure even in an objectively "good" franchise system. Franzy reportedly turns away prospective franchisees who seem too independently entrepreneurial/likely to deviate from a franchise system's playbook.

## Named example franchises/concepts and their assessed economics
- **Card My Yard** (yard-sign rental/greeting business, e.g., birthday/celebration lawn signs): cited as the cheapest concept discussed, ~$10,000 to start; can generate roughly $30,000/year — framed as a low-risk part-time/side-hustle option, suitable to run with a teenager/family member as an entrepreneurship learning experience.
- **Garage renovation franchise** (epoxy flooring, custom slatwall shelving): cited as Alex's pick if given $150,000 to deploy; average revenue over $1M, no heavy equipment needed, small crew (2-3 people); noted as recently used and publicly praised by Damon John (Shark Tank) on Instagram. (Note: Koerner separately mentions having interviewed a non-franchised epoxy-flooring operator who also does well — implying this niche works both inside and outside a franchise structure.)
- **Chick-fil-A**: average owner-operator take-home is about $650,000/year, but Alex argues it isn't really a true "franchise" in spirit — the company builds the building, owns the land, and takes 50% of profit as royalty; only ~$15,000 is needed to get started because the franchisor bears nearly all capital risk, but in exchange you're effectively "buying a job" (very high income ceiling and low failure risk, but capped upside — you'll never scale to run many units).
- **Crumbl Cookies**: assessed as likely a fad — declining same-store numbers cited as evidence, alongside recent menu-expansion moves (pies, dirty sodas) interpreted as a sign of a brand trying to offset a declining core product (an anecdote is given about a previously-launched Crumbl ice cream product that was reportedly poor quality and was pulled from the menu shortly after).
- **Senior home-accessibility infrastructure franchises** (installing wheelchair ramps, stairlifts, etc. for aging-in-place seniors): described as a "boring" but highly profitable niche; demand evidenced by informal market research calls to multiple cities (Philadelphia, Cincinnati) posing as a family seeking senior in-home care, consistently finding 6-12 month waitlists everywhere; one cited brand does $10M+/year in revenue with strong margins; framed as favorable because it's install-and-leave work with low ongoing labor/liability management and is frequently paid for by insurance rather than out-of-pocket by the customer (aligned incentives, no direct "payer resistance").
- **ABA/autism behavioral therapy clinics**: mentioned as a similarly structured "mission-driven + profitable + insurance-funded" business category; a specific verified private-equity deal is cited — four corporate-owned ABA clinic locations sold for $64 million, later growing into a company with 100+ locations valued near $1 billion (though such extreme multiples are no longer available in that space today, per Alex).
- **Subway**: called "absolutely" the worst franchise deal today by Alex, based on a secondhand account from a former Subway franchise business coach who described the brand's strategy of deliberately opening new locations within a block or two of existing ones to force them to "fight it out," closing the loser — with surviving owners sometimes taking home only $20,000-$30,000/year despite significant personal investment.
- **Non-franchise rollup analogy** (independent optometry practices): a personal anecdote about "my eye doctor" being part of a large rollup company started by one optometrist's son-in-law who offered to buy out his father-in-law's and colleagues' back-office/admin burden in exchange for ownership, eventually scaling into a multi-billion-dollar rollup — cited as evidence that the same "find an unbundled service pain point, get in early with a repeatable playbook" pattern applies whether or not the vehicle is a formal franchise. A related tactical detail: an eye doctor acquaintance reportedly gets a 60x return on ad spend using direct mail postcards, running 9 clinics primarily through that channel.
