## The business idea(s)
Gumloop itself isn't the side hustle — it's the tool. The actual business ideas discussed are services/agencies built ON TOP of Gumloop (or an equivalent AI-workflow builder):
1. **"Gumloop expert" / automation implementation freelancer**: learn the no-code drag-and-drop tool and get hired to build custom AI workflows for businesses. No engineering background required.
2. **Niche automation-as-a-service agency**: package a specific repeatable workflow (e.g., competitor ad analysis, lead research, document processing) as a recurring paid service, charging a flat monthly fee while the work is fully automated on the backend — client never knows it's automated.
3. **White-labeling Gumloop workflows**: build a customer-facing product/website (e.g., a simple web form) that triggers a Gumloop workflow via webhook on the backend — the end customer never sees or knows Gumloop is involved.
4. **Programmatic SEO / content-at-scale services**: use Gumloop to auto-generate large numbers of landing pages or blog posts (e.g., turning a YouTube video/lecture into a blog post, or generating city-specific landing pages from a spreadsheet of names/keywords) — sold as an SEO/content service.
5. **"10x engineer" identification/retention tool for large companies**: an idea Max floated for someone else to build — use an LLM-based code-review bot to continuously analyze a large company's engineering codebase/PRs and flag which engineers are having outsized impact, so management can retain/reward them (positioned as either a retention tool or, more cynically, a cost-cutting tool to identify low performers).

## Concrete numbers
- Example pitch economics quoted directly: "Pay me $300/month, I'll send you an email every Friday with your top 3 competitors' ad strategy, fully automated" — while the client believes you're spending ~4 hours/week doing manual research.
- Gumloop expert freelance rates: highly variable, "anywhere from $100 to $5,000" per project depending on scope; a $100 flow build can turn into an ongoing $1,000/month retainer if it evolves into full automation of a client's process.
- Real example: a Gumloop expert was hired full-time (internally at Gumloop) after impressing on customer calls — went from freelance automation builder to an in-house role purely by mastering the drag-and-drop tool.
- Gumloop's own customer example: Instacart has ~1,000 of its 3,000 employees actively using Gumloop, spread fairly evenly across ~10 departments; only ~18% of active users are engineers, meaning the tool empowers non-technical staff to build automations.
- Company growth (self-reported, no precise revenue given): "grew 50% two months ago, 30% last month."

## Process / how to actually monetize this
- Identify a workflow you deeply understand well enough to write out as a numbered list of steps — Max's rule of thumb: "if you can define something as a list of steps, it's ripe for automation"; workflows full of unpredictable conditionals/edge cases are poor automation candidates.
- Common patterns Gumloop customers automate (a starting menu of ideas to build a service around): web scraping, outbound sales prospecting/lead enrichment, CRM enrichment, document/PDF processing (e.g., extracting order number/amount/address from emailed PDFs into a spreadsheet), lead scoring/categorization (bucket into "high value / extremely high value / normal / low value" rather than a numeric score — LLMs are described as more reliable at discrete categories than continuous scores), general admin/data labeling, and content generation.
- Concrete example workflow demoed: when someone signs up for a product, auto-scrape their email/company, enrich with LinkedIn, funding stage, industry, one-line company description, and monthly web traffic, then post a summary to Slack and draft (not auto-send) a personalized outbound email into the founder's Gmail inbox — a sellable version of this is a "new-lead research & first-touch email" service for any B2B company.
- Concrete example workflow demoed for local/blue-collar businesses: a "which lead is worth calling back immediately" triage — e.g., a handyman/plumbing business's web form asks "homeowner or investor?"; investor leads (repeat/high-value customers) trigger an urgent push text with full background research on the person (LinkedIn, Twitter, company), while homeowner leads (low margin, low close rate — example given: ~20% close rate, ~$100 job) get routed into a nurture email sequence instead of a phone call.
- Concrete example: a real client (a medical billing company annotating skilled nursing facility records) had staff manually Ctrl+F searching thousands of pages of PDFs for billable diagnosis codes (e.g., "Alzheimer's," "diabetes") and missing revenue opportunities — an automated GPT-wrapper workflow was built to catch all instances, reportedly saving "thousands of dollars a month." General heuristic given: any repetitive manual "Ctrl+F" or inbox-triage task (e.g., manually deciding which of 800 daily emails to forward to support based on sentiment/keywords) is a strong automation candidate.
- Concrete sellable idea (Facebook/Meta ads competitor-analysis-as-a-service): input a competitor's Facebook Page link, the workflow pulls all their currently running ads across Instagram/WhatsApp/Facebook, runs each ad through a vision-capable model (e.g. Gemini) to describe strategy/creative, stitches all ad descriptions into one prompt fed to a stronger reasoning model (e.g., OpenAI's o1) to produce a formatted weekly report (e.g., ad-type breakdown: X% display, Y% Stories, etc.) — scheduled to auto-run and email every Friday. Extension: cross-reference the UK Meta Ad Library (which legally must disclose ad spend, unlike the US version) to find how much competitors are actually spending.
- Concrete example (programmatic SEO): feed a spreadsheet of hundreds/thousands of city names + a few keyword templates into a workflow that auto-writes unique landing page copy per row (e.g., a real production example: a "pet cremation leads" site with 5,500 landing pages across the top 1,100 US/Canada cities by population, built on Webflow) — described as work that would traditionally be sold as an expensive "programmatic SEO as a service."
- To pitch this to a local business owner cold: look for signals of manual repetitive work (staff transcribing PDFs, manually triaging leads/emails) and quote a fast automation build (the PDF-extraction example was described as settable up "in like 30 seconds" once you know the pattern).
- Distribution/finding clients as a "Gumloop expert": Gumloop maintains an informal Airtable-based "expert directory" (as of this recording, only ~10 people) that Gumloop itself refers inbound client requests to — stated to currently have more client demand than available experts, though lead quality varies (an expert getting 4 leads/day might find only 1 qualified).

## Model-selection guidance (from the founder, useful for anyone building these workflows)
- Use the most powerful/expensive reasoning model (e.g., GPT-4o, o1, Claude 3.7) for judgment-heavy or long-form synthesis steps where quality really matters and cost is not the constraint.
- Use cheap/fast models (e.g., GPT-4o Mini, Gemini Flash) for high-volume, simple/obvious classification tasks (e.g., labeling tens of thousands of produce items) where the task is easy and volume is the challenge.
- No universal "best" model — described as largely a stylistic/preference choice (Claude noted as subjectively stronger for creative writing tasks).

## Caveats / risks
- Fully autonomous, no-human-review "agent" workflows are explicitly discouraged by the founder for anything with real stakes or nuance — even Gumloop's own founder keeps a human-in-the-loop for personalized outbound emails (drafts only, reviewed before sending), reserving full autopilot for low-nuance templated messages.
- A workflow/AI automation is only as good as the operator's own understanding of the underlying process — "if you ask an agent to just write a sales email with no context, it's going to write slop"; success requires encoding real domain expertise into the workflow (treat AI like a new employee that needs explicit training/context, not a mind reader).
- Templates are explicitly called a "band-aid" — Max's view is that needing a template means you couldn't yet go from idea to working workflow yourself; useful for inspiration but not a substitute for understanding the tool.
- A proposed "record everything an employee does and have AI infer automations from the screen recording" feature was explicitly walked back by the founder as likely to underdeliver and burn user trust, since much real work/reasoning isn't visible on-screen — a general caution against over-promising "fully autonomous" AI products.
- No official Gumloop workflow marketplace exists yet (as of recording) specifically because of credential/security risk in sharing workflows that may contain sensitive API keys — anyone building a workflow-resale business should be mindful of this same risk.
