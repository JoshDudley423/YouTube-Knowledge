# He Makes $3M/Year From Tiny AI Tools (Here's How You Can Too)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SiRlIMtZygI | Published: 2025-10-05

Interview with John Rush (X: @johnrushx), a solo/bootstrapped indie builder who runs 24 small AI-powered web products/tools generating a combined ~$3M/year in recurring revenue, with no employees. Deep dive into his idea-generation, validation, building, and growth process using AI/no-code tools.

## Business model and numbers
- 24 total projects/products, combined revenue ~$3 million/year; the "80/20 rule" applies -- 3 products generate most of the revenue, the rest contribute smaller amounts (some tools make as little as ~$1,000/month, a few make ~$10,000/month).
- Flagship product: "SEO Bot" (an autonomous SEO agent), doing $1M ARR at time of interview, previously doubling month-over-month, dipped to a plateau of ~$110k/month over one summer.
- Approach: runs every product as a solo operator, deliberately avoiding hiring so he's forced to personally learn accounting, design, development, growth, and marketing -- uses that friction to identify the ~20% of tasks that drive ~80% of results, then automates those specifically with AI agents.
- Process for each product: build and run it purely for himself first (sometimes for 1-3 years) until stable, then open it to friends who are also founders, and only then launch publicly -- filtering out ideas that don't survive that internal-use phase.
- Cross-selling is a major growth channel: roughly half of new signups across all his products come from users discovering one tool and moving to another; users rarely fully leave "his universe" once they enter one product.
- Directories (simple niche listing sites) are a recurring product type -- most earn only ~$1,000/month individually but are valued for driving otherwise-unreachable traffic.

## Idea generation framework
- Primary source (99% of his ideas): "scratch your own itch" -- solve a real problem encountered doing actual work himself.
- Secondary method: search demand signals directly via Google autocomplete. Example demoed: typing "website builder for" into Google search reveals autocomplete suggestions ranked roughly by search volume (small business, free, photographers, portfolio, artist, nonprofits, etc.) -- each represents an underserved niche.
- Validation of a keyword-derived idea: feed the candidate keyword into a keyword-research tool (unnamed in transcript, functions like Ahrefs/SEMrush-style tools) to check monthly search volume and competition; example cited: "website builder for artists" and related phrasings totaled roughly 100k combined monthly searches. Another example: "tux heaven" showed ~100k searches with low competition (low ad competition = valuable/underexploited keyword).
- Suggested workflow to systematize this: generate a list of 50-60 keyword variations, run each through the research tool, then use an AI deep-research tool (he names Perplexity) to build a comparison table of existing niche competitors for a given keyword (excluding large generalist players like Wix/Squarespace) and their estimated traffic (via a tool like SimilarWeb), to find categories with high demand but few competent niche competitors ("high demand, low supply").
- Framing/rationale: VC-backed startups all chase the same large markets because they need to prove billion-dollar scale; this leaves many smaller, real, profitable niches ("million-dollar businesses," not billion-dollar ones) completely unaddressed and available to solo builders/indie hackers.

## Validation process (before building)
- Mindset: actively try to "kill" your own idea rather than confirm it, because founders in love with an idea will rationalize away bad signals.
- Step 1 -- fake landing page / waitlist test: build a minimal landing page (using a website builder like Framer, Unicorn, or Webflow -- explicitly NOT app-building tools like Lovable/Bolt/Cursor, which he reserves for actual product builds) offering just an email-signup waitlist, no live product.
- Step 2 -- distribution test for the waitlist: post to relevant social media, reply to related conversations on Reddit/X even without an existing following (search for people in the target niche discussing adjacent topics, reply, then DM/email them), or run small paid ads (historically Google/Meta) to see if cold traffic converts to signups.
- Interpreting results: getting either zero signups or unusually strong signups after ~100 outreach actions are both useful "good" outcomes because they're clear signals; getting a lukewarm handful is the ambiguous, less useful result. Cited real example: publicly tweeting a plan to build "Dev Hunt" (a Product-Hunt-style launch platform specifically for developers) conditional on 100 signups -- hit 100 signups, with people actively recruiting friends to help reach the threshold because they wanted the product to exist.
- Step 3 -- pre-booking / payment test (final validation gate): offer the waitlist a steep discount (70-90% off) to pre-pay before the product is built. If fewer than ~10% of the waitlist actually pays, that's a strong negative signal even if the waitlist itself looked large -- indicates you may have attracted an audience unwilling/unable to pay (e.g., students) or priced/positioned wrong, and this problem is described as very hard to fix after the fact (the underlying keyword traffic may fundamentally skew toward non-paying audiences).
- Pricing-model signal: when pre-booking customers push back on payment structure (e.g., "I'd pay once but not monthly" for a directory-listing tool, or conversely "I'd rather pay $9 upfront than $100 lifetime" for an analytics tool), that direct feedback should reshape the actual pricing model before launch.
- SEO as an early validation signal: because Google indexing now shows initial traffic within weeks (down from ~2-3 months a few years ago), he starts SEO content work on day one for every idea, since products with zero organic traffic after a few months signal a keyword arena that's uncompetable, versus one where you unexpectedly win easily.
- Timing note: waitlist conversion doesn't meaningfully decay if you delay launch -- if a cohort of waitlist signups goes cold, you can simply repeat the same acquisition process again later; the exception is highly seasonal/timely product ideas.

## Tech stack
- Prototyping/MVP: Lovable (his stated favorite), also uses Bolt and Cursor for rapid vibe-coded prototypes and UI experimentation.
- Production/serious builds: a proprietary internal AI coding tool he calls "Morse X," used once a prototype graduates to a real product.
- Automation/integration: uses Make, Zapier, and Lindy AI (all three, for different automation needs).
- Landing pages/marketing sites: purpose-built website builders (Framer, Unicorn, Webflow) rather than app-building tools, because they're optimized for SEO and simple static pages.

## Growth tactics (in priority order, "80% of growth" from these five)
1. **Social media** -- described as the highest-leverage channel; going viral or building a following causes secondary effects (bloggers, newsletter writers, and directory curators start citing/including your product for free), and social growth compounds into SEO backlinks as others link to you.
2. **SEO / content** -- write or generate articles targeting validated low-competition keywords; can be outsourced to an agency if funded, but he estimates ~1 hour/day for a month can produce decent results without outsourcing.
3. **Affiliate/partner programs** -- set up via a portal tool (referenced generically, e.g., "first promoter"-style services) giving partners unique referral links/codes; recommends unusually generous commissions (as high as 50% if the product has no significant marginal cost, or 25-30% if it does) to actually motivate active promotion.
4. **Ubiquitous placement** -- get the product listed everywhere plausible: directories, launch platforms (e.g., Product Hunt-style sites), forums, Reddit, Quora, and relevant social conversations. Rationale beyond direct traffic: LLMs crawl these listings/comments/articles and incorporate them into training data, so being present across many such sources can generate substantial "free" traffic later when people ask AI assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, Grok) for product recommendations -- he reports some products saw a simultaneous traffic surge across multiple AI chat platforms in the same week, suggesting shared underlying training data sourcing.
5. **Free lead-magnet tools** -- build small free utility tools adjacent to a paid product (example: a free "album cover generator" tool that captures an email to deliver the image, with a soft pitch for the paid product in the follow-up email) to warm up cold traffic before pitching the paid offering; conversion is higher here because the user already had a positive experience with the free tool first.
- On directories specifically in the AI era: he believes curated directories built by people/entities with existing influence/trust will become more valuable, not less, because AI systems (like Google's old backlink-based ranking) need a trust signal to rank/select from an increasingly AI-generated flood of content, and are already leaning on upvote-style trust signals (e.g., Reddit) -- directories serve a similar filtering function for both humans and AI systems.
- Suggested tactic: DM/partner with people who already have topical influence (even a small, niche-specific audience of a few thousand people, not necessarily a "big" influencer) to co-build a directory or small tool under their name/brand, since a branded/reputable source stands out once AI makes it trivial for anyone to clone the same generic tool.

## Agent-orchestration concept
- His agents are designed to interoperate: e.g., "SEO Bot" can call another of his tools, "Rapidfy," a vibe-coding tool, to have it build a small supporting tool and send it back automatically -- analogous to one employee (a marketer) asking another (a developer) for help internally.
- Design philosophy: agents should start minimal (literally a single on/off toggle in the earliest version of SEO Bot) and only gain user-facing controls in response to specific customer requests for more control, because he believes most non-power-users don't actually want granular control, they want autonomous execution with periodic checking in (frequent involvement early, tapering to quarterly check-ins as the agent "learns" the user).
