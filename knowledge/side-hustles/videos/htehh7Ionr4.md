# How to Turn Your Idea Into a Profitable Amazon Product
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=htehh7Ionr4 | Published: 2024-06-18

Q&A format episode: Chris Koerner takes live calls/submitted questions from listeners about specific business ideas.

## Call 1: Magnetic glasses-case organizer (Amazon physical product)
- Caller's idea: a magnetic, stackable case system to organize multiple pairs of glasses (computer, sunglasses, prescription) that stick together via thin internal magnetic panels, plus a small travel container.
- Validation done by caller: informal survey of ~10 friends/family who wear glasses; most said they'd buy it, one said no.
- Koerner's assessment — reasons to like it: cheap to prototype (can literally assemble from cardboard/magnets at home, no injection molds needed), cheap to manufacture, addresses a real subset of the population (roughly 20-30% wear glasses), lightweight/under 13 oz so it ships via cheaper USPS First Class rather than Priority, and — most importantly — it's highly "short-form-video-able": the value proposition can be shown in a 5-second clip, which he says outweighs weak existing Amazon search demand for this specific product.
- Reasons for caution: low existing Amazon search volume for a novel product category (can't rely on keyword-match traffic); the product is easily copied once it goes viral (first-mover can hold a lead but competitors will chip away at market share); price point is too low (roughly $20 or less) to profitably run paid ads, which typically need at least a ~$50 product to pencil out.
- Naming/branding strategy recommended: invent a distinctive, brandable name (e.g., "Rhino Case," something with a color or shape) rather than a generic descriptive name — the goal is to create net-new search demand across TikTok, YouTube, Google, and Amazon simultaneously, since a made-up/ownable term has no autocomplete competition. Buy the matching bare .com domain (not "name+case.com").
- Direct comparable case study cited: Kevin Espiritu (Epic Gardening) sells a garden kneeling brace that reportedly can't be kept in stock, purely because a 3-5 second video instantly and viscerally shows the value proposition.
- Suggested product tiering: offer a cheap plastic version and a premium leather/pleather version — expectation that some buyers upgrade after trying the cheap version.
- Recommended launch sequence: (1) hand-make ~100 units at home, sell to friends/family/Facebook groups, gather feedback and iterate design (e.g., rounded corners); (2) do informal market research in relevant Facebook groups/Reddit to see what features people want; (3) calculate true unit economics (example walked through: $4 material+labor cost at conservative $20/hr wage assumption and ~5 units/hour output → ~75% gross margin at a $20 retail price, dropping to ~50% after shipping); (4) list on Amazon and see if it captures organic traffic; (5) get the demo video into the hands of someone who can make it go viral (DM creators, or run it as a paid ad on Instagram Reels — normally not recommended for organic-style content, but justified here since it's a paid product with a strong visual hook); low CPM on such ads signals the platform is amplifying it organically because of engagement.
- Manufacturing recommendation: order raw/component materials from China but assemble domestically rather than have finished units assembled and shipped from China — assembly + shipping from China is estimated at "literally three times more expensive" due to dimensional/volumetric shipping costs on bulkier assembled goods, and keeping assembly in-house avoids the risk of an entire first production run (e.g., 10,000 units) being defective, which could hand the market to a fast-following competitor.

## Call 2: Finding/validating physical product ideas to sell (niche product sourcing)
- Caller's goal: find popular existing physical products with some deficiency, then build an improved version (as opposed to inventing something wholly new) — coming from a background of doing this successfully for mobile apps (using SearchAds/Sensor Tower for app-store demand data) but not knowing the physical-goods equivalent.
- Recommended tools/methods:
  - Jungle Scout — Amazon-specific tool showing units/revenue sold per product per month (directionally, not perfectly, accurate) and how many competing sellers exist for a given product (useful for gauging both demand and competition/"red ocean" risk).
  - Alibaba, AliExpress, Temu — browse categories and note which products are ranked/pushed most prominently as a signal of current demand trends.
  - A Shopify "best-selling products" URL suffix trick: find a batch of Shopify stores (e.g., via BuiltWith.com), put their URLs in a spreadsheet, use a CONCATENATE formula to append the same URL suffix to every store URL, producing a link to each store's products sorted best-to-worst-selling — can be done manually or delegated to a VA at scale.
  - Gummy Search — a Reddit-focused research tool showing which topics/products are discussed most within any given subreddit; useful for finding under-served demand signals not easily visible via paid-ad platforms.
- Reddit-based organic marketing tactic explained in detail: write a value-first, storytelling post (no promotional link) in a relevant subreddit, styled after whatever post format has already gone viral in that specific subreddit (check "top posts of all time/month" as templates — some subreddits reward humility, others reward bragging, tailor accordingly); only add a product link later by editing the post once it's visibly accelerating (Reddit shows real-time view counts — e.g., climbing from 100/hr to 1,000/hr to 5,000/hr) — adding a link from the outset kills a post's chance of taking off because it reads as overtly promotional. Comments asking "what's your product?" can then be answered directly with the link too. Must be done from the same account each time (buying pre-aged Reddit accounts is possible but risks bans with repeated overtly-promotional use).

## Call 3: Amazon FBA / retail arbitrage as a side income (general caution against it)
- Direct answer: Koerner does NOT recommend Amazon FBA or retail arbitrage as a side-income strategy for most people, distinct from the earlier caller who is building/owning a genuinely unique physical product.
- Reasoning: the market is extremely crowded with people who've seen "make $X/month on FBA" content; because e-commerce/FBA is difficult and not actually passive, many practitioners who found modest success (e.g., ~$2,000/month profit) pivot to selling courses teaching others FBA rather than continuing to compete themselves — meaning there is now more money in selling FBA education than in doing FBA.
- Heuristic offered for evaluating any "make money" content/course: prefer advice from creators who are not financially incentivized to sell you the underlying business opportunity (e.g., Koerner says he tweets home-service business ideas like gutter cleaning/pressure washing without selling a related course) over creators actively selling courses/mentorship in that exact niche.
- General principle stated: "there is a direct correlation between how ugly a business is and how much profit you can make from it," and inversely, "sexy and passive-looking" business opportunities correlate with higher risk of losing money, because such opportunities are marketed with an easy/passive-income hook specifically to sell courses, with no incentive on the seller's part for the buyer's actual outcome.

## Call 4: Scaling a virtual math tutoring business via Facebook groups (college student)
- Business: virtual, solo-operated tutoring (algebra, geometry, pre-calculus, K-12 math), acquiring customers primarily by posting in local/regional Facebook parent and school-district groups since November of that year.
- Concrete performance data: ~8 introductory calls booked → 7 converted to at least one paid session (a ~90% close rate on booked calls); of those 7, about 4 became recurring customers for roughly 2 months each.
- Pricing at time of call: $24.99 per 30-minute session, 1-2 sessions/week — yielding roughly $200-250/month per recurring customer (host recalculates as ~$25-50/session cadence).
- Flat pricing regardless of subject/grade level (same price for high school and middle school) — flagged by Koerner as a missed opportunity to price-differentiate by grade/subject demand.
- Facebook outreach pitch used verbatim: "Hello parents, I'm a first-year student at [University] and I'm a virtual tutor. If your child could use some help in algebra, geometry, or any K-12 math concepts, feel free to send me a message. I have availability every day of the week and I'd love to help." Sometimes localized with "Hello parents of [town]."
- Koerner's specific recommendations:
  - Raise prices significantly — a ~90% close rate on sales calls is a strong signal of being underpriced relative to what the market would bear.
  - Change the call-to-action from "DM me" to "comment below," because Facebook Group algorithms show a post to only a small initial fraction of members (cited: as low as 5%) and expand distribution based on engagement signals like comments, not just DMs.
  - Add urgency/scarcity language (e.g., "I just had a cancellation, only a couple slots left") rather than "I have availability every day," which can unintentionally signal low demand/lower perceived quality.
  - Use marketplaces beyond organic Facebook posting: (a) niche tutoring marketplaces that sell tutor leads for a flat fee (caller was already using one called "Tutors.com" at roughly a $20/lead cost); (b) Upwork — pay Upwork a one-time acquisition fee (cited ~$5) to land a client, then move the relationship off-platform to avoid recurring platform fees; Koerner notes Upwork clients may self-select toward higher-paying, "more white-collar" customers.
  - Competitive-intelligence tactic: check how competing tutoring platforms order their subject dropdown menus (the first-listed items are likely the most in-demand, e.g., algebra); search large, non-local parent Facebook groups (hundreds of thousands of members) for keyword frequency of subjects (math, language arts, science, etc.) and read comments for recurring complaints/pain points, then use those specific pain points and highest-demand subjects when posting in smaller, local groups.
  - Sequencing advice: don't hire help/delegate tutoring until fully booked and "drowning in work" — only then will you be forced to learn the actual scaling mechanisms needed to hand off delivery.

## Call 5: What AI-related business to start with limited capital
- Idea 1 — "personal inbox LLM" service: build a business helping individuals/business owners turn their own email inbox history into a queryable knowledge base/FAQ repository (not a generic chatbot) that surfaces past answers to repeated questions. Personal example given: Koerner's RV-park investor-relations inbox gets the same 15-20 recurring investor questions (e.g., how bonus depreciation works, minimum investment size); he wants a tool that exports/segments his own investor-related emails and lets him instantly retrieve a past answer rather than rewriting it. He notes this is currently a manual, time-consuming DIY process for him. Rationale for the opportunity: large AI companies (Google, OpenAI) are currently reluctant to integrate deeply with personal email inboxes due to privacy concerns, leaving a gap for smaller/independent operators willing to build "hacky" workarounds — he frames emerging categories where people are improvising creative technical workarounds as generally strong opportunity signals.
- Idea 2 — automation agency (AI-optional): a retainer-based service (cited pricing: $2,000-$3,000/month ongoing, sometimes $4,000 for the first month) building and maintaining business-process automations (Zapier/Slack integrations, etc.) for small-to-medium businesses that can't justify a full-time $6,000-$10,000+/month automation hire. Concrete example from his own business (Texas Snacks): an automated email sent to customers the day their package arrives, including a personalized, non-AI-sounding detail about the recipient's city — intentionally designed to not look automated/AI-generated, which he credits with improving repeat-order rate by making customers feel like they have a one-on-one relationship with the brand rather than receiving generic polished marketing email.
