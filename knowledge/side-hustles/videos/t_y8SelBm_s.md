# The Simplest $7M Business You've Never Heard Of
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t_y8SelBm_s | Published: 2026-03-17

Interview with Michael Clark, co-founder/CEO of **The Flower Letters** — a subscription business that mails fictional stories to subscribers via physical letters, two per month, over the course of a year. Related earlier episode referenced: an interview with "Hannah" of a different snail-mail letter business (organic/TikTok-driven growth).

## The business model
- Product: subscribers get **2 letters/month for 12 months** telling a serialized story (mostly historical fiction, one fantasy series); each letter/envelope contains multiple physical items — the letter itself (often multiple pages), postcards (12 per story, roughly one per month), plus extras like a themed "advertisement card" from the story's era, a designed bookmark, a "telegram" story element, stickers, etc. Contents intentionally vary letter-to-letter rather than following a fixed formula, which the founder credits as a differentiator (customers frequently reach out unprompted just to comment on how much was included).
- Origin story: co-founder Hannah was an artist selling paintings on Instagram (selling out within ~20-30 minutes of posting, prices from a couple hundred dollars up to ~$1,000 for large pieces) and wanted a way to grow her email list; the idea of sending a physical letter instead of an email as a subscription incentive evolved into a full serialized-story-by-mail subscription, partly inspired by an existing similar business they personally subscribed to ("Letters From Afar," a simpler standalone-letter-per-month model, mentioned as an explicit influence/comp).
- Launched with a homemade first Instagram account (no paid ads, no existing following) — got 33 signups from that initial organic post. Explicit advice given: don't validate a new product by asking friends/family, since they'll give a "false positive" (they'll buy out of support, not real demand) — you have to find real, cold customers.

## Pricing and unit economics
- Initial and current price: **$12/month** (2 letters/month), cost of goods per letter/mailing originally estimated at $2-3 (paper, stamp, envelope, plus "extras" like nice stationery/stickers) at their current volume.
- Prepaid annual option is the primary growth driver: currently $100/year (~$8.33/month, a discount vs. $12/month monthly), a $10 add-on storage tin has also been very well received, and the tin itself is separately sold for $30.
- They deliberately keep pricing low/accessible relative to comparable subscriptions, prioritizing reach/impact over margin per customer.
- Customer acquisition cost (CAC) trajectory: started at **$15-20/customer** via early Facebook/Instagram ads; has since risen to **$40-50/customer** as the ad channel has matured/saturated (explicitly noted CAC "can't stay at $15-20" for any e-commerce brand at scale).
- Customer lifetime value: approximately **$180 over the customer's engagement** (not per year — many customers buy multiple stories/gifts over time, e.g., gifting to a mother and sister then eventually buying for themselves, sometimes accumulating $300+ in spend; one customer bought 10 subscriptions for friends).
- Annual churn: approximately **5%** — described as very low, attributed to (a) it being a serialized story people want to see through to completion, and (b) the product being highly "giftable," meaning many subscribers can't unilaterally cancel since someone else bought it for them (founder notes recipients who dislike a story sometimes have to have the original gift-giver call in to cancel on their behalf).
- Rough cost structure breakdown estimated in the interview: roughly one-third of revenue to cost of goods sold (postage, paper, supplies), roughly one-third to Meta ad spend, remainder to payroll/operations, with the business reinvesting essentially all profit into growth rather than extracting cash (deliberately structured as a "generational" business, not a lifestyle business) — net profit is kept intentionally low both for reinvestment and tax reasons.

## Growth trajectory and scale
- Within the first ~4 months (through end of 2020, launched August 2020), reached **~3,000 customers and ~$36,000/month recurring revenue**, run by just the two co-founders.
- 2024 revenue: crossed **$5 million**. 2025 revenue: crossed **$7 million**. Current volume: **~120,000 letters mailed per month**, ~3.5 million letters mailed cumulatively since launch.
- 2025 Meta ad spend: **$2.6 million**.
- Operations: 16-person team (peaked at 25) running a warehouse in Orem, Utah, doing 100% manual/hand-assembly of every letter (no packing automation) — an operations-efficiency improvement took them from 15 people stuffing 20,000 letters/month to 5 people handling 120,000/month under the same manual process, credited to a strong operations manager's process design rather than automation/AI.
- Explicit acknowledgment that AI is not used for the creative product itself (story writing) — used instead for business intelligence tasks: analyzing email threads, running efficiency analysis on Facebook ad data, financial analysis, and even generating a webpage in minutes — not for envelope-stuffing or letter-writing, which the founder treats as intentionally, permanently human/artisanal.

## Customer demographics and acquisition channel notes
- Core demographic: **female, 35+, peaking around 45-55**. Marketing specifically to men has not worked — the founder notes men don't engage with the hook/concept within the first ~30 seconds of an ad, so campaigns are targeted almost entirely at women.
- Primary buyer relationship pattern: **daughters buying for mothers** most commonly, more than any other relationship (including husbands buying for wives).
- Seasonality: **Mother's Day is their single biggest sales period ("our Super Bowl"), bigger than Christmas**; Christmas is comparatively more operationally stressful due to staffing challenges (employees wanting time off) despite similar sales volume.
- Email list building was treated as a first-class acquisition/retention channel from day one specifically as a hedge against platform risk (losing access to Meta/Facebook) — an on-site funnel offers a discount in exchange for an email address; email-driven revenue reportedly accounts for **38-50% of total revenue** depending on the period.
- Because subscriptions are sold per-individual-story (not an auto-renewing generic subscription), the company doesn't process annual/prepaid subscriptions through standard subscription billing software (avoiding the ~1% subscription-platform fee on prepaid annual payments) — they run monthly billing through subscription software but handle prepaid separately themselves.
- Growth channel is almost entirely **paid (Meta/Facebook and Instagram ads)**, not organic — explicitly contrasted with the referenced "Hannah" business, which grew organically via TikTok content. The founder is candid that they haven't cracked organic growth (they're a physical product/story business, not a personality-driven brand where "the founder is the product"), and that meaningfully investing in organic content would essentially require the creative co-founder to also become a full-time content creator — an unresolved tradeoff they haven't pursued.

## Retail and channel expansion
- Started expanding beyond direct-to-consumer: partnered with the gift shop at the Newport Mansions (Rhode Island) to sell a custom Gilded Age-themed story exclusive to their shop.
- Building toward big-box retail distribution (example floated: Costco) using a gift-card-style redemption model — built a redemption website where an in-store purchase includes a QR code the buyer scans, enters their address, and the subscription ships to them, similar to how physical gift cards work at retail.
- Exploring format expansion beyond print: an internal podcast ("Stories Told Through You," featuring real subscriber life stories) already exists; audio narration of their stories and even a TV adaptation are floated as future directions but not yet built.

## Advice for someone starting a similar snail-mail subscription business
1. **Know your specific customer** — the founder explicitly rejects "anyone with a mom" as a viable target despite Mother's Day universality; success requires narrowly identifying who the message actually resonates with (in their case, women 35+, not a gender-neutral audience).
2. **Understand what you're signing up for based on format complexity**: a serialized story (must arrive in order, must be internally consistent, requires real narrative writing skill) is dramatically harder to execute than a standalone monthly item (e.g., a single self-contained letter or recipe each month, cited as "way simpler" — pen-pal-style or single-issue formats were named as easier entry points than a full story).
3. **Pair a creative person with a business-operations person** (in their case, spouses) — the founder argues creative founders often struggle commercially because they just want to create, while business-minded founders can lack the creative "magic" that makes the product resonate; combining both is presented as close to a prerequisite for this category, generalized as advice for any creative-product business, not just snail mail.
4. **If you can't identify your target customer, start with a customer of one — yourself.** Cited example: the first story ("Audrey Rose") was written by Hannah purely for her own taste/preferences, not for a defined market, and turned out to resonate broadly — framed as "we like people who are like ourselves," a real driver of brand loyalty.
5. **Validate low-lift ideas by asking whether you yourself would use the feature** before building it — example given: the founder talked his own wife out of adding cooking-demonstration videos (via QR code) to her own new snail-mail recipe subscription business, because she admitted she personally never watches cooking videos while cooking — reasoning that if the creator wouldn't use their own planned feature, customers likely won't either, so either skip it or delay testing it for a couple months.
6. **Cheap, fast way to test market demand for any new product idea**: build a simple "coming soon" landing page describing the product/service, collect email signups, and run a small amount of ad spend against it — if you can get a meaningful pool of people willing to hand over their email (a real "transaction," even if non-monetary), that's a strong leading indicator of a viable market before investing further.
7. **Markets are larger than they feel, and failure to sell doesn't mean no market exists** — the founder pushes back on the instinct to give up after one flopped launch, framing market discovery as an iterative search process, not a single go/no-go test.
8. Once a niche is proven, market fit still comes down to real e-commerce marketing skill: the founder specifically calls out learning **conversion rate optimization (CRO)** as essential — small changes (button color, button placement, checkout flow bugs specific to certain devices/browsers) compound meaningfully at scale (moving from 3% to 3.2% conversion is materially significant at high traffic volumes).

## Other niche/format ideas discussed but not built by this company
- A cookbook/recipe-club letter subscription (referenced as something the podcast host's own wife was independently launching, inspired by this same interview format, called "Snail Mail Supper Club") — recipes framed around a monthly theme (a country or holiday), noted to have strong referral/group dynamics since it's naturally shared among friend groups.
- A comparable existing business named "Peachy Recipe" that lets customers upload their own family recipes and receive a printed family cookbook.
- Personalized "Santa letters" around Christmas — acknowledged as an existing, working niche run by other companies, though outside The Flower Letters' own brand positioning.
- Pen-pal-style standalone subscriptions (simpler format, cited as an easier category than full serialized fiction).
- A true-crime "solve a mystery" letter/puzzle box format, explicitly modeled on the existing $150 million/year business **Hunt A Killer** (a true-crime subscription box company) — floated as a proof that the "serialized mystery via mail" concept has a large addressable market and could be adapted into pure letter form (puzzles/codes to decode).

## Caveats
- Nearly all growth has come from paid acquisition (Meta ads), not organic content — this is explicitly not a low-cost-marketing playbook; expect meaningful ongoing ad spend and rising CAC as a channel matures.
- The founder states this business was deliberately configured as a long-term/generational venture with reinvestment prioritized over near-term profit extraction — the $7M revenue figure does not translate to $7M (or anywhere close) in owner take-home profit; net profit is being intentionally kept low for growth and tax reasons.
- A serialized-story format is explicitly described as significantly harder to execute well than a simpler standalone monthly mailing — the founder recommends beginners consider simpler formats first.
