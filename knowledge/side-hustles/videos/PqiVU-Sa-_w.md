# How Rolling Suds Built A Multi-Million Dollar Empire (Pressure Washing)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PqiVU-Sa-_w | Published: 2024-05-15

Interview with Aaron Harper, CEO/founder of Rolling Suds, a pressure-washing franchise (residential + commercial), covering both the franchisor economics and the unit-level (franchisee) economics. Longtime franchise-industry operator (drywall-repair franchise before this).

## The core business idea (for a franchisee/buyer)
- Rolling Suds is majority a **commercial**, not residential, power-washing business (60-70% commercial by revenue in most markets from month one; the founding location moved from 32% to 56% commercial over 5 years). Commercial jobs: parking garages (~$20,000 jobs), courthouses (~$4,000 jobs), shopping centers (six-figure annual contracts with ~3% renewal escalations on 2-year terms).
- Differentiated equipment/process: proprietary manufactured equipment (not available for purchase elsewhere) lets a crew power-wash a 3,000 sq ft house from setup to teardown in 20-25 minutes for a $400-500 job — creates a real cost/speed moat vs. competitors using off-the-shelf ~$500 Home Depot pressure washers.
- Market structure insight that made this attractive to the founder: unlike most home-service categories (HVAC, plumbing, solar — all crowded with 5-10 national franchise brands), power washing had no national branded leader at the time (compared to 1-800-GOT-JUNK's early dominance of junk removal before College Hunks/Junk King/Junk Luggers followed) — a "first professionalized mover" opportunity.

## Numbers — franchisee (unit) level
- Startup capital needed: roughly $300,000 total working capital for 2 territories (minimum purchase), covering: ~$35,000 truck down payment/equipment/wrap/shipping, $549,000 for territory fees at minimum 2 territories (~$274,500/territory — note: transcript states "territory is 549" for the minimum two combined, i.e., roughly $274K each, plus incremental $549K quoted elsewhere for additional single territories — treat exact per-territory figure as approximate), plus additional working capital/startup expenses.
- Recommended minimum net worth to buy in: ~$900,000 (guideline: never invest more than ~1/3 of net worth into one business).
- Territory size: ~250,000 population per territory (larger than typical home-services franchise norms of ~200,000 or per-household models) — sized deliberately so multi-territory buyers can add many trucks within their footprint rather than immediately butting against a neighboring franchisee's territory.
- Trucks cost ~$150,000 each (financed separately from the territory fee).
- Revenue per truck: average gross revenue per truck was $373,000 in the prior year, rising to $422,000 most recently, attributed to efficiency gains and heavier-than-expected commercial mix. Target/average day rate: ~$2,000/truck/day, ~20 operating days/month ≈ $40,000/truck/month at full utilization (~$80,000/month across 2 trucks).
- Profitability: at multi-truck scale (economies of scale kicking in), EBITDA margins run 30-40% per the most recent Franchise Disclosure Document (based on the founding Northeast location's 4 full-time trucks operating 205 days/year). A single non-optimized job example: a $400-500 house wash in 20-25 minutes has extremely high margin given minimal labor/material cost.
- Typical development path: franchisee buys minimum 2 territories, opens with 1 truck, orders a 2nd truck ~4 months in (arrives ~6 months in) — the $300K working-capital figure is specifically sized to fund that 2nd truck's down payment without needing new capital raised.
- Fees: 8% ongoing royalty on gross revenue, 2% brand fund (national marketing — not yet meaningfully deployed since systemwide sales haven't hit the $100-200M threshold), and a required minimum local ad spend of $2,500/truck/month (target customer acquisition cost ~$15-20).
- Franchisor does NOT offer SBA financing currently — cost of capital (~11% at time of interview, ~$7K/month debt service on an SBA loan for a business this size) makes early-stage debt service difficult while reinvesting in growth; buyers are expected to bring cash/personal capital.
- Minimum buy-in is 2 territories (not single-unit) by design — Rolling Suds explicitly avoids "side hustle" single-territory buyers, targeting people who want to build a multi-truck, multi-million-dollar enterprise ("entrepreneurship through acquisition" buyer profile). Some larger operators (including one from a $42M/year fast-casual restaurant franchise system, and people with 9-figure prior exits) have bought 4-6 territories.

## Numbers — franchisor (Rolling Suds corporate) level, useful context on how a franchise system is actually built
- A franchisor needs realistically $1-2 million in capital to properly launch a franchise system (half a million is an absolute floor, and that's if selling territories in-house rather than outsourcing to a franchise sales org, which requires $2-4M since sales-org fees eat the franchise fee that would otherwise fund growth).
- Rough allocation of a $1M franchisor launch budget: $50K-150K for legal/franchise documents/operations manual; ~$200K to stand up an in-house franchise sales function; a minimum $100K/year (ideally $150K+) operations hire to help franchisees actually get open and profitable; the rest funds 6+ months of infrastructure before any meaningful royalty income arrives.
- Key economic reality: franchisees need the most support in their first 6-12 months, which is exactly when their royalty payments (often just $50-100/month early on) are smallest relative to the cost of supporting them — a franchisor must be capitalized to absorb this gap until reaching "royalty self-sufficiency" (royalties fund the support infrastructure).
- Rolling Suds' scale (at time of interview, ~15 months after launch): 51 franchisees, 187 territories across 26 states, 36 operating locations, 28 full-time corporate employees dedicated to supporting them — described as unusually well-staffed support relative to the ~14 other residential/commercial service brands the founder has worked with.
- Founder personally handled all 51 franchise sales conversations himself for the first 15 months to ensure correctly aligned expectations before recently handing off sales.
- Turned away 59 prospective franchisees (representing >$10M in territory-fee revenue) who had the capital but weren't judged a good cultural/operational fit — explicit philosophy of disqualifying poor fits even at direct cost to near-term revenue.

## What makes a good franchisee (buyer persona), per the founder
- Best fit: people comfortable being highly relationship-driven/extroverted for the initial 6-18 months of commercial B2B sales (pressure washing has a heavy warm-but-still-effortful outbound/relationship-sales component — e.g., convincing an $8M painting-company owner to hand over power-washing work) — introverts can work with a partner/spouse who covers that role.
- People who want to build large, scaling enterprises ("goalpost movers" who keep raising their target after hitting a milestone) do better than people testing a side-hustle ("let's see how it goes" mentality) — single-unit franchisees fail more often in the founder's experience because of this mindset difference.
- Counter-intuitively, prior successful independent entrepreneurs/founders sometimes do *worse* as franchisees than long-tenured W2 employees who are used to following a playbook, because entrepreneurs resist the "don't be creative, follow the system" discipline franchise systems require (illustrated with a Crumbl franchisee training anecdote: "we don't want you to be creative... you will have the most success just trusting the process").
- "Searchers" (entrepreneurship-through-acquisition buyers actively hunting for a small business to buy, e.g. on Twitter/BizBuySell) are a strong buyer pool: franchising offers proven systems/cash flow for typically less capital and less risk than buying an independent business outright, though most searchers overlook franchising as an option.

## General M&A/searcher tactical advice (tangential but given directly)
- Founder argues most "searchers" waste 18-24 months in due diligence when they could move much faster: cold-calling/texting a large volume of small business owners directly (rather than waiting for listings to hit BizBuySell, which is often already a bid-up seller's market by the time a deal is listed) can surface real interested sellers within an hour, since roughly 1 in 20 owners contacted (his rule-of-thumb: "talk to 100, five will be ready to sell") will engage seriously.

## Market saturation / cannibalization management
- Founder estimates the US could support roughly 900 Rolling Suds territories nationally, based on ~250,000 population per territory.
- Deliberately allows and expects consolidation: weaker-performing franchisees (who realize the business is more work than expected) often sell their territories to stronger neighboring operators who want to scale — the franchisor explicitly permits and even likes this dynamic as a wealth-building mechanism for both parties.

## Caveats
- Rolling Suds had not yet been open 12 months at time of interview (first franchisees launched July of prior year), so no franchisee performance data existed yet in the Franchise Disclosure Document (FTC rules require ~12 months of data before disclosure) — buyers into a young/emerging brand are betting on the founder/team/vision, not a proven track record.
- Franchise ownership generally is explicitly framed as NOT low-risk or passive — "it is not a get-rich-quick scheme... you are still starting a business from scratch," just with more support/tools than doing it solo. The founder personally rejects prospective buyers he believes are a poor fit even when they have the capital and would likely "crush it" elsewhere.
- Much of the franchisor-economics detail here (capital requirements, royalty structure) is broadly applicable to anyone considering *starting* a franchise brand, not just buying into one — treated by the host as equally valuable "how a $500K business is run" vs. "how a $100M+ business is run" content.
