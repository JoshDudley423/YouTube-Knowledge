# Start This Simple Side Hustle Before Everyone Else Does
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VGAOCH2vYwM | Published: 2025-08-31

Mobile sauna / cold plunge (contrast therapy) business breakdown: Chris ranks 5 real operators, then interviews Danny Geurink of Purely Nomadic Sauna Co. (Grand Rapids, MI) in depth.

## The business idea and market context
- Mobile sauna and/or cold plunge rental: buy a trailer-mounted sauna (and often an add-on cold plunge), rent it out by the hour/day/weekend, drop off and pick up (or run pop-up/event sessions).
- Market sizing cited: cold plunge market ~$318M two years prior, projected to hit ~$500M by 2030; search volume for "cold plunge"/"sauna" keywords up 370% over a couple years. Industry described as still in an early "education phase" — expect to still be early 3-5 years out.
- Reported operator range: $10,000-$50,000/month revenue, ~50% gross margins, some reaching breakeven in as little as 75 days.

## Model 1 (ranked #5): Denver Mobile Sauna (Micah)
- Pure drop-off rental model, no fixed location, no special partnerships.
- Pricing: $300/2 hrs, $450/4 hrs, $525/8 hrs, $600/overnight, +$200 for a full weekend — tiered/anchored pricing pushes ~70% of customers to the higher weekend option since fulfillment labor is similar regardless of rental length.
- At just 10 rentals/month @ ~$450 avg = $4,500/month revenue; costs are mainly time/labor/gas plus equipment maintenance and insurance.
- Does some wellness-studio pop-up partnerships for recurring revenue without a fixed location.
- Can be run as a weekend-only side hustle: 2-3 rentals/weekend ≈ $1,000-$2,000/month extra income.

## Model 2 (ranked #4): Cedar Grove Sauna, Maine (Jackie Stratton)
- Started 2020 as an off-season side hustle; now serves 8,000-12,000 people/year across 2 fixed locations + 1 mobile unit rotating weekly across New England, with 8 staff.
- Cost of goods is almost entirely wood: ~10 cords/year at $400-$900/cord = ~$4,000-$9,000/year in wood cost.
- Having a fixed physical location signals permanence/premium positioning (compared to purely mobile competitors) and supports higher pricing — direct analogy drawn to mall-kiosk vs. storefront pricing power.
- Built an email list of repeat "sauna aficionados" and does international trips (Finland, Lithuania, Latvia, Sweden) to build authentic expertise/authority — grew from side hustle to full-time purely by following overwhelming organic demand.

## Model 3 (ranked #3): Chill Wagon Wellness, Arizona (Vincent & Brock, age 22)
- $60,000+/month revenue. Revenue streams: private bookings $350-$500/2-hr minimum; public event sessions at festivals/farmers markets $35-$45/person; corporate half-day bookings $1,500 (unlimited employee use).
- Operational cost per event: only $30-$60 (ice + gas).
- Custom trailer setup: 2 jetted cold plunges + 5-person sauna, compression boots, synthetic turf flooring.
- Built a branded event format ("Plunge") combining workout → cold plunge → sauna → coffee social, widening the customer funnel beyond just wellness enthusiasts.
- B2B partnerships with local gyms (e.g., Burn Boot Camp) — symbiotic co-marketing (analogous to a Great Clips inside a Walmart) positions them as a "wellness concierge," not just a sauna vendor.
- $3,500 Facebook ad spend generated ~80% of leads (with likely more organic spillover from shares). Prioritizes business-owner testimonials over consumer reviews since B2B partnerships are core to the model.
- Benefited from a wealthy, fitness-obsessed, year-round-hot market (Scottsdale/Phoenix) and zero competition at launch. Has since added mobile IV therapy as an upsell to increase revenue per customer.

## Model 4 (ranked #2): Drip Sauna Co., Cleburne, TX (Sam)
- Serial entrepreneur (previously ran a fencing business and a stock-tank/cold-plunge installation business — stock tanks bought at Tractor Supply ~$500 + ~$100-200 pool equipment from Amazon, graded land, charged $3,000-$5,000/install, grown via Facebook ads).
- Contrast-therapy business idea sparked by a personal bachelor-party experience; spent 2 years looking for a standalone location before instead piggybacking inside unused space at an existing gym (~2-bedroom equivalent footprint) for $2,000/month rent.
- Startup equipment: 4 infrared saunas at $7,000-$8,000 each (financeable) + 2 cold plunges at $10,000 each.
- Within a few months: 130 monthly recurring members paying $75/month for unlimited 45-minute sessions; walk-ins pay $30/session. That's $10,000+/month recurring revenue, and the business broke even (including paying off equipment) in under a year.
- Competitors in the area charge $150-$175/month for the same equipment and require a separate location from the gym — Sam undercuts them by half while being more convenient (one-stop-shop inside the gym).
- Regret/lesson: didn't install drains under the cold plunges, making water changes a hassle — install drains if building this.
- Side offering: will install a stock-tank cold plunge at a customer's home for ~$1,500 profit/job — a lumpy high-ticket offer that complements the smoother recurring membership revenue. Nearly all customers sourced via Facebook ads.
- Ranked #2 specifically because of the "piggyback" gym-partnership model: built-in day-one customer base/foot traffic, cross-promotion, and shared email list meant minimal need for independent marketing — "your location is your marketing."

## Model 5 (ranked #1, full interview): Purely Nomadic Sauna Co., Grand Rapids, MI (Danny Geurink)
- One-man mobile wood-burning sauna rental business, run alongside his separate home-inspection business (home inspection is seasonal, so the sauna business offsets that seasonality — same principle recommended for a pressure-washing business adding a Christmas-light-install business in winter).
- Origin: personal cold-plunging (cutting ice in a lake) → built a backyard cold plunge and infrared sauna → disliked infrared → decided a real wood-burning sauna was better → realized buying a used mobile trailer sauna from another operator (in Minnesota, who was exiting/downsizing) was cheaper than building one himself given his own time cost.
- Bought first unit for just over $20,000 (~10 ft hot room + ~4 ft changing room, solar-powered lighting, nicer stove, "classic" look, 3,500 lb tow weight — needs a full-size truck). Bought a second, lighter unit for under $10,000 shortly after (aluminum-frame, cedar 2x4 structure, translucent thermoplastic shell) — much lighter, movable by hand, no changing room but comparable capacity; in hindsight he says he'd have started with this cheaper/lighter unit first.
- Forecasting full payback on both units within 12-18 months at ~50% profit margins.
- Wood-burning (not electric/infrared) is essential for a *mobile* rental because you can't guarantee a power source (30-50 amp outlet) at every drop-off site; electric/infrared makes more sense for a fixed home installation.
- Wood choice: needs seasoned hardwood (oak is best/most available locally; maple works) to reach 190-200°F — softwood/pine burns too fast. Saunas are wood-efficient (a couple logs to start, one or two more during use). He has access to free wood via his parents' farm — otherwise cost/time of sourcing and cutting wood is the main recurring input cost. Operator tip from peers: never let customers supply/burn their own wood (risk of them burning treated lumber/deck boards).
- Cold plunge is an add-on, not sold standalone — uses cheap inflatable tubs (a few hundred dollars each) rather than fixed units, since keeping water cold in summer without a chiller (chillers run $1,000-$2,000+) is difficult; ice is used instead.
- Pricing: $350/day, $650/weekend (Friday evening–Monday morning), $1,200/week.
- Customer acquisition: 100% via Facebook Marketplace listings (no paid ads, no boosted posts) — posted day-rate pricing plus a link to a Squarespace website with Acuity scheduling embedded as a booking funnel. First-mover advantage in his metro area (no direct mobile sauna competitor in Grand Rapids at time of filming) — recommends checking for competitors via AdScraper or a simple Google Maps search; argues both high and low competition can be good signals (high = validated demand, low = first-mover opportunity, assuming you can educate the market).
- Customer profile is broad: bachelorette/wedding parties (killing time while getting ready), birthday parties, health/wellness enthusiasts, groups renting a sauna at a lake house/cabin, and people wanting to "test drive" before building their own.
- Seasonality: summer is a weak season for saunas (a peer operator largely stops marketing/pauses in summer); demand picks up heavily starting in early fall through winter. Exploring off-season partnerships with Airbnbs/short-term rentals (sauna as a listing add-on), especially at lakes, to smooth demand.
- Costs: insurance is "a couple thousand bucks" (plus roughly $500 per additional unit); wood, gas, and time are the other main costs — Danny estimated 50%+ net margins are plausible given only gas/insurance/wood/time as costs.
- Explicitly framed as a family project — wants his 4 kids to learn entrepreneurship through it, not solely a profit vehicle; not his primary income source.

## Cross-cutting takeaways (Chris's synthesis across all 5)
- Facebook (ads and/or free Marketplace listings) is the dominant acquisition channel across every operator profiled — driving 60-80% of leads. Recommends starting with a free/cheap Marketplace post before committing to paid Facebook ads.
- Partnering with an existing business serving the same customer base (gyms, wellness studios, Airbnbs) consistently outperforms going fully standalone — provides built-in customer base, cross-promotion, and reduces marketing need.
- Tiered pricing (hour/day/weekend) anchors customers toward higher-value bookings.
- Offering a free first session is used as a conversion/retention tactic (creates a taste of the experience that drives repeat/referral).
- For B2B-oriented models, business-owner testimonials build more trust with prospective partners than individual consumer reviews.
- Corporate/event bookings expand surface area — every employee/attendee who tries it on their employer's dime is a potential future individual customer.
- Physical or digital "location" (a fixed storefront presence, or ranking well on Google Maps/organic search) meaningfully affects perceived legitimacy and pricing power — don't underestimate it.

## Caveats / risks raised
- This is explicitly framed as an early-stage/emerging market — patience is required; genuine enthusiasm for saunas/cold plunge as a user is called a practical advantage for sustaining the business.
- Chiller-based cold plunges add real cost/complexity (chillers $1,000-$2,000+); several operators solve this cheaply with inflatable tubs and ice instead.
- Wood-burning saunas require an ongoing wood supply chain (sourcing, cutting/seasoning, transport) and carry fire/safety considerations; letting customers add their own wood/materials to the fire is flagged as a specific risk to avoid.
- None of the profiled operators has this as their sole income source (all have a second income stream or seasonal side business), suggesting it's commonly built as a diversification/side-income play rather than sole livelihood, at least early on.
