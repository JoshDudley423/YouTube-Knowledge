# Exploring a New Business Idea: Drone Roof Inspections and Selling Leads to Roofers
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JLu1Mt3hCm4 | Published: 2024-03-03

Live call-in Q&A format ("Kerner's Corner," episode 3 of a planned 10-episode series). Chris takes live listener questions on business ideas/problems and gives rapid-fire prescriptive advice. Covers many distinct ideas/situations; each summarized below by caller topic.

## 1. HVAC data SaaS for commercial property managers (caller: Tamone)
- Idea: software that gives historical data on a building's HVAC system output/performance, sold to commercial property managers.
- Problem: can't find these property managers on LinkedIn.
- Advice: they are on LinkedIn, just under titles you haven't identified yet — if you have zero customers, first nail down what job title to search (interview/talk to a few first); if you do have some, look at what titles/companies your existing customers hold and use a tool like **Apollo** to search LinkedIn by that title for cold outreach. If stuck identifying the right customer segment, hire a **virtual assistant via Upwork** to research it — VAs who do this kind of research all day are full of ideas. General framing: hard-to-reach customer segments are often offset by strong recurring revenue/low churn/high ticket once you break in — persist.

## 2. Cold email lead-gen agency pricing (caller: Corey)
- Context: caller runs a cold-email lead-gen service connecting business owners (initially met via family's self-storage properties) to buyers — e.g., an Amazon FBA seller suggested pricing like a real-estate wholesaler's 10% commission model; caller was considering ~2% commission, or a $500 onboarding fee + $250-350 per booked meeting.
- Advice: **don't do commission/percentage pricing from day one** — you don't yet know your conversion rate, lead quality, or how many calls it takes to close a deal, so percentage-based pricing is premature. Instead, start with a simple **flat pay-per-booked-meeting fee** (e.g., $50-$200, whatever feels fair), track outcomes obsessively ("how'd that guy work out?"), and once you have real data (e.g., "it takes 5 calls on average to close"), use that as a case study/proof point ("we deliver a 20x ROI") to justify testing higher percentage-based pricing with a client later. Framed generally: **start cheap to get data and a case study, raise prices later** — don't try to extract maximum margin before you've proven the model.

## 3. "Drunk Tow" — rebranded towing as a convenience/home-delivery service (caller: A. Ortiz)
- Idea: rebrand towing specifically around drunk drivers — someone who's had too much to drink at a bar schedules (via app/text) to have their car towed home overnight so it's in their driveway by morning, instead of leaving it at the bar or driving drunk. Ortiz proposed the domain "drunktoes.com" (unclaimed at time of recording).
- Business model: **no owned trucks/equipment** — pure lead-generation/dispatch layer subcontracting to existing local tow companies (many of which sit idle 90-95% of the time outside of illegal-parking contract work), charging the consumer a flat fee on top of the base tow cost as the middleman margin (Uber-style: gross booking + take rate).
- Chris's take: liked it once framed as a positioning/marketing exercise rather than needing to reinvent the towing industry itself. Recommended: pitch a single small tow company as a fulfillment test partner (no profit yet, just testing local PR interest and whether bar owners care), get their pricing, and validate demand cheaply before scaling. Suggested it's more attractive if the tow driver also gives the customer a ride home (saves them an Uber). Marketing idea drawn from Chris's own past business (an iPhone-repair shop that gave bars free Tyvek wristbands printed with the shop's ad, distributed to bar patrons, redeemable as a repair coupon) — recommended offering bars free drink-special wristbands carrying the tow service's ad. Also suggested early-morning (5am) flyers left on cars still parked outside bars ("your car could have been home already").
- Open question flagged, unresolved in the call: exact margin/fee structure beyond "charge a flat consumer price, negotiate a percentage with the tow operator on the back end."

## 4. Eclipse glasses bulk sales — two separate callers with the same product/problem
- Both callers independently built businesses selling solar eclipse glasses (ahead of an April 2024 total eclipse) after paying $40 for a pair back in ~2017/2023 and deciding to source/sell their own. One had 40,000 units in inventory; both had sent thousands of cold emails to schools/event venues with poor response.
- Chris's core critique: **the underlying event only lasts ~2.5 minutes and glasses aren't needed for future eclipses** — he argues the skill/hustle invested here would be better redirected toward a more durable business, even though he acknowledges writing a prior newsletter on making real money (potentially six-to-seven figures) from eclipse-adjacent business *at scale* (e.g., running an eclipse-viewing event/race, which can be repeated at future eclipses, unlike inventory of glasses).
- Given existing inventory that must be liquidated: recommends abandoning B2B outreach to schools/venues (they won't prioritize a multi-minute event) and instead going direct-to-consumer via **local Facebook buy/sell/garage-sale groups along the eclipse path**, appealing to individuals' "get-rich-quick" impulse — buy wholesale cheaply (e.g., $0.50-$5/unit) and resell at markup (e.g., $1 → $20, or $5 → $20) with volume price breaks (discounts at 500 or 1,000 units) and free shipping to move volume fast; adjust price downward if sales don't materialize within a couple weeks (since post-eclipse the inventory is worthless). Also suggested personally selling a batch at full margin in person near the eclipse path (e.g., Austin) rather than only wholesaling.

## 5. Drone roof inspections + selling leads to roofers (the video's title idea — caller: Alex)
- Idea: use a drone to inspect homeowners' roofs (marketed via door hangers, signs, Facebook), charge homeowners directly for the inspection (proposed ~$99, twice yearly), and if issues are found, sell the resulting lead (with homeowner consent to get quotes) to local roofing companies.
- Chris's advice:
  - Validate cheaply first: test with a small sample (~20 homes) before committing further, since it's a novel/unproven business model.
  - Consider **proactively drone-photographing every house on a block/street without prior arrangement**, then offering to sell the photo to the homeowner — cited a real example (caller's uncle) doing this commercially at scale (e.g., photographing a newly built high school) with a reported **~60% conversion rate** on selling the resulting photos; framed the privacy/annoyance risk as low and not a major concern.
  - Recommended door-knocking in the evening with multiple possible pitch angles: "want a nice framed photo of your home?" vs. "want your roof inspected once/twice a year for $100?" vs. "want a free roof inspection?" (monetizing the lead-sale side instead of the homeowner directly).
  - Marketplace framing: you can monetize the buyer side (homeowner pays for inspection), the seller side (roofer pays for the lead), or both — but Chris recommends **only monetizing one side initially** to isolate whether the core offer works before testing pricing on the other side; e.g., if most homeowners will accept a free photo, then separately test what percentage would pay for one, rather than conflating both tests.

## 6. IoT waste-management startup — sales/booth strategy at a trade conference (caller: Odetta)
- Context: caller is a data engineer (not a salesperson) at a funded IoT startup selling a device that sits under commercial/residential waste bins to measure fill levels every 10 minutes, enabling accurate route planning and per-bin invoicing (versus averaging tonnage across a whole route) — customers include waste haulers and institutions with ESG/landfill-diversion reporting requirements (some now legally mandated). Struggled at an industry conference (Wisconsin Dells) identifying/approaching unfamiliar waste-hauler prospects.
- Advice: build a target list by profiling your **existing customers** (already skewing toward education institutions and haulers with corporate ESG-reporting pressure) and use tools like **Outscraper or Google Maps** to find similar companies within a set radius (e.g., one-hour drive of the conference), filtering for B2B-focused haulers rather than pure consumer-facing ones (visible from their websites). Prioritize the top 10 B2B-focused candidates specifically because **more sophisticated operators who already sense the coming regulatory/ESG pressure are better prospects than laggards who won't adopt until it's too late.**
- Tactical approach for cold conversations: lead with a "fear tactic"/survey framing (e.g., "did you know about [upcoming ESG requirement]? We're doing a quick survey on how companies are preparing") rather than an upfront sales pitch — this either surfaces an existing pain point or educates them on one, and either way opens a real conversation; don't try to sell immediately, learn their pain points and what messaging resonates first. Also recommended reaching out to a shortlist of prospects *before* the conference to set up warm meetings, and checking whether conference organizers sell attendee lists (sometimes bundled with premium sponsorship packages).
- Separate hiring question (same caller): concerned about the risk of hiring, having only heard negative anecdotes online. Advice: hire **young, humble, fresh-out-of-college people** rather than "industry experts" or competitors' employees — more moldable even though they require more training, net positive overall; Chris reports mostly positive personal experience managing/hiring and recommends going in expecting good outcomes (a "positive confirmation bias").

## 7. "Beyond the Page" — LinkedIn video-testimonial/resume enhancement service for job seekers (caller: Andrew Hill)
- Idea: a $99 one-time service (plus $25/year hosting after) where job seekers record short (1-2 min) videos per past role/education via a third-party tool (VideoAsk), get them professionally post-produced with name graphics, then embed the videos into their LinkedIn profile's experience sections (LinkedIn supports a media-link field per role) so employers can see/hear them instead of just reading bullet points; optional add-on: a longer "mock interview" video and a talk-show-style podcast appearance.
- Chris's critique: $99 is too low to be a strong standalone business at scale, and targeting "any job seeker" makes conversion/results too unpredictable. Recommended **narrowing exclusively to salespeople** — there's already an entire industry built around evaluating/placing salespeople specifically because they directly generate revenue, making a compelling video demo commercially valuable in a way a generic job-seeker video isn't.
- Concrete reformulated product suggestion: a single, two-segment video — (1) ~30 seconds of the salesperson introducing themselves and their track record (e.g., "3 years selling Salesforce, top 4%, $375k last year"), followed by (2) a 2-3 minute **"highlight reel" of real sales calls** (audio from both sides), aggregated from multiple real calls using AI video-editing tools (mentions CapCut, "Clap" and similar AI tools, roughly ~$80/month) — analogous to a sports highlight reel. Suggested tracking engagement via Vimeo-hosted video analytics (views/click-through) since there was no engagement tracking in the original plan.
- On pricing: dislikes the idea of adding a podcast appearance to justify a higher price (skeptical anyone would watch it, undermining perceived value); instead suggests tracking who views a candidate's profile/video and charging a **placement fee** (a discounted version of a traditional recruiting placement fee) to the employer or candidate at the point of hire, rather than a flat low upfront price.
- Also recommends testing cheaply before building full infrastructure: get 5-10 job seekers (ideally same industry, to control variables) to self-record with a phone/Loom and manually run the concept before investing in the full production pipeline/website.

## 8. Virtual assistant (VA) staffing agency based in India (caller: Shaan)
- Context: caller is a student, previously an investment-banking intern doing middle-office/admin support work, based in India where he can hire capable full-time VAs for roughly **$100-150/month** (8hr/day, 5 days/week).
- Chris's take: viable, still early innings for VA agencies, and being based in India personally is a real competitive advantage (most VA agency operators aren't themselves overseas — most VA agencies are built around the Philippines, Pakistan, or Latin America talent pools instead). Recommends embracing "cheapest" as a deliberate positioning rather than competing on being a premium/curated provider — cites a comparable model (e.g., "graphic designers in India for $400/month flat, unlimited requests" vs. competitors charging $5,000-$10,000/month for similar output) as a proof point that heavy underpricing relative to Western agencies with a strong niche framing works well.
- On what to actually sell (Chris's own stated pain point as a potential customer): he currently juggles 5 different part-time VAs doing 5 different tasks and finds that fragmented; suggests the more valuable positioning/brand is a **"VA CEO"** — a single accountable point of contact who manages a bench of specialist VAs behind the scenes, so the client only ever has to interface with one person/manager rather than coordinating several individually, even if the client ends up paying somewhat more for that consolidation and accountability.

## 9. Scaling/rebranding a local trophy & awards engraving business; "free inventory" via trophy recycling (caller: Charb Wire)
- Context: caller's family previously ran a recreation center; his mother separately started a laser-engraving/sublimation trophy and awards business (named after her), which caller bought via seller financing. It's currently his side business (~$90,000/year revenue) alongside a larger, unrelated merchant-services/point-of-sale business that consumes most of his time (80/20 focus split). He wants to scale/rename the business to be less locally bound, and separately floated monetizing free trophy "donations" people offer him.
- Differentiation examples already in place: an exclusive licensing deal with a horse-racing hall of fame museum to produce/sell reprint commemorative plaques of famous racehorses (Secretariat, Man o' War, etc.) both in-store and online — a recurring, differentiated revenue line beyond one-off local orders.
- The "free inventory" idea: people regularly call offering to donate old/unwanted trophies (e.g., a departing karate studio's large trophies, or a family's old trophies being cleared out) since the parts/manufacturer for old trophy components are often discontinued, making them unsellable as-is but sometimes salvageable (removing/replacing the top figure and plate to repurpose as, e.g., a basketball or golf trophy) — larger repurposed trophies can resell for over $100 each. Caller wanted to formalize this into a recycling/monetization program but noted most donated trophies (an unspecified, "not a lot" percentage) are actually usable/salvageable; the rest have some scrap value in their marble/lead/plastic components, but recyclers price "dirty"/mixed material lower.
- Chris's critique: this specific "recycle random people's trophies one at a time" angle **doesn't scale well** — too much manual labor (miter saw work, laser re-engraving) relative to the ~$100-200 resale value per item, and the local market is limited.
- Better version suggested: run **organized "trophy recycling drive" events with local schools** (parents notified via flyer/email to drop off old trophies for recycling) — a single pickup trip nets dozens/hundreds of units at once instead of one-off individual calls, meaningfully reducing raw materials cost (Chris estimated maybe cutting a third off what the business would otherwise spend on new components) while generating positive local PR/goodwill (parents feel good their kid's trophy isn't simply thrown away) and free marketing/brand recall for future new-trophy purchases — likened to how electronics or pharmaceutical take-back/recycling drives work.
- Separate marketing idea (unrelated to the recycling angle): start a **TikTok channel showing funny/novel laser engravings** (e.g., an inside joke engraved on a parent's mug, revealed as a gift) alongside actual footage of the laser engraving process itself — Chris predicts strong performance on Reels/Shorts/TikTok specifically because people enjoy watching the engraving process, which could drive nationwide e-commerce traffic; contingent on first updating the outward-facing Shopify storefront (caller noted their customer-facing Shopify site is outdated; a separate internal supplier-facing platform is more robust but not meant for customers).

## 10. Bootstrapped multi-product "startup studio" seeking small investor capital (caller, name not fully clear — "Build for Growth")
- Context: caller runs a part-time dev-shop/startup studio model — hiring part-time designers/developers/product managers to build and launch multiple small products (e.g., a job-seeker product, a small AI "micro-SaaS" app) over the past ~8 months, has launched four products, wants outside capital (a "very low six figures," e.g., $10k-$50k range) specifically to pay contractors faster/better rather than to spend on paid ads, without giving up traditional VC-style equity/board terms.
- Advice: don't pursue traditional venture/angel investors chasing billion-dollar outcomes — instead target **local small-time investors/small business owners with low-seven-figure net worths who are comfortable writing $10k-$50k checks**, typically people already in your personal network who know and trust you. Lead with your best 2-3 case studies (what you built, how much you put in, what it returned) even if there's only one strong example, and **offer very investor-friendly terms on the first deal** specifically to make it easy to say yes — the first deal is the hardest to close, but a strong outcome from it becomes the case study that makes subsequent capital-raising much easier. Tactically: **pick up the phone and call people directly** rather than emailing — much harder for someone to say no to a direct call than to ignore an email.

## 11. Balancing an existing profitable operating business against pursuing a bigger new venture (caller: Ross, runs a Salesforce consultancy, "Pong Solutions")
- Context: caller struggles with "shiny object syndrome" — wants to pursue a larger new venture requiring investor pitches, but is worried about diverting focus from a rapidly growing existing consultancy.
- Advice: you have to get through the "messy middle" by **hiring a CEO/operator for the existing business** so you're comfortable it runs at 70%+ of your own effectiveness (Chris's experience: a hired CEO ends up performing either around 70% as well as the founder, or sometimes 200% better than expected) — necessary if you're serious about pursuing the second venture, since investors are unlikely to back someone visibly still splitting focus/pulled by shiny-object syndrome. On who to hire to replace the caller's own sales role specifically: recommended prioritizing someone with strong people skills and the ability to conceptualize/apply client business concepts to the product (i.e., trainable consultant-type talent) over someone who already has direct competitor/product experience, since competitor-experienced hires can bring bad habits ("terrible at selling" was Ross's own complaint about typical hires in this space) that are harder to unlearn than teaching a capable generalist your specific method from scratch.

## 12. "Carfax for homes" — aggregated home-repair/insurance-claim history report (caller: Mike)
- Idea: a paid report (proposed ~$200) aggregating a home's repair history — insurance claims, past roof work, HVAC work, etc. — sold to prospective home buyers, potentially disrupting/replacing traditional home inspectors.
- Chris's key challenge flagged: **data sourcing** — unlike cars (where repair history reporting to a central database is a more established practice), most home service providers (plumbers, roofers, HVAC companies) have no obligation or existing infrastructure to report work performed, except for insurance claims (which do have an existing claims database and are suggested as the easiest first data source).
- Comparison/framework offered: **build a "Plaid for homes"** — Plaid became a multi-billion-dollar company not primarily through superior technology but by grinding out one-by-one manual integrations with thousands of small credit unions/community banks that had no APIs; a comparable moat here would require literally going business-by-business (a handful of HVAC/roofing/plumbing/foundation-repair companies in one small town at a time) negotiating access to their repair/address/cost history — with an unresolved incentive question (what's in it for the contractor to share this?) that Chris floats a revenue-share (e.g., 10% of each report sold that used their data) as a possible answer, though flagged as still unsolved/tedious to execute. Alternative data-sourcing idea floated: approach home-inspection companies directly, or better, the **software platforms that home inspectors themselves use** to conduct inspections, since that would aggregate data at a much higher level than one-by-one inspector outreach; suggested using an LLM/AI tool to clean/standardize the aggregated data into a per-address report.
- Go-to-market once data exists: sell to real estate agents as an add-on/upsell alongside a standard home inspection (compared to "gap insurance" — priced attractively even if buyers aren't sure exactly what it covers).

## 13. Zillow-meets-Pinterest matchmaking platform for custom home builders (same caller: Mike, second idea)
- Idea A (higher-tech version): a visual discovery platform (map-based, drill down by state/metro) where users browse photos of local custom-home builders' past work, "like" (double-tap) images they're drawn to, and an AI-driven recommendation engine surfaces a personalized shortlist of 2-3 builder matches based on style preference, then sells that qualified lead to matching local builders — addressing that individual custom-home builders notoriously have poor/no digital marketing presence.
- Chris's preferred alternative (lower-tech, faster to execute): treat it as a **local lead-generation brokerage**, one market at a time (example used: "BuildGreenBay.com"). Process: scrape/manually research every builder in one local market (their history, quality, reputation, using a VA to compile it), build a simple high-SEO landing page targeted at consumers wanting to build a custom home, capture just an email address up front (low friction), then route them to a more detailed Typeform-style questionnaire (square footage, style preferences, etc.) that still captures partial data even if abandoned. A dedicated salesperson then calls the lead, builds a relationship (like a real estate agent would), and plays matchmaker to the best-fit local builder.
- Monetization rationale: builders already routinely pay real estate agents 3-6% commission for referrals despite doing all the actual work themselves (a dynamic builders reportedly resent) — so a referral fee of 1-2% (still cheaper than what they're used to paying) is an easy sell, and pitched to homeowners as a completely free service (paid by the builder, not the buyer). Expect only a fraction of local builders (e.g., 3-5 out of 20) to sign a formal referral agreement initially; expect roughly a year to build a working pipeline, with **five-figure referral checks per completed build** once established — then replicate the same playbook market-by-market.

## Caveats / risks (general, apply across most segments above)
- This is a rapid-fire live-call format — Chris's advice is improvised, high-level, and not based on deep due diligence on any single caller's specific market; several ideas (drone roof inspections, Carfax-for-homes, trophy recycling) are explicitly flagged by Chris himself as unproven/risky and recommended only with small, cheap validation tests before further investment.
- Several ideas (eclipse glasses, drunk tow, Beyond the Page) reflect callers' early-stage/pre-revenue efforts; no confirmed financial outcomes are available for most of them within this transcript.
- Only the trophy business (~$90,000/year revenue, historical/current) and the Salesforce consultancy (unspecified but described as "growing very rapidly") represent established, already-revenue-generating businesses among the callers.
