# My RV and Mobile Home Park Investing Masterclass
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A4Gv_mmr5tM | Published: 2024-08-03

Chris Koerner's rapid-fire list of 18 concrete value-add tactics he's used across 31 mobile home/RV park acquisitions (with investors) over 6 years, applied post-purchase to raise property value. Business model: buy an existing mobile home/RV park, add value via low-cost operational improvements, raise rents responsibly, and only maintain the underground infrastructure (not the housing units themselves, where avoidable).

## The 18 value-add levers
1. **Signage** — install large new signage on day one of closing; roads by these parks often see 10,000–30,000 cars/day of wasted visibility otherwise.
2. **Local newspaper ads** — still effective for advertising vacancies in rural towns.
3. **Landscaping** — spend $10K–$20K on day one to justify a rent increase; example math given: a $50/pad rent increase across 50 pads = $450,000 in added property value at a 7.5% cap rate, easily justifying a $20K spend.
4. **Road repair** — fix potholes/gravel roads; upgrading to concrete/asphalt is never cost-justified ("never really penciled").
5. **Portable self-storage units** — add 10x10 self-storage on unused park land; self-sufficient, increases tenant stickiness/retention. Use a market-research tool like SquareFoot to check local self-storage demand/saturation before investing.
6. **Backfill vacant pads** — buy used RV/mobile homes and seller-finance them to new tenants to reduce move-in friction (waiting for a tenant to arrive with their own unit is slow). Caveat: legality of seller-financing units varies by state; in some states you must be a licensed mortgage broker to do this.
7. **Convert park-owned homes to tenant-owned** — also varies by state (straightforward in Texas, Oklahoma, and across the Southeast); goal is to avoid maintaining individual housing units and only maintain underground infrastructure.
8. **List vacancies on Facebook Marketplace and Craigslist early** — post before even getting the property under contract (not before closing, but before contract) to gauge real local rental demand risk-free; claim almost no competing parks do this, and inquiries can start arriving before you even own the property. Takes ~30 minutes.
9. **Add a billboard** — where zoning/space allows, billboards can rent for $400–$33,000/month with no maintenance required (mentions parceling off and selling a billboard-equipped section of one East Texas park).
10. **Raise rents** — typically done in year one and periodically thereafter, but only after adding real value to the park, and giving tenants ~90 days notice — contrasted with "big REIT" players who raise rents annually and treat tenants poorly.
11. **Build a simple website** — Wix (their favorite) or Squarespace; template is reused/copy-pasted across each new park acquisition (swap photos, site map, name, address).
12. **Add online rent payment** — AppFolio for mobile home parks (found Rent Manager too clunky), Campspot for RV parks (a newer platform, not around 5–10 years ago, now widely used). Also digitizes complaint/application submission. Migration can take months since many tenants lack bank accounts — solved by issuing reloadable debit cards.
13. **Eliminate on-site managers where feasible** — for smaller parks without heavy amenities, replace a live-in manager with a Thumbtack-sourced handyman checking in monthly/quarterly. Not viable for larger or amenity-heavy parks.
14. **Be personally reachable** — answer phone/text/email promptly, especially important once an on-site manager has been removed.
15. **Send small tenant-retention gifts** — Amazon gift cards or fruit baskets on 1/2/3-year tenant anniversaries; inexpensive but described as something "no tenant has ever experienced," aiding both retention and attracting good tenants.
16. **Trim trees in month one** — an easy, commonly-overlooked cosmetic fix; previous owners rarely do this, and it visibly transforms the park's appearance (raising the canopy so trees look "like a lollipop" rather than sagging onto the units).
17. **Find your informant tenant** — identify the one chatty tenant who "spills all the secrets" about the park's real problems and history (his term: "my Bridget," after the first one); this substitutes for a $3,000/month formal property manager's intel-gathering.
18. **Personally contact every tenant in week one** — ideally meet in person day one plus a follow-up phone call; learn complaints/likes and fix the most common cheap-to-fix issues immediately. Claim: tenants often already expect a rent increase (knowing their rent is below market) and won't resent it if they see the park genuinely improving rather than just extracting value.

## Caveats
- Legal/regulatory variation by state is repeatedly flagged for seller-financing (#6) and park-owned-to-tenant-owned home conversion (#7) — what's simple in TX/OK/Southeast may require mortgage-broker licensing elsewhere.
- Self-storage add-on (#5) should be checked against local demand data first — market can be oversaturated.
- This is an operational playbook for an already-acquired park, not a guide to sourcing capital or acquiring the park itself; assumes investor capital and an existing acquisition (compare to Koerner's separate account of a partnership going wrong in this same space — see "What I Learned From My Failed $50m Business Partnership").
