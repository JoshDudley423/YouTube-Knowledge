# These "Boring" Items Make Him $17.3k/Month (Side Hustle)
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VmhEvIB_XNA | Published: 2025-11-10

Interview with Shannon Jean (liquidation/reselling expert, repeat guest, runs shannonjean.com/mastermind at $5/month, ~1,800 members) on reselling liquidation/auction inventory, from $0-capital flipping to buying truckloads.

## Core thesis: "boring products can make you rich because everyone ignores them"
- Trendy/hyped categories (electronics, iPhones) attract too many competing resellers and bidders who know exactly what things are worth, compressing margins. Unglamorous categories (rugs, books, restaurant equipment, parking meters, pressure washers) get ignored by sophisticated buyers, leaving deep discounts.
- Analogy drawn to stock market capital flows: money/attention chasing AI stocks (Nvidia, Tesla) starves "boring" stocks (Chipotle, McDonald's) of attention even though they're still solid — same dynamic applies to liquidation categories: attention flows to "sexy" resale categories, leaving boring ones underbid.

## Step 0: Research before buying (Shannon's framework)
1. **Find what sells in your specific market/platform** (eBay vs. Facebook Marketplace vs. Poshmark differ) — check top sellers in a category to see what's moving.
2. **Study active listings/competitors**: use incognito browser mode logged into Facebook to see unbiased top search/filter results; check a specific competitor seller's *profile* to see their full inventory (not just top search hits) — reveals what a known reseller is actively selling and what they consider profitable.
3. **Use a scraping tool** (mentioned: Instant Data Scraper) to pull a seller's active/sold listings into a spreadsheet to systematically map out real market pricing in your hyper-local area.
4. **Identify your "unfair advantage"**: physical space (barn, warehouse, storage unit), extra labor (teenage kids to move heavy items), a skill (repairing small mechanics, upholstery), a personal network/industry connection, or AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT's video feature used to diagnose/fix a broken appliance or lawn mower) — then match product categories to that advantage rather than buying whatever's cheap. Explicit example: don't buy heavy appliances if you're a single person with a small garage and no help.

## $0-capital tier: examples cited
- **Mike McCabe** (a weatherman, side hustle): picks up free/near-free couches from Facebook Marketplace listings, cleans/repairs them (bought a ~$20-30 upholstery repair kit off Amazon to fix rips/tears), resells for profit — reported ~$40,000 in sales so far in the year cited, and one couch example projected to net $500-600 within 24 hours. Uses time-lapse cleaning videos as content/marketing.
- **Mindy**: bought two pallets of books from GovDeals (government surplus auction site) for $1 total, sold them over 3 months for $5,000 on eBay (books niched into homeschooling/education categories sold for $45-68 each) — zero shipping cost since she picked up locally with a truck.
- Framing: "the building of the thing is the research" — cheap/free items let you learn the resale game with near-zero financial risk before scaling into real capital outlay.

## Mid-tier: buying pallets/truckloads via government and liquidation auctions
- **GovDeals.com**: public-sector surplus auction site (schools, cities, states); Shannon estimates fewer than 20% of GovDeals lots have a reserve price, because the seller's real alternative is paying a junk-removal company to haul it away — meaning many lots are essentially "must-sell" with no floor.
- Restaurant/commercial equipment example: Hobart commercial mixers, which reliably sell for $4,000-$5,000 retail (they break often and repair costs are high), can often be bought on GovDeals for ~$500-$1,000+ — a good niche if you have restaurant-industry connections/buyers.
- Poorly-photographed/described lots are prime targets since fewer bidders understand their value — example: a "motorcycle simulator" (police pursuit-training equipment) listed for $160 with a bad photo; using ChatGPT to research the make/model and typical resale value ($5,000-$15,000 for a complete/working unit) helped validate it as a strong buy. Tip: call the seller's listed phone contact directly (rather than asking questions in the public listing, which leaks the opportunity to other bidders) to get more detail.
- Parking pay stations (Flowbird-branded, sold off by a city/state government) bought at ~$100/unit with no reserve — suggested reuse: partner with an underused parking lot owner on a month-to-month lease, install cheap parking stations, and start a small parking-management side business.
- Bulk furniture liquidation example: 34 hotel rooms' worth of furniture (~12 items/room = ~408 items: sofa, chair, coffee table, TV+stand, 2 nightstands, headboard, dresser, mirror, table) bought for a $2,000 "buy it now" price (~$5/item). Suggested resale execution: post one aggregated Facebook Marketplace listing (or one listing per item type across all units, priced low), state a fixed pickup address/hours, refuse to respond to "is this still available" messages, don't negotiate, and let buyers self-serve pickup — avoids per-item logistics/delivery. Estimated ~8x return (e.g., $40/item average resale × 408 items ≈ $16,000 against $2,000 cost).
- Chris's personal anecdote: cleared an entire office/warehouse by posting one massive, keyword-stuffed Facebook Marketplace listing covering every item (racks, chairs, whiteboards, TVs) — got 10,000+ clicks, ran it like a scheduled flea-market pickup window, and also collected buyer emails/contact info at pickup to build a repeat-customer list for future liquidation sales.
- **Advanced Auto Parts / Pep Boys acquisition example**: after Pep Boys acquired Advanced Auto Parts, huge truckloads of brand-new specialty auto parts (retail value $117,441 for one lot) sold at auction for as little as $1,650 (~$0.17/unit) — general principle: watch for corporate acquisitions/mergers (cited Dick's Sporting Goods buying Foot Locker as another example) since the acquirer often needs to rapidly liquidate excess/overlapping inventory at steep discounts.
- Costco customer-return pallets (via auction/liquidation channels): example of 15 units of exercise equipment (treadmills, trainers) with a Costco retail value of ~$16,000, bought for ~$2,879 all-in (~$191/unit) — cross-checked resale value directly on Facebook Marketplace (Nordic Track models found reselling for $700-$900 used). Advised timing bulk exercise-equipment buys in November to have inventory ready for the January fitness-resolution demand spike. Advised checking item-condition photos carefully (e.g., furniture stacked upside-down or crushed in a pallet is a red flag; boxed/protected items are safer bets).

## Specific "boring but profitable" category deep-dive: rugs
- Nobody actively resells rugs as a category despite steady demand (average household replaces a rug roughly every 8 years); works best in markets with hard flooring (tile/wood) rather than wall-to-wall carpet.
- Premium brand reference point: Ruggable rugs (washable-backing system) retail around $500.
- Cited real closed-auction results: a lot of rugs with $8,847 total Costco retail value sold for $500 (~5-6% of retail); another lot worth $10,600 retail sold for $200 for 67 units (~1.9% of retail, under $3/unit).
- Presentation matters for resale: time-lapse video of vacuuming/steam-cleaning the rug as the first thing buyers see in a Marketplace listing ("stops the scroll").
- Research method demonstrated live: search a generic rug listing on Facebook Marketplace, find an active reseller with meaningful feedback (76 in the example), review both her active *and sold* listings to infer sourcing and pricing patterns, then scrape her listings (via Instant Data Scraper) into a spreadsheet as informal market research for your own local area.

## Specific operator case studies (revenue trajectories)
- **Joe** (pressure washers, Sacramento): started with a single ~$1,500 pallet of pressure washers, netted $1,200 profit in 13 days on that first deal despite no prior category knowledge; scaled by partnering with people who could do repairs; has sold 700+ units this year; hit $17,300 in sales in a single month (October, per the recording), ~$42,000 over the trailing 3 months. Later diversified into a $5,000 truckload of patio furniture, cash-flow-positive within 36 hours.
- **Jim Row**: bought a utility truck off GovDeals priced essentially at the resale value of just the crane/welder attachment on it (worth $4,000-$5,000 alone); flipped the whole truck within 48 hours for $4,000 profit. Also runs a physical retail store ("The Barking Shop," a former Sears location, ~6,000 sq ft, doing $3,000-$5,000/day) built up from a self-storage unit, then a rented tow-truck lot, then the current storefront — deliberately sells Costco staples (bottled water, toilet paper, paper towels) at cost/breakeven purely as a customer-traffic driver for higher-margin liquidation goods, especially useful if located far (~90 minutes in his case) from the nearest Costco.
- General earnings framing: modest, repeatable niches (e.g., "I make $15K/month selling used pressure washers in Sacramento, working ~2 hours/day") are presented as legitimate, sustainable income, not just side-hustle noise — Shannon frames this as intentionally hard to label/describe to others but genuinely lucrative.

## High-capital / advanced examples mentioned (illustrative, not typical starter deals)
- Gold bars (via auction, e.g., Costco gold bar sales at self-checkout, resold on the secondary/auction market): a lot of 43 one-ounce gold bars was cited trading with an estimated ~$15,000 profit margin factored against gold spot price minus a ~5% buyer's fee — described as an efficient/liquid market where returns come from being disciplined (not bidding emotionally) rather than from deep informational edge.
- GSA Auctions (gsaauctions.gov — official US federal government surplus site, distinct from the privately-run GovDeals): examples browsed included a UH-60 Blackhawk helicopter (starting bid $500,000), government-surplus houses/land (a private island with a 1,700 sq ft house previously sold for ~$300,000 was mentioned as a past example), pallets of MREs, and pallets of bottled water (1,920 bottles for $150).

## Caveats / risks raised
- Don't buy first and research second — Chris explicitly flagged that his own advice ("the building of the thing is the research") shouldn't be read as "just buy blindly"; large unresearched purchases (e.g., a hypothetical $2,800 spent sight-unseen on broken treadmills) is framed as gambling, not a calculated resale bet.
- Bulky/heavy categories (furniture, appliances, exercise equipment) require real physical logistics capacity (truck, storage, labor) — mismatch between your unfair advantage and the product category is called out as a common beginner mistake.
- Condition risk in bulk lots is real — some items in a truckload will arrive damaged; the strategy accounts for this by needing only a small fraction of units sold at full "single item" price to recoup the entire lot's cost.
- Reselling neighbors' free stuff at your home can create neighborhood friction (trucks/traffic) — suggested mitigation is to intentionally cut neighbors into good deals so they feel positively about the activity rather than annoyed.
