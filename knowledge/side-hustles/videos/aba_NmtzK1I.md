# 15 Years of Domain Name Investing Knowledge in 15 Mins
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aba_NmtzK1I | Published: 2024-11-29

- Business idea: buying undervalued/expiring/expired domain names cheaply, then holding, flipping, or building sites/affiliate income on them. Host says domain names have been a "common thread" across ~75 businesses he's started.
- Primary tool: Dynadot ("Dyno Do" in transcript) — used for aftermarket expired auctions, an "expired closeout" bargain section, domain appraisal tool, and a name-suggestion tool.
- Two acquisition channels on Dynadot:
  - **Aftermarket expired auctions**: bid against others; filter by max characters, TLD, and domain age.
  - **Expired closeout section**: unwanted domains available for as little as $5 (vs. $10–20 elsewhere), no bidding/competition.
- Filtering strategy for auctions: no more than 10 characters, .com only to start, minimum 5 years old (older = more Google trust/authority). For closeouts, tightened to 6 characters max, not limited to .com.
- Backlinks are the key value signal: a backlink = another site vouching for yours (SEO authority), scored via "domain authority" (1–100 scale). More backlinks from high-authority sites = more valuable domain. Example: a Spanish news domain with 262,000 backlinks and 5 years of age was flagged as a strong buy (idea: AI-summarize Spanish news daily across many landing pages, one per city).
- Naming best practices: .com > other TLDs (older/less tech-savvy users trust .com most); short, memorable, easy to spell; fewer syllables beats fewer letters; avoid dashes; make the name describe what the business does; avoid using your own first name (except for big/established brands, where it can help you look smaller/friendlier).
- Domain appraisal tools give an estimate but are unreliable — he's seen domains sell for 99% less than the appraised value; bid based on your own monetization plan, not the appraisal number.
- Concrete deals/examples from his own portfolio (~130 domains owned):
  - AnimalAftercare.com (dash version) bought for $12 to validate a pet-cremation lead-gen idea; once proven, negotiated AnimalAftercare.com (no dash) from its owner for $3,000 — now a "thriving business."
  - TexasSnacks.com: bought the available variant first to test, later paid $1,500 for the "TX" abbreviated version once proven, reasoning people would type the natural version directly.
  - Bought AndrewTate.com for $10 during a viral Twitter moment — an "asymmetric bet," unused since.
  - Bought SkeleToilet.com and SkeletonToilet.com after a viral meme, unused but has an easy affiliate-content path (link to skeleton-toilet manufacturers).
  - DoomsdayInABox.com bought after seeing demand for a bundled survival-gear/food-storage product; unused.
  - Bought misspellings of a former competitor's brand name ("Minor Depot" spelled with a "t"); cites an example of a mattress company that drove millions in traffic by owning misspellings of competitors.
  - Bought and later sold Door.com for $750 (declined an initial $100 offer, countered $1,500).
  - Bought trend-related domains speculatively: ChatGPT-adjacent names when LLMs took off, "scalp care," "seed oils," "organ meat," "microplastics" — using Google Trends to spot rising search topics before buying matching domains.
- Traffic angle: ~15% of web traffic reportedly comes from people typing URLs directly into the browser (not search) — cited as the reason a clean, guessable domain has standalone value (example given: a friend's Vidaliaonions.com gets significant direct-type traffic).
- Caveat/anecdote: bought a scammer's own name as a domain and published an evidence dossier on it; years later the FBI reportedly contacted him citing that site as part of their case — used as an aside, not a repeatable business tactic.
