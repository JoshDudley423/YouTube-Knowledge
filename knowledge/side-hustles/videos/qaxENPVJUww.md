# How to Start a $200K/Year Sprinter Van Rental Business
Channel: The Koerner Office | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qaxENPVJUww | Published: 2024-08-05

Interview with Raymond, co-owner of Exclusive Livery, a luxury Mercedes Sprinter van rental/"livery" (chauffeured black-car) service in Las Vegas, serving celebrities, hotels, and events.

## The business model
- "Livery" = a chauffeured transportation service (historically horse-and-carriage term; modern equivalent is a black-car/limo service with a driver), common in cities like Chicago/NYC but framed as under-provided in Las Vegas.
- Vehicles: custom-outfitted Mercedes Sprinter vans, positioned as a modern replacement for stretch limos ("the new limousine"), designed to feel upscale/corporate rather than "party bus."
- In business ~2 years at time of interview; founder had ~15 years and ~8 years of groundwork as a hospitality GM for two prior transportation companies before launching his own.

## Concrete numbers
- Cost to build one van: ~$174,000 total (base Sprinter chassis from Mercedes ~$79,000, custom outfitting by a certified Mercedes builder brings it to ~$150K-$174K "out the door," depending on interior spec).
- Rental pricing: $150-$175/hour standard retail rate (weekday vs weekend/holiday rate variants); hotel-partner rate is a 10% discount off retail; minimum booking is effectively the hourly rate (no true short-ride minimum).
- Target per-van revenue: ~$200,000/year gross per van.
- Cost structure per van: roughly 60% of gross revenue goes to costs (insurance, vehicle payment, driver pay, maintenance), leaving an estimated 30-40% net profit margin per van.
- Commercial insurance: ~$40,000/year per vehicle (up from ~$30,000/year the prior year) — driven by Nevada's $5M liability policy requirement for any vehicle over 16 passengers (bus-class vehicles) and a high regional accident/claims rate; explicitly cited as ~20% of a van's gross revenue and "the downfall" of the business's margins right now.
- Driver pay: $20-$30/hour for CDL-licensed commercial drivers; some drivers work on a partial commission basis when they self-source bookings (commission tier reviewed and adjusted monthly based on performance).
- Licensing: Nevada Transportation Authority (NTA) governs two license types — a numbered "limousine license" (under 15 passengers, capped/quota-based, can take a couple years to get approved) and an unlimited "bus license"/CPCN for vehicles over 16 passengers (Sprinters qualify, no cap on fleet size). Application/legal filing costs cited at roughly $5,000-$8,000.
- Financing approach: finance (don't buy outright) vans with a substantial down payment; run them ~3 years (~70,000 miles/year implied), then sell at positive equity and roll that into the down payment on the next van, keeping the fleet consistently new/well-maintained.
- Typical big-event bookings (Super Bowl, F1, conventions) run 12-hour blocks; the van/driver is reserved exclusively for that client for the full period even if idle between rides.

## Customer acquisition strategy (the key strategic insight)
- Explicitly avoided direct-to-consumer, one-ride-at-a-time acquisition. Instead targeted institutional accounts that generate volume: became an approved vendor for ~4 major hotel groups' sales/marketing/events teams, pitching group transportation logistics for conventions (airport greeters, round-robin shuttles, welcome service) — "one relationship that gets you 100-1,000 rides" instead of one-to-one customer acquisition.
- How to break in without an existing relationship (as an outsider, per Raymond): start smaller — get a foundational account (e.g., a shuttle contract for a restaurant, gun range, or small local business/vendor) and build up relationship credibility from there; Vegas specifically is described as small/relationship-driven ("everybody knows each other"), so cold-building trust with any account, however small, is the entry point.
- Playbook for launching in a new city with zero relationships (Raymond's own hypothetical answer when asked how he'd do it in Dallas/Baltimore/Minneapolis): call ahead to identify the Director of Guest Services/VP of Operations at major hotels (Ritz, Four Seasons), then show up in person, professionally dressed, and pitch a hotel-background-credentialed transportation partnership — especially valuable in cities where the airport is far from downtown hotels (unlike Vegas, where the airport is 5-15 minutes from the Strip), since that structural gap increases demand for a dedicated black-car/Sprinter service.
- To diversify beyond airport/tourist dependency (~50% of current business is airport-driven), building recurring relationships with local country clubs/HOAs for member shuttle services (e.g., weekly Friday-night shuttle from a country club or a further-out community into the city for dinner/events).

## Market sizing / saturation insight
- Despite Vegas being visibly saturated with limo/transportation options, Raymond believes the livery niche specifically is under-served — insurance cost increases pushed larger operators to scale back, opening room for mid-size operators.
- General framework discussed: apparent oversaturation (e.g., "too many casinos in Vegas," or "too many dessert shops in a Utah strip mall") doesn't necessarily mean a market is saturated if demand for that category is what draws people to the area in the first place.
- Growth tailwinds cited for Vegas specifically: new hotel openings (Fontainebleau), Oakland A's relocating there, new pro sports leagues (hockey, football, soon baseball/basketball) driving new visitor segments and previously slow weekday demand.

## Tangential/adjacent business ideas raised (not run by Raymond, floated as opportunities for others)
- **Personal shopper/concierge add-on**: pairing the driver/van service with a personal-shopping concierge who accompanies clients (e.g., to Neiman Marcus/Saks), holds bags, assists with purchases — framed as an untapped high-margin add-on.
- **Trip-planning/activity concierge referral service**: Raymond already informally curates full itineraries for bachelor parties (golf, shooting range, dinner, nightclub recommendations) for free; suggested idea is to formalize this as a referral partnership with a professional trip planner who charges ~$1,500/trip, splitting 20-30% back to the livery company for the referral — described as a pure-margin, no-labor add-on.
- **AI-powered free trip-planning lead magnet**: build a landing page offering a "free AI-powered personal trip planner" (backed by ChatGPT) in exchange for an email address; auto-generate and email a personalized itinerary, then follow up with a transportation-booking pitch once the plan is delivered.
- **"Uber for black car services" marketplace app**: an app aggregating available black-car/Sprinter drivers/companies across a city so customers can book on-demand from any participating provider — described as not yet built for the livery industry in Vegas, seen as a potential future opportunity.
- **Niche CRM/texting tool for livery/limo operators**: mentioned as an example of the kind of "tangential" tooling gap operators notice but are too busy to build themselves (the episode's host explicitly frames "what tangential ideas are you too busy to pursue" as a recurring interview question).

## Caveats
- High fixed overhead (vehicle cost + ~$40k/year insurance per van) is explicitly cited as the single biggest structural risk/downside of the business model.
- Success is heavily relationship- and market-dependent (hotel sales relationships, local regulatory politics, an ~8-year relationship-building runway before Raymond felt "comfortable" with his client base) — not a fast, low-relationship business to break into.
- Vegas is described as a somewhat unique market (transient/tourism-driven, airport proximity, hospitality-dense) — Raymond himself is unsure whether the exact same playbook transfers cleanly to a market like Dallas without hospitality-industry relationships to leverage.
